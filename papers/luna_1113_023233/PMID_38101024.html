Subthalamic Functional Connectivity Associated with Freezing of Gait Dopa-Response

Introduction:

Freezing of gait (FOG) is a prevalent and debilitating feature of Parkinson’s Disease (PD). The subthalamic nucleus (STN) is a center for controlled locomotion and a common DBS target. The objective of this study was to identify STN circuitry associated with FOG response to dopaminergic medication. In this study, we compare BOLD functional connectivity of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) in participants with and without dopa-responsive FOG.

Methods:

55 PD participants either with FOG (n=38) or without FOG (n=17) were recruited. Among FOG participants 22 were dopa-responsive and 16 were dopa-unresponsive. STN whole-brain connectivity was performed using CONN toolbox. The relationship between the degree of self-reported FOG dopa-response and STN connectivity was evaluated using partial correlations corrected for age, disease duration, and levodopa equivalent daily dose.

Results:

Right STN connectivity with the cerebellar locomotor region and the temporal/occipital cortex was greater in the dopa-responsive FOG group (voxel threshold p<0.01, FWE corrected p<0.05). Left STN connectivity with the occipital cortex was greater in the dopa-responsive FOG group and connectivity with the postcentral gyrus was greater in the dopa-unresponsive FOG group. Strength of connectivity to these regions correlated with L-dopa induced improvement in UPDRS Item-14 (FOG), but not UPDRS Part-III (overall motor score).

Discussion:

We demonstrate that dopa-unresponsive FOG is associated with changes in BOLD functional connectivity between the STN and locomotor as well as sensory processing regions. This finding supports the conceptual framework that effective treatment for freezing of gait likely requires the engagement of both locomotor and sensory brain regions.

Introduction

Freezing of gait (FOG) is a prevalent and debilitating feature of Parkinson’s Disease (PD) that causes an increase in falls and a reduction in overall mobility1. FOG can be characterized by a transient loss of effective forward stepping despite the intention to do so2. To date, there are no reliable, targeted treatment approaches whereby FOG-specific symptoms are addressed. While dopaminergic medications may be effective at improving FOG in some patients, this response is highly variable3. Beyond pharmacological treatments, FOG has an inconsistent response to surgical interventions such as deep brain stimulation (DBS)4. In the EARLYSTIM clinical trial, only 50% of FOG patients receiving DBS targeting the subthalamic nucleus (STN) experienced improvements in freezing.5 This lack of consistent treatment outcomes for FOG highlights the need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying effective response. Interestingly, FOG response to dopaminergic therapies is the most important predictor of FOG response to STN DBS.6–8 Thus, understanding the circuitry underlying FOG response to dopaminergic medications not only informs pharmacological effects on FOG circuitry but may also inform the development of novel, targeted brain stimulation interventions including deep brain stimulation (DBS).

FOG is associated with disrupted connectivity within a network of cortical and sub-cortical locomotor brain regions including the supplementary motor area (SMA), the mesencephalic locomotor regions (MLR), the cerebellar locomotor region (CLR), and the subthalamic locomotor region (SLR)9. Several human neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that there are changes in the functional and structural connectivity between these regions in PD FOG participants compared to PD participants without FOG and healthy controls9–12. While our group has previously established that changes in neuronal architecture measured through diffusion imaging impact dopaminergic response, there are no published studies to our knowledge that have identified functional connectivity changes associated with FOG dopa-response13. In this study, we set out to investigate whether the functional connectivity of a subcortical locomotor center, the subthalamic nucleus (STN) contributes to FOG dopa-response. The reasons for investigating the connectivity of this region in the context of dopa-response were two-fold. First, the STN is uniquely positioned as its activity can be modulated by the dopamine-rich striatum via the pallidum and its anatomical projections interact with brainstem and cerebellar locomotor centers14,15. Secondly, the STN is the primary target for DBS in patients with PD and dopaminergic response is a well-established predictor of DBS response16. Thus, characterizing changes in STN circuitry associated with FOG dopa-response has the potential to inform both the neural basis of dopa-response and future studies investigating FOG response to STN-DBS.

In this prospective, cross-sectional, observational functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study, we recruited thirty-eight (38) FOG participants and evaluated their response to a dopaminergic medication challenge. This study aimed to: (i) identify differences in subthalamic functional connectivity between DOPA-responsive FOG participants and DOPA-unresponsive FOG participants and (ii) determine linear relationships between the strength of these functional connections and the degree of FOG DOPA response.

Methods

Study Participants

A total of 55 participants with a diagnosis of Parkinson’s Disease based on the UK Brain Bank Criteria17 were recruited for a cross-sectional, observational study of FOG, which included a battery of behavioral assessments and a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan. This study protocol was approved by the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Pro00037836). All participants were informed of procedures and signed informed consent documents. Participants were characterized as PD participants with FOG (n=38) or PD controls without FOG (n=17). FOG was determined to be present if the participant reported freezing on the new Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (nFOGQ) or a score ≥1 on Item-14 of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS). Accuracy of these responses and their concordance with behavior was confirmed by the study movement disorders neurologist (GJR). Details regarding participant inclusion and exclusion have been previously published in detail12. FOG participants were assigned to either the dopa-responsive group or dopa-unresponsive group based on whether the movement disorders neurologist (GJR) observed improvement in freezing behavior during the medication ON state (relative to the medication OFF state). In 35 of the FOG participants dopa-response was additionally quantified using participant-reported change in UPDRS Item 14-score (ON state score relative to the medication OFF state score). There were no specific cutoffs for change in UPDRS Item-14 as group assignment was determined by objective assessment by a movement disorder neurologist. Among participants with FOG, 22 were recruited into the dopa-responsive group and 16 were recruited into the dopa-unresponsive group. The purpose of the PD non-FOG control group was to have a rigorously age and disease matched group to interpret the directionality of connectivity changes and whether group differences observed are the result of changes in the dopa-responsive or unresponsive group.

MRI Data Collection and Preprocessing

MRI data Collection:

Anatomical and resting-state functional MRI (RS-fMRI) scanning was performed using 3T Siemens Prisma scanner at Medical University of South Carolina’s Center for Biomedical Imaging. All scans were acquired with a 32-channel head coil. T1 (MPRAGE) anatomical scans (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.26 ms, TI = 900 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, field of view = 256 mm, flip angle = 8°) and RS-fMRI scanning (T2*; TR = 2200 ms, 36 transverse slices, thickness = 3.0 mm, interleaved or ascending slice order, 119 vol, 4 minutes and 30 seconds) were acquired in the medication ON-state while participants fixated on a cross.

Resting-state fMRI Preprocessing.

Functional and anatomical data were converted to NifTI images using dcm2niix and were preprocessed using a flexible preprocessing pipeline specified in the open-source CONN toolbox (Version 19.c) as implemented in Matlab (Mathworks R2020b)18. Preprocessing included realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, slice timing correction, outlier detection, direct segmentation and MNI-space normalization, and smoothing. Functional data were realigned using SPM realign & unwarp procedure, where all scans were co-registered to a reference image (first scan of the first session) using a least squares approach and a 6 parameter (rigid body) transformation, and resampled using b-spline interpolation to correct for motion and magnetic susceptibility interactions19. Temporal misalignment between different slices of the functional data was corrected following SPM slice-timing correction (STC) procedure, using sinc temporal interpolation to resample each slice BOLD timeseries to a common mid-acquisition time20. Potential outlier scans were identified using ART as acquisitions with framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes above 5 standard deviations, and a reference BOLD image was computed for each subject by averaging all scans excluding outliers21. Functional and anatomical data were normalized into standard MNI space, segmented into grey matter, white matter, and CSF tissue classes, and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels following a direct normalization procedure using SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm with the default IXI-549 tissue probability map template22,23. Last, functional data were smoothed using spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full width half maximum (FWHM). Denoising: In addition, functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline including the regression of potential confounding effects characterized by white matter timeseries, CSF timeseries, motion parameters and their first order derivatives (12 factors), outlier scans (below 30 factors), and linear trends (2 factors) within each functional run, followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz21,24. CompCor noise components within white matter and CSF were estimated by computing the average BOLD signal as well as the largest principal components orthogonal to the BOLD average, motion parameters, and outlier scans within each subject’s eroded segmentation masks21,24,25. Mean head displacement did not differ between the freezers (0.15 ± 0.08 mm) and PD control (0.17 ± 0.13 mm) groups.

Statistical Analyses

Demographics and Parkinson’s Disease variables.

Within the FOG group, differences in characteristics between participants with and without dopa-responsive freezing were evaluated using 2 sample t-tests or Chi-squared tests where appropriate. Variables analyzed included demographics and PD related severity measures (disease duration, H&Y staging, LEDD, MMSE, UPDRS Part-III scores). Group differences were considered significant if p<0.05. Group differences between participants with and without FOG from this cohort has been previously characterized and published12. PD control and PD FOG groups were age matched and only differed with respect to LEDD (greater in the FOG group) and years of education (greater in the PD control group). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (V28) software.

Functional Connectivity Statistics.

A seed-to-voxel approach was used to determine left and right STN (AAL atlas) connectivity to whole brain. Group level analyses comparing dopa-responsive FOG and unresponsive FOG were performed using a general linear model (GLM) as implemented in CONN toolbox and inferences were performed at the level of individual clusters (groups of contiguous voxels) with a voxel-level threshold p < 0.01 and cluster size threshold p-FWE < 0.0518. Covariates included in the GLM included age, disease duration and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). These variables demonstrated differences between groups and/or were expected to potentially influence connectivity values across individuals. To determine linear relationships between the degree of FOG dopa-response and identified STN connectivity, change in UPDRS Item-14 scores (ON vs OFF states) were correlated with the beta values representing the strength of connectivity between the STN and significant clusters identified by the STN seed-based analysis. The beta values were calculated as the average within significant clusters. To address the influence of age, disease duration and LEDD on this correlation, non-parametric partial correlations were performed by using Spearman correlations of the residuals after linear regression of covariates with the UPDRS Item-14 change scores and the STN connectivity measures.

Results

Characterizing Dopa-Responsive FOG and Dopa-Unresponsive FOG Participants

Summary statistics of demographic and PD severity variables between non-FOG PD controls, dopa-responsive FOG and unresponsive FOG participants are displayed in Table 1. Dopa-responsive participants were younger than dopa-unresponsive participants (t=2.126, p=0.040), however, PD duration was greater in the dopa-responsive group (t=2.251, p=0.031). No group differences were observed for education, Hoehn and Yahr staging, UPDRS Part III scores, mini-mental state exam scores, LEDD, or freezing severity as measured by the nFOG-Q severity.

STN Connectivity: Dopa-Response versus Dopa-Unresponsive FOG

Group differences in left and right STN connectivity are displayed in Table 2 and Figure 1. Right STN connectivity to the left cerebellar vermis (FWE corrected cluster p=0.0182) and left inferior temporal gyrus/lateral occipital cortex (FWE corrected cluster p=0.0159) was significantly lower in the dopa-unresponsive FOG group relative to the dopa-responsive group. No brain regions had significantly greater right STN connectivity in the dopa-unresponsive FOG group relative to the dopa-responsive FOG group. Left STN connectivity to the lateral occipital cortex was significantly greater in the dopa-responsive FOG group (FWE corrected cluster p=0.0030), while connectivity to a region within the postcentral gyrus (FWE corrected cluster p<0.0001) was significantly greater in the dopa-unresponsive FOG group.

STN Connectivity Relative to PD Controls

To explore how STN connectivity differed between dopa-responsive and unresponsive FOG relative to PD controls without FOG, 2-sample t-tests comparing group Fisher transformed z-scores were performed. PD control and dopa-responsive FOG groups did not differ in STN-cerebellar connectivity (t=1.37, df=37, p=0.1787), however, the dopa-unresponsive group had significantly more negative connectivity values than PD controls (t=2.68, df=31, p=0.0116). Left STN connectivity with the postcentral cluster did not differ between PD controls and the dopa-responsive FOG group (t=1.271, df=37, p=0.179) but PD controls had significantly less connectivity than the dopa-unresponsive FOG group (t=2.978, df=31, p=0.0056). Left STN connectivity with the left occipital cluster was significantly lower in PD controls relative to the dopa-responsive FOG group (t=2.232, df=37, p=0.0318) but did not differ with the dopa-unresponsive FOG group (t=0.966, df=31, p=0.342). Right STN connectivity with the left occipital cluster did not significantly differ between PD controls and dopa-responsive (t=1.302, df=37, p=0.201) or unresponsive FOG group (t=1.123, df=31, p=0.270). Group differences in Fisher-transformed z-score connectivity values are displayed in Figure 2.

Relationship Between STN-CLR Connectivity and Degree of Dopa-Response

The strength of functional connectivity (Fisher-transformed correlation coefficients) between the right STN and cerebellum (ρ=0.419, p=0.0122), the left STN and left postcentral gyrus (ρ=−.432, p=0.0096), the left STN and left occipital gyrus (ρ=.501, p=0.0022), and the right STN and left occipital cluster (ρ=.516, p=0.0015) were correlated with change in FOG severity (UPDRS Item-14) ON versus OFF dopaminergic medication. All these correlations remained significant after Bonferroni correction for the four correlations performed (p-value threshold of 0.0125). Meanwhile the strength of functional connectivity between the STN and these same clusters was not correlated with overall change in motor severity (UPDRS Part III) ON versus OFF dopaminergic medication (see Figure 3). Correlations were performed using residuals to correct for age, disease duration and LEDD. Confirming our assumption of linearity, residuals of the fitted model were plotted, and no trends were observed.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the hypothesis that differences in subthalamic functional connectivity would be associated with divergent FOG responses to dopaminergic medication. In support of this hypothesis, we demonstrate that FOG dopa-response is associated with differences in connectivity between the subthalamic nucleus and a cerebellar locomotor region (CLR) as well as visual and somatosensory processing brain regions (i.e., the occipital cortex and the post-central gyrus). Meanwhile, dopa-responsive FOG participants appeared to have STN connectivity to the CLR and postcentral gyrus similar to disease matched controls. Furthermore, we found a linear relationship between BOLD connectivity strength and self-reported change in FOG score following a dopaminergic challenge. FOG participants with stronger STN-CLR and STN-occipital BOLD connectivity had greater self-reported FOG response to dopaminergic medication. Meanwhile, participants with stronger STN-postcentral BOLD connectivity had a reduced self-reported FOG response to dopaminergic medication.

Previous neuroimaging studies have established a relationship between functional connectivity and overall dopa-response, however, until now the relationship between functional connectivity and FOG dopa-response remained widely unexplored. Dopa-response of overall motor symptoms has been associated with widespread connectivity changes in the motor cortex26, basal ganglia27, cerebellum and brainstem28. Expanding upon this existing literature, our study identified that changes in STN connectivity with the CLR are associated with FOG dopa-response. Multiple lines of emerging evidence indicate the CLR is a critical brain region for locomotor control and FOG symptoms29. Lesion network mapping studies for example, have demonstrated that brain lesions to regions with connectivity to the CLR are associated with FOG behavior30. Evidence from pre-clinical tracing studies and human neuroimaging studies suggest that there are disynaptic anatomical connections between the STN and cerebellum, which are relayed through the pontine nuclei31,32. Thus, this connection may indirectly mediate cerebellar synchrony with the subthalamic locomotor region. Based on our findings, it appears that in participants with unresponsive FOG, dopaminergic modulation is no longer able to reestablish “healthy” connectivity between these locomotor centers.

In addition to group differences in connectivity with locomotor centers, we observed changes in STN connectivity to brain regions associated with visual and somatosensory processing. These connectivity changes may represent dysfunction in how sensory information is integrated with locomotor plans to initiate and perform forward stepping behavior. Previous neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that participants with FOG increase recruitment of visual cortex when performing virtual reality stepping tasks33. Meanwhile resting-state connectivity studies have shown increased connectivity between basal-ganglia and visuospatial processing regions34. The clinical significance of sensory-locomotor integration is highlighted by evidence that externally guided behavioral interventions such as rhythmic sounds, or visual cues helps to trigger walking in individuals with FOG. Thus, changes in connectivity between the STN and sensory regions in dopa-unresponsive FOG may prevent adaptive strategies and behaviors from being engaged during the ON state35. These sensory modalities and their associated behavioral strategies should be explored further in relation to FOG dopa-response.

There are limitations that should be considered when interpreting results from this study. First, we primarily report associations between connectivity and observed changes in FOG behavior rather than objective measures. While we acknowledge this limitation, there is a high degree of variability in objective gait measures for FOG and little consensus over which measures accurately reflect FOG behavior. To improve upon the subjective nature of these reports, these were not self-reported questionnaires, but instead, subjects were interviewed in the OFF and ON state after an objective gait assessment to determine their severity score. Secondly, the spatial resolution of standard BOLD fMRI measures is large (3mm) relative to the size of the STN. Thus, we cannot rule out partial voluming effects from neighboring voxels. In addition, resting-state connectivity data were only collected in the ON-state which may limit our interpretation of how connectivity changes following administration of dopaminergic medication. Future studies should consider scanning in both the ON and OFF states to directly evaluate effects of dopaminergic medication state on STN connectivity. We chose to perform scanning in the ON state to reduce participant burden as well as confounds associated with excessive movement, and participant discomfort. Despite this limitation, there is reason to believe that the ON state (relative to the OFF state) is more reflective of connectivity associated with medication response. This is because behavioral differences in FOG behavior between groups in more pronounced in the ON state rather than the OFF state where both dopa-responsive FOG and dopa-unresponsive participants experience FOG. Finally, it should be noted note that there are non-connectivity-based features of the resting state BOLD signal which were not evaluated in this study, but which may inform FOG response to dopaminergic medications. The amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (ALFF) captured during resting-state appears to reflect spontaneous neural activity within localized brain regions36. Previous studies have demonstrated disrupted ALFF within the basal ganglia and cerebellum are associated with FOG and predict overall dopa-response37,38. Although they are beyond the scope of the current study, future studies should examine whether functional connectivity and ALFF contribute to overlapping or unique features of neural activity underlying FOG dopa-response.

In summary, FOG response to dopaminergic medications is associated with changes in STN functional connectivity to locomotor and sensory brain regions. These findings may be informative for studies developing targeted, circuit-based approaches to alleviating FOG, particularly for those individuals with a poor response to dopaminergic medications. Given the strong relationship between dopa-response and STN-DBS outcomes, future studies should consider how these functional connectivity findings can leveraged to develop a mechanistic understanding of FOG response to STN-DBS.
