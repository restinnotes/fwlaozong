Abstract
Turning is the most common trigger for freezing of gait in people with Parkinson’s disease, posing a substantial threat due to falls and the risk of traumaticinjury. Within the basal ganglia, the subthalamic nucleus plays a central role in subthalamo-spinal motor integration, yet the neuronal processes underlyingregular turning and freezing while turning have sparsely been investigated, hampering both mechanistic understanding and therapeutic conceptualization forfreezing of gait. We recorded subthalamic local ﬁeld potentials synchronized to gait kinematics in the medication-OFF and stimulation-Off state in 13Parkinson’s disease patients with freezing of gait. We analysed spectral modulation and beta burst characteristics across locomotor states including regularturning, freezing while turning, unimpaired walking, as well as the transition periods before initiating turning. We found speciﬁc subthalamic activation-deactivation abnormalities both before and during freezing while turning, conﬁned to the low beta range (12–20 Hz), which were associated with kinematicabnormalities (“sequence effect”) preceding freezing while turning. Our burst analyses revealed signiﬁcant state-dependent differences with shorter burstsoccurring in quasi-automatic movement during unimpaired walking or the transition period of regular turns, whereas freezing while turning was characterizedby prolonged bursts. Guided by these neuronal and kinematic insights, we trained a generalized linear mixed-effects logistic regression model based onneuronal oscillatory (burst duration) and kinematic features (mean angular velocity, stride length) extracted from both the pre-turn transition and subsequentturning phase. Classiﬁcation performance was quantiﬁed using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, showing good discriminatory performance (accuracy70 ± 19%, AU-ROC: 0.84 ± 0.14) in differentiating between regular turning from freezing while turning. Our ﬁndings suggest that defective supraspinal-motorintegration precedes and further drives freezing while turning. Beyond these pathophysiological insights, our results may inform the conceptualization offuture adaptive neuromodulation therapies.
Introduction
More than half of patients with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) are affected by Freezing of Gait (FoG),1 a debilitating symptom that is most frequently
encountered during turning (94%), accounting for up to 80% of freezes occurring in daily life.2 Its clinical relevance is underscored by the fact that PwPD turn
up to 700 times on a single day.3 This poses a substantial risk to PwPD due to falls, morbidity through traumatic injury, and risk of early nursing home
admission. While considerable progress has been made in the understanding of the clinical phenomenology and deﬁnition of FoG in recent years,4 theneuronal processes underlying regular turning and freezing while turning require closer characterisation to advance mechanistic understanding andtherapeutic conceptualization. Prior research on FoG mainly built on freezer vs. non-freezer group-level contrasts, using various imaging modalities
encompassing fMRI-5,6 or fNIRS-studies,7–10 some of which integrated virtual reality paradigms. The advent of ambulatory recording modalities including
EEG,11 basal ganglia local ﬁeld potentials (LFP),12,13 in combination with kinematic recordings,2,3,14–26 has for the ﬁrst time enabled the study of neuronalcorrelates of FoG with high temporal precision.
PwPD and FoG showed impairment of implicit lower-order automatic behaviour during turning and hence compensated by switching to a more cortically-
represented mode of cognitive executive control-,27–29 the latter being associated with increased activation of the bilateral inferior frontal regions and the left
supplementary motor area (SMA).5,6,10 These regions are functionally connected with the subthalamic nucleus (STN), converging on the ‘hyperdirect
pathway’ to mediate rapid action inhibition.30–32 Recent work indicated that coherence between the SMA and STN at higher beta frequencies (21–30 Hz)reﬂected activity related to this pathway and, that an overactive hyperdirect pathway in PwPD may promote pathological subcortical synchrony in the lower
beta frequency band,33 hindering executive motor planning and effective motor output, and hence, contributing to FoG.5,10,34 Furthermore, theta (4–7 Hz)
activity has been associated with executive and attentional functions mediated by the frontal and premotor cortical areas.35 These oscillations also engage
in the hyperdirect pathway, especially in tasks that require stronger cognitive contributions36,37 with alterations in theta-power previously linked to FoG.38
While accumulating evidence points toward cortical involvement in FoG,39 how beta and theta rhythms converge within the STN and how this may relate tofreezing while turning has not been characterized. This represents a key gap in our understanding of circuit dysfunction in PD and an ongoing challenge inthe development of future adaptive DBS (aDBS) paradigms that require spatial and temporal speciﬁcity. Here, we addressed this gap by integrating bothbilateral STN-LFP and kinematic data from PwPD to characterize subthalamic spectral alterations during different locomotor states related to freezing whileturning. Additionally, we sought to characterize periods of turn transitioning and assessed whether their neuronal signatures differ depending on theoccurrence of subsequent freezing during turning. Therefore, we ﬁrst set out to address beta and theta band dynamics, determining their activitymodulations in the time-frequency dimensions and their relation to the different locomotor states. We then characterized beta burst proﬁles and assessedtheir context-dependent effects on turning. Finally, we trained a classiﬁcation model integrating neuronal and kinematic features to differentiate betweenlocomotor states. Speciﬁcally, we addressed the following questions: (1) To what extent does the spectral proﬁle of FoG-Turns differ from regular turns andhow do beta and theta activity relate to FoG before and during turning? (2) Do subthalamic beta bursts exhibit distinct modulation patterns during and beforeturning compared to forward walking in PwPD? (3) Can FoG-Turns be differentiated from regular turns using multimodal markers combining gait kinematicand neuronal subthalamic burst characteristics?
Material and Methods
Participants and electrode localization
We studied subthalamic correlates of turning motions in 13 PwPD (six female, age at onset: 53.1 ± 10.5 years; disease duration 12.6 ± 5.3 years) who hadpreviously undergone bilateral STN-DBS therapy under regular clinical care and who were implanted with the Medtronic Percept™ impulse generator (timewith DBS 30.6 ± 36.0 months) (Table 1). Because our analyses focused on the characterization of subthalamic activity when FoG occurred while turning, weselected subjects who had been classiﬁed with “deﬁnite FoG” (certainty level 2B; FoG is clinically veriﬁed during off-assessment) which complied with the

All subjects provided written informed consent, and the studywas approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tübingen (166/2020BO1) in accordance with the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental paradigm
Here, we report ﬁndings from turning motions with and without FoG in PwPD, in whom we previously reported neuronal abnormalities related to FoG
occurring during regular walking.40 Brieﬂy, subjects performed a standardized gait task (Fig. 1A) at least 6 months from DBS implantation and after overnightwithdrawal of dopaminergic medication (MedOFF), while different locomotor states were assessed. STN-DBS was discontinued 20 minutes prior to all
recordings to obtain a clinical off-state (StimOff) and to limit potential carry-over effects.41,42 We drew the following locomotor states from sequentialrepetitions of the gait task: 120s of i) Standing, followed by ~ 240s of gait testing for each of the subsequent conditions: ii) Walking through a narrow corridorand iii), Walking being interrupted by internally generated voluntary stops. Gait testing took place in a 15 x 2.5 m hallway. A 2D colour video camera waspositioned to cover the assessment area with the intention to clinically verify FoG.
Neurophysiological recordings
LFP and contact selection for sensing of subthalamic time series and electrodelocalization
Bipolar pairs of non-adjacent sensing contacts that had shown the highest beta peak amplitude in BrainSense™ “survey mode” during MedOFF/StimOff werechosen as sensing contacts prior to all recordings. Throughout the experiments, we used BrainSense™ “streaming mode” which enables the recording of fullneuronal time series at 250 Hz but restricts sensing to one bipolar contact pair for each STN, respectively. Further details on oﬄine synchronization withother recording modalities are given in Supplementary Information 2.

Local ﬁeld potentials and artefact-derived data quality assurance
LFP recordings from DBS sensing-electrodes may potentially be susceptible to non-genuine activity, including ECG-,43,44 motion-related-,45,46 and hardware-related-artefacts. Particularly low-frequency ranges (theta & alpha) can show false enhancement from ECG artefacts that may otherwise disguise any
underlying cognitive modulation.44 We hence recorded simultaneous ECG and kinematic time series using three lightweight inertial measuring units (IMU;Opal, APDM Inc., Portland, OR, USA) attached to both left and right ankles and lumbar spine (L5). We subsequently screened all LFP recordings thoroughly for
potential sources of artefacts (Supplementary Information 3&4) according to our pre-established standards published elsewhere.40 Due to a malfunction inIMU recordings in subject 6, analyses requiring kinematic input were excluded.
Analyses
To ensure clarity and reproducibility of our analyses, the following section provides operational working deﬁnitions of the different locomotor states in this
study. Decisions on the presence of FoG were made clinically from video annotations according to the current IC-FOG standards.4 Further, we integratedkinematics for precise temporal segmentations of the clinically annotated episodes. Sharp temporal delineations are particularly important when analysingneuronal time series that may show meaningful dynamics on short time scales. Standing was deﬁned as a stationary posture without forward progression orturning. Time series of 152 ± 47 s in duration (mean ± standard deviation) were collected at the beginning of each trial. Standing was used as a baselinereference for computing event-related spectral perturbations of the different locomotor states. Regular Walking corresponded to full gait cycles, deﬁned asthe timespan between two consecutive heel strikes of the same leg, which can be precisely delineated from the synchronized kinematic time series. RegularWalking was drawn from time series with uninterrupted straight walking, free from turning, FoG, or stops. We excluded gait cycles at gait initiation (and the 3steps from initiation), and those reﬂecting transitions (i.e. two gait cycles before FoG, stop or turning onset), since earlier ﬁndings suggested that
premonitory neuronal activation abnormalities may precede FoG.40
Regular and FoG-Turning: Turning motions were ﬁrst segmented by framewise video-based annotations using a predetermined operationalization47: Turnstart was deﬁned as the frame at which a toe-off occurred for the step which initiated a change in walking direction (i.e. the foot which rotated ﬁrst into turndirection). Turn end was deﬁned as the frame, at which a heel strike occurred for the step concluding the change in walking direction. Annotations for turn
detection were carried out by two independent raters (PK, HM) due to slight inaccuracies of automated turn-detection algorithms19,47–50 that were notoriginally trained on FoG and hence insuﬃciently detected full turns (~ 180°) in subjects with frequent and long-lasting FoG-related interruptions. Inter-raterdiscrepancies for turn segmentation greater than two standard deviations from the mean were identiﬁed for further review and discussed until a consensuswas achieved. Albeit annotations were video-based, the synchronized kinematic time stamps were later used for a more precise segmentation. Reg-Turnswere turns free from FoG. FoG-Turns were deﬁned as turns containing clinically annotated FoG. We did not use the FOG-index, which was recently found to
display incomplete classiﬁcation performance to detect FoG while turning.51 Information relating to the overall inter-rater agreement using limits ofagreement (LOA), root mean square error (RMSE), Spearman’s rho and intra-class correlation (ICC), as well as turning metrics of individual subjects and thecomputation of the turn signal are reported within Supplementary Information 5.
Turn transitions were studied provided our interest in pre-monitory STN activation abnormalities preceding a turn. Therefore, we analysed the 3-secondepochs before turn start of either locomotor state and labelled epochs Pre Reg-Turn if preceding a Reg-Turn, and Pre FoG-Turn if preceding a FoG-Turn.
Data processing
Gait and turn kinematics
Turns were characterized kinematically using the L5-IMU sensor based on Cockx et al.10 yielding turn duration (s), turn degrees (°), peak angular velocity (°/s)and mean angular velocity (°/s) (Fig. 1C). Kinematics related to either Pre FoG-Turn or Pre Reg-Turn were computed using the MobilityLab algorithm (APDMInc. Portland, OR USA). These included step duration (s), stride velocity (m/s), stride length (m), cadence (steps/min), peak shank angular velocity (°/s),relative stance and swing time (%).
Spectral and time-frequency analyses
To characterize the subthalamic spectral alteration, artefact-free recordings were ﬁrst ﬁltered (highpass = 2 Hz, lowpass = 98 Hz, 5th order Butterworth ﬁlter)and transferred to the time-frequency domain using Morlet wavelets spanning 6 cycles with a frequency resolution of 1 Hz (cwtft.m). The resulting time-frequency decomposition was further smoothed by averaging within a 200 ms sliding window to reduce noise in the data and normalized to the total poweras a sum across the frequencies from 2–98 Hz. Normalized data were epoched based on their predeﬁned start and end points. To ensure comparability, asepochs varied both within and across subjects, we linearly interpolated all turning cycles within the time-frequency domain (1000 samples) resulting in therepresentation of full turn cycles with turn start (0%) and turn end (100%) relative to the total duration of a turn. To assess the movement-related spectralchanges, we then calculated the average interpolated time-frequency response for each subject within each locomotor state and for each STN separatelyand subsequently averaged across subjects’ STNs before averaging across all subjects.
To assess the dynamic activity modulations within the beta band, event-related spectral perturbations (ERSP) were computed by dividing the respective time-frequency epochs by the spectral information of standing (dB). Frequency domain spectra were generated by averaging normalized activity over time,displayed as ‘relative power’ (arbitrary units). To further explore potential associations between low frequency oscillatory modulation and turning, wecomputed the single-trial mean theta band activity (4–7 Hz) in different locomotor states. In addition, we extracted the single-trial peak theta power within
the 4–7 Hz range to isolate the dominant oscillatory component above the background spectrum.52,53 We compared these locomotor states against Regular
Page 5/19
Walking given that walking constitutes a quasi-automatic locomotor pattern that requires less cognitive engagement compared to turning or the pre-turning
transition.6,54 Finally, we compared differences in spectral activity between Reg-Turns and FoG-Turns. To ensure comparability between different locomotorstates, we drew epochs of equal length from either state (Reg-Turn, FoG-Turn). Speciﬁcally, for each participant, we used the average onset and offset timesof FoG episodes during turning. These subject-speciﬁc temporal markers were then used to segment time series of equivalent duration from Reg-Turns.
Beta bursts
Previous research suggested that pathological beta activity is represented by prolonged bursts.55,56 While this association is established in relation to PDakinesia and rigidity, its role in FoG has only scarcely been studied. We therefore quantiﬁed beta burst proﬁles across locomotor states by adopting the
methods implemented by Bange and colleagues (github.com/manubange), derived from an original burst analysis framework56, who had previously
investigated the context-dependent effects of beta bursts during ﬁne motor control in PwPD.57 Brieﬂy, raw LFP time series for both STNs were ﬁrst low- andhigh-pass-ﬁltered (see above), demeaned and detrended (Fig. 1B). Pre-processed LFP signals were then bandpass-ﬁltered around the individual peak betafrequency (± 2 Hz) displayed during 30 seconds of Standing and subsequently rectiﬁed and smoothed with a 200 ms moving average window to calculate thebeta envelope amplitude dynamics. The same beta frequency was then used to extract beta power in all other locomotor states. Beta bursts were deﬁned asconsecutive time points at which the amplitude signal exceeded a threshold deﬁned as the 75th percentile of the signal amplitude distribution within the
standing interval.55 Bursts shorter than 100 ms were not considered to limit the contribution of spontaneous amplitude ﬂuctuations related to noise. Meanburst durations and burst amplitude were computed with trials exceeding twice the subject-speciﬁc median removed to limit the effects of outliers. Thedistribution of burst durations was further assessed by categorizing them into blocks of 100 ms starting from 100 to > 600 ms in duration. Descriptives onindividual peak beta frequencies are given in Supplementary Table 5.
Statistical analysis
If not stated otherwise, all statistical analyses were performed with Matlab (v2025a, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics (v30).Continuous values are and reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data for pairwise comparison were ﬁrst assessed for normality using theKolmogorov–Smirnov test and later subjected to paired-samples t-tests or to Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The signiﬁcance threshold was set to P < 0.05.
Cluster-based permutation statistics
To test for subject-level differences in power per frequency in both frequency- and time-frequency domain analyses, we performed cluster-based permutation
testing with a ﬁxed number of 5000 permutations. An alpha of 0.05 was deﬁned as a cluster-building threshold. Based on our previous studies,40,58 we had aclear a-priori hypothesis underlying the spectral characteristics of STN alpha- and beta-activity (7–35 Hz) with an expected movement-relateddesynchronization and therefore considered a single-sided cluster alpha threshold of 0.05. In keeping with existing recommendations on statistical reportingof cluster-based permutation statistics, identiﬁed clusters do not allow precise statements about the exact temporal or spatial cutoffs and will therefore be
reported in a way to outline the statistical contrast.59
Linear mixed-effects models
A linear mixed-effects model (LME) was applied to investigate whether beta bursts depended on different locomotor states (ﬁtlme.m). The model wascreated by combining both disease dominant and non-dominant hemispheres with single-trial mean beta burst duration entered as the dependent variableand conditions locomotor states entered as independent variable. To account for cross-subject variability in the model, intercepts and slopes betweendependent and independent variables were entered as random effects. Thus, the ﬁxed effects give the estimated population mean values of the slopes in thetested within-subject relationship across all subjects. Only subjects displaying FoG-Turns were included in the model. Separate LME models were appliedtesting for differences in single-trial mean and peak theta power across conditions.
Repeated measures analysis of variance
To further investigate the distribution of bursts with different durations across subjects and between locomotor states, we performed an ANOVA with a 95%conﬁdence interval. Where results were non-spherical, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used, and the corrected degrees of freedom shown in the text.Signiﬁcant main effects and interactions were further investigated with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests.
A generalized linear mixed-effects logistic regression model to differentiate FoG-Turns
To differentiate the outcome of FoG-Turn and Reg-Turn, we used multimodal features from both turn transitions and turning. Speciﬁcally, we were interestedwhether the combination of burst duration from both the transition period and turning, together with kinematic parameters of mean turn velocity and pre-turnstride length, could differentiate the outcome of in-turn-freezing. To mitigate concerns regarding data-driven feature selection, included parameters were
additionally guided by prior literature linking these burst-derived and kinematic parameters to the occurrence of FoG,18,60–62 and because of their futurepotential for continuous monitoring and feedback in aDBS systems. A binomial generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLME) was applied given that GLMEmodels offer excellent statistical power, control for both ﬁxed and random effects as well as for consideration of non-independence in the data which arises
from hierarchical structures.63 Data were ﬁrst pre-processed by scaling and centring of numeric variables. The model was computed using the lme4 packagev1.1-35.5 in R v4.4.2 in RStudio v2024.09.0. Random intercepts and slopes were selected in a step-wise approach, comparing models with ANOVA andselecting the ﬁnal model with the lowest AIC to ensure good ﬁt with few parameters. The ﬁnal model formula was: ‘FoG ~ Burst Duration (Turn) + MeanAngular Velocity (Turn) + Burst Duration (Pre-Turn) + Stride Length (Pre-Turn) + (1 + Mean Angular Velocity | Subject)’. Subjects were included in the model as a‘random effect’ to account for the hierarchical structure of the data. Residual diagnostics were obtained using DHARMa. Semi-partial R2 are calculated using
the method by Nakagawa et al. 2013 and 2017.64,65 To evaluate the model’s performance, we chose a ‘leave-one-out cross validation’ (LOOCV) approach,whereby the model was trained using data subsets of all but one subject (training set) that in turn were used to validate the model on the remaining subject
Page 6/19
(testing set). This process was then re-iterated until all subjects were left out. Summary statistics of accuracy, Cohen’s kappa, receiver operatingcharacteristics (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated for each estimate of the model’s performance and averaged (LOOCV Model).Prior assumptions of the GLME model were assessed.
Results
The aim of this study was to characterize the subthalamic signature of freezing while turning. STN-LFPs synchronized to gait kinematics in the medication-OFF and stimulation-Off state of 13 PwPD with FoG were recorded. We subsequently analysed the spectral modulation and beta burst characteristics acrosslocomotor states including 224 regular turns (n = 13), 122 turns containing freezing (n = 11), unimpaired walking (1191 gait cycles, n = 12), as well as thetransition periods before initiating turning (Fig. 1). Overall, there was a high level of agreement between raters when identifying turn start/end (rho of 0.99,ICC of 0.99 and LOA 0.99) (Scatter and Bland-Altman plots are presented in Supplementary Information 5). Detailed demographic and clinical data can befound in Table 1 with further within-subject stratiﬁcation of collected trials listed in Supplementary Table 4.
Low beta band decoupling is impaired in FoG-Turns compared to Reg-Turns
We ﬁrst set out to address beta band dynamics across locomotor states, by comparing their spectral proﬁles. Compared to Standing, time-frequencyspectrograms of FoG-Turns showed a lack of event-related desynchronization throughout turning, limited to the low beta range (13–20 Hz; non-parametriccluster-based permutation analysis: P = 0.0002). Alpha and upper beta desynchronization were preserved (Fig. 2A). Instead, time-frequency spectrograms ofReg-Turns and Regular Walking displayed a signiﬁcant desynchronization encompassing low beta, alpha, and high beta activity (non-parametric cluster-based permutation analysis – Reg-Turn: P = 0.0002; Regular Walking: P = 0.0002) compared to Standing (Fig. 2B-C). When directly comparing the frequency-domain spectra of FoG-Turn and Reg-Turn, FoG-Turn showed increased low beta band activity (12–15 Hz; non-parametric cluster-based permutation analysis:P = 0.0274) (Fig. 3A).
Defective low beta desynchronization may precede FoG-Turns compared to Standing
To determine whether event-related desynchronization abnormalities would already precede FoG-Turns, we analysed spectral changes 3000 ms before theonset of turning (Pre FoG-Turn). We found a lack of low beta band desynchronization in the time-frequency domain in Pre FoG-Turns compared to Standing,while desynchronization of the high beta (non-parametric cluster-based permutation analysis: P = 0.002) and alpha bands was preserved (P = 0.006)(Fig. 2D). Similar to Regular Walking, Pre Reg-Turns showed full attenuation of both the alpha and low- / high beta bands (non-parametric cluster-basedpermutation analysis: P = 0.002; Fig. 2E). When directly comparing the frequency domain spectra of Pre FoG-Turn and Pre Reg-Turn, however, no differenceswere found (Fig. 3B). In addition, kinematic analyses detected lower stride length in Pre FoG-Turns (Wilcoxon signed-rank test: P = 0.037) and lower stridevelocity (P = 0.049) compared to Pre Reg-Turns (Fig. 1D). Together with an increasing cadence in Pre FoG-Turns compared to Pre Reg-Turns (Wilcoxon
signed-rank test: P = 0.037), this indicated a ‘sequence effect’ preceding FoG-Turns.18,66
Enhancement and attenuation of theta power across locomotor states
We compared single-trial broad-band theta power of different locomotor states against the activity found in Regular Walking which constitutes a quasi-automatic locomotor pattern that requires little cognitive engagement compared to turning or its pre-turning transition (Fig. 4A). While no signiﬁcantdifference was found between Regular Walking and Standing (GLME, P = 0.285), FoG-Turns showed decreased theta activity compared to Regular Walking (P = 0.029). Instead, both Reg-Turn (GLME, P = 0.527) and Pre Reg-Turn (P = 0.955) showed numeric but non-signiﬁcant increases in theta activity. In addition,we computed the peak theta power which is assumed to better reﬂect the strength of a coherent rhythmic activity contained within the theta band (Fig. 4B).Both Reg-Turns (GLME, P = 0.036) and Pre Reg-Turns (P = 0.014) showed signiﬁcant increases in peak theta power compared to Regular Walking while thesigniﬁcant decrease in peak theta power during FoG-Turn compared to Regular Walking was preserved (P < 0.001). Moreover, signiﬁcant attenuation in peaktheta power was found in Standing (GLME, P = 0.006) compared to Regular Walking. Overall, this suggests that FoG-Turn in our cohort is characterized in partby an attenuation of walking-related theta dynamics
Decoupling failure of pathological beta bursts in FoG-Turns
To assess how motor processing may be affected by increased subthalamic beta synchronization during FoG-Turn, we assessed the extent of beta burstmodulation during different locomotor states by calculating single-trial beta burst durations and comparing effects of the locomotor state against Standingusing a linear mixed-effects model. Regular Walking (GLME, P < 0.001) and Reg-Turns (P = 0.002) exhibited signiﬁcantly shortened burst durations comparedto Standing, whereas burst durations in FoG-Turns did not change from Standing (Fig. 5A). Next, we characterised burst proﬁles and compared thedistribution of burst durations across pre-speciﬁed intervals (Fig. 5B). A repeated measures ANOVA (post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected) indicated a signiﬁcanteffect between locomotor states (Regular Walking vs. FoG-Turns) and burst duration [F(2.003, 18.024) = 8.455, adjusted P = 0.003], with a distinct shift andhence redistribution from shorter bursts (0.1–0.3 s) towards longer lasting bursts (> 0.4 s). No such interaction was observed between burst duration andlocomotor states for Regular Walking vs. Reg-Turn [F(2.182, 19.636) = 1.400, adjusted P = 0.271].

Walking vs. Pre FoG-Turn) and burst duration. Post-hoc comparison indicated a signiﬁcantly lower proportion of burst durations (− 11.09% of bursts, adjustedP = 0.010) in the 100–200 ms interval (window 1) in Pre FoG-Turn compared to Regular Walking (Fig. 5C). Unlike Pre FoG-Turn, no signiﬁcant interactionbetween Regular Walking and Pre Reg-Turn and burst duration was found [F(1.610, 14.489) = 3.199, P = 0.079].
A binomial logistic regression model to help differentiate FoG from Reg-Turns
Given the heterogenous nature of FoG, we sought to develop a GLME model capable of differentiating FoG-Turns from Reg-Turns by integrating a set ofkinematic and neuronal oscillatory features. Features were selected based on prior literature as well as supporting analyses. First, we explored kinematicparameters associated with freezing. Mean angular velocity during FoG-Turns was signiﬁcantly reduced compared to Reg-Turns (30 ± 14 °/s vs. 40 ± 10 °/s;Wilcoxon signed-rank test: P = 0.004) (Fig. 1D) and displayed a signiﬁcant negative correlation with burst duration (rho: -0.48; Spearman’s rank correlation: P = 0.038). No such correlation was found in Reg-Turns (Fig. 5D) or when correlating mean angular velocity with burst amplitude (Fig. 5E). We subsequentlyﬁtted the GLME model by combining kinematic and oscillatory covariates that had shown prior signiﬁcant associations with both Pre FoG-Turns and FoG-Turns. The model hence included the following covariates: mean angular velocity (Turn), stride length (Pre-Turn) and burst durations (Turn and Pre-Turn) toaccurately discriminate the outcome of in-turn freezing. Of these, mean angular velocity during turning showed the strongest negative associations with FoGoccurrence (β = −2.668, P < 0.001), indicating that lower mean angular velocity signiﬁcantly increased the probability of detecting FoG during turning. Thiswas followed by stride length during the pre-turn transition period (β = −0.978, P < 0.001). In contrast, burst duration during turning was positively associatedwith FoG-Turns (β = 0.709, P = 0.001), whereas burst duration during the pre-turn phase showed only a trend toward signiﬁcance (β = −0.317, P = 0.091).Overall, the ﬁxed effects accounted for a moderate proportion of the variance (R² = 0.476) in the full model (Table 2). Using an LOOCV-approach, our modelshowed good discriminatory ability to differentiate between FoG-Turn and Reg-Turn at an AU-ROC of 0.84 ± 0.14 (Fig. 5F&G). Given that the AU-ROC is athreshold-independent determination of the model’s performance, we calculated the accuracy at a threshold of > 0.50 (e.g. a turn was determined a FoG-Turnif the model’s probability for a FoG-Turn exceeded 50%). Based on this threshold, the model displayed a moderate accuracy of 70 ± 19% with Cohen’s kappaof 0.34 ± 0.16 (Fig. 5H).

Discussion
We characterized the subthalamic spectral proﬁles of PwPD with conﬁrmed freezing in different locomotor states including freezing while turning, regularturning, as well as the transition period preceding these turns. To this end, we obtained synchronized ambulatory recordings from STN-LFPs, gait kinematics,and frame-wise video annotations as ground truth. Spectral markers encompassing theta and beta oscillatory activity, as well as pathological beta burstsdifferentiated the different locomotor states in a mutually exclusive manner (a schematic summary of ﬁndings is given in Fig. 6). As such, FoG-transitionsand FoG-Turns differed from regular walking and turning by exhibiting a decoupling failure of low beta band oscillatory activity and by a lack of theta bandincrease. Further, FoG-Turn – but not Pre FoG-Turn – showed prolonged beta bursts. Based on these markers, we trained a classiﬁcation model incorporatingneuronal oscillatory and kinematic features that showed good performance in differentiating between regular turning and freezing while turning. Together,our ﬁndings advance the pathophysiological understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying freezing while turning and highlight the transition period asa critical window during which neuronal circuits are particularly vulnerable to FoG.
Spectral and kinematic properties differentiate locomotor states before and duringturning
Turning requires several locomotor adaptations, that are considered to be mediated predominantly at the supraspinal level rather than within the spinal motor
neuron circuitry.14,24,40,67,68 These involve in particular i) adaptation of postural control with anticipatory prediction of the body’s momentum and arc of
Page 8/19
intended turn prior to turning onset, followed by shifting the centre-of-mass (COM) towards the inner side of the turn for dynamic stability along a curved
trajectory;69 and ii) neural commands that redirect the cyclical motion of the lower limbs towards asymmetric modulation of step length.69 For any given turn
to unfold smoothly, the underlying motor program is assumed to sequentially deploy motor commands sent to relevant target muscles.70 These movementsequences are acquired during child development, become overlearned, and are widely regarded as automatic in adulthood, i.e. as internal motor commands
that require little attention and explicit cognitive processing.71 Yet, with further disease progression, this quasi-automatic processing becomes impaired in
PwPD and FoG,72–76 including turning.5,7–9,77 In consequence, PwPD may compensate this lack of automaticity by switching to a more explicit, cortically-
controlled mode of locomotor processing prior to turn onset.27,29,78
In this light, we identiﬁed and reproduced kinematic abnormalities not only during turning itself, but already in the preceding “transition period”, i.e. the lastfew steps before initiating the turn. In contrast to the transition of regular turns, transitions preceding FoG while turning were marked by a decrease in stridevelocity, step length and an increase in cadence, consistent with the “sequence effect” that has reproducibly been described in relation to FoG turning and
FoG while walking.18,66,79 This kinematic abnormality reﬂects an instance of temporal imprecision of the step cycle and stepping asymmetry at a time of
anticipatory adaptation, placing additional strains on already increased lower limb coordination needs for effective steering during the upcoming turn.80,81 Atthe supraspinal level, we could associate the sequence effect followed by FoG while turning to impaired cyclic event-related desynchronization (ERD) andsynchronization (ERS) conﬁned to the low beta range (12–20 Hz). In comparison, both regular turning and its preceding transition were characterized by an
unconstrained ERD, locked to the gait cycle, similar to what has been described in regular walking or other forms of cyclic locomotion.12,40,45,46,82 As such,
the basal ganglia not only encode volitional commands in leg muscle activation,12 but have also been considered to play a key role in selecting motor
programs83,84 and regulating transitions between motor states.85,86 Particularly lesioning-studies in decerebrated cats have recognized the importance of the
subthalamic locomotor region (SLR) in cerebral control of locomotion.87 Here, the SLR can be understood as facilitating goal-directed and adaptivelocomotion compared to the more caudally-located mesencephalic locomotor region (MLR) and spinal locomotor network that are believed to produce more
‘rudimentary’, machine-like stepping motions.87 Cyclic subthalamic ERD may therefore be an essential prerequisite to effectively facilitate subthalamo-spinalmotor integration and hereby stabilise the rhythmic locomotor output to lower extremity motor neurons for dynamic limb coordination during turn-transitioning. This was supported by recent evidence that identiﬁed dysfunctional synchronization of the STN and antagonistic leg muscles, contributing to
defective reciprocal inhibition of leg muscles, during gait freezing.40 Increased low-beta synchronization and the lack of its temporally aligneddesynchronization, arising already in the transition period, may therefore emerge as a novel marker of defective supraspinal-motor integration during turning.
The increase of low beta activity preceding turns with FoG over regular turns may further be indicative of unsuccessful cortical compensation attempts.Neuroimaging studies have shown that PwPD with FoG increasingly rely on frontal lobe areas during turning, regions mainly associated with cognitive control
to compensate for striatal impairments.5,7–9,77 Increases in prefrontal cortex (PFC) activity have been reported immediately before and during anticipated
turns in known freezers,8,9,77,88 including turns containing FoG,5,7 whereas regular turns were marked by a decrease in PFC activity.7 Similarly, recentcomputational modelling using simultaneous cortico-subthalamic recordings and connectivity analyses revealed that frontal high beta activity propagated
through the ‘hyperdirect pathway’ and contributed to low beta synchrony within the STN.33 This hyperactivity was considered to reﬂect compensatory cortical
reorganisation,89 yet may at times be insuﬃcient to cope with complex locomotion, as was shown elsewhere.76,88 By extension, impaired subthalamic betadesynchronization during both transition and FoG turning may be associated with unsuccessful top-down compensatory attempts in stabilising defectivesupraspinal-motor integration during transitioning and turning.
The role of subthalamic theta activity during turning
Compared to regular walking, we found signiﬁcant differences in theta power differentiating regular turning from freezing states and their precedingtransitions. Here, both regular turning and its preceding transition were marked by signiﬁcant increases in peak theta power, whereas FoG-Turns showed anopposite pattern with reduced power relative to regular walking. Interestingly, transitions preceding FoG turning were not different from regular walking.
Consistent with our ﬁndings, reduced STN theta power has been reported in freezing precipitated by cognitive dual-tasking,38 alongside low-frequency (4–13
Hz) decoupling between cortical areas (motor cortex, SMA) and the STN in gait freezing.68 While the relationship of theta-band characteristics in FoG ispresently less clear, our ﬁndings may point towards possible modulation effects of theta power in turning. In this sense, cortical theta power has been shown
to play a key role in upholding cognitive control within the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical (BGTC) motor circuits36 and maintaining goal-directed movement.90
Cognitive processing has further been captured at motor STN sites,37,91,92 with increased theta PFC-STN coherence captured at moments demanding
cognitive control.90,93,94 However, PwPD with FoG exhibit disturbances within fronto-subthalamic circuits of the cognitive control network, circuits that partly
mediate executive inputs required for adaptive motor control.29,95–97 Just recently, reduced between-network connectivity between parietal and frontal
cortical regions was observed to precede freezing episodes.98 The observed increases in subthalamic theta power during regular transitions and turning,
relative to walking, may therefore index successful cognitive engagement in the execution of complex motor tasks.54 In the context of turning, insuﬃcienttheta-synchronization before and during FoG turns may signal impaired and therefore unsuccessful dynamic allocation of attentional resources towards
effective gait adaptation and result in ineﬃcient processing within the BGTC-motor circuits.99 To orchestrate adaptive gait adjustments during the transitionand enable eﬃcient steering during upcoming turns, low beta and theta oscillations may need to work in an opposing, yet synchronous interplay. Anybreakdown of this coordinated interplay may subsequently promote pathological STN-synchronization, predisposing the STN to faulty supraspinal-motorintegration and FoG.
The role of beta burst proﬁles in facilitating supraspinal-motor integration
The duration of beta bursts signiﬁcantly differentiated FoG-Turns from their preceding transition. Compared to regular walking, we further observed asigniﬁcant shift in the distribution of burst durations, with a reduction in short duration bursts in the transitions preceding FoG-Turns, as well as an increase in
Page 9/19
long lasting bursts in FoG-Turns. This redistribution was exclusive to locomotor states associated with freezing, whereas no such redistribution wasobserved during or before regular turning. Additionally, we found a signiﬁcant correlation between turning velocity and prolonged burst duration consistent
with previous studies that assessed the effects of pathologically prolonged beta bursts on motor impairment.56,63,100–103
Physiological short beta bursts are considered to be an important feature maintaining communication within the BGCT motor circuit,55,56,104 with evidencefor the partial segregation of motor processing streams related to upper and lower limb movements in the spectral and spatial domains of beta
activity.105,106 Depending on their occurrence relative to the initiation of movement, bursts have been shown to directly affect motor performance.103,107
Elevated beta in these bursts is thought to index key circuit dysfunction, as excessive synchronization compromises information coding capacity and lastly
BGCT-circuit performances.55,56,108 However, mean-power effects and burst statistics are not independent which needs to be taken in consideration wheninterpreting these ﬁndings. The fact that burst duration increased from quasi-automatic locomotion in regular walking to turn-transitioning and later regular
turning stands in line with previous evidence pointing to context-dependent effects of bursts during motor control57,103,104 as well as freezing while
walking.109 Here, we could show that freezing while turning was speciﬁcally associated with a signiﬁcant redistribution of bursts longer than 400 ms,whereas the preceding transition period was characterized by a signiﬁcant reduction in short bursts (< 200 ms). This aligns with previous work exemplifying
the opposing relationship of short (< 400 ms) and long duration beta bursts (> 400 ms) on motor impairment,55,56 suggesting that different functional rolesmay be attributed towards the temporal categorization of bursts. In the case of turning, it could be hypothesized that the facilitation of eﬃcient supraspinal-motor integration needs to occur on a shorter timescale, and that prolonged bursting (> 400 ms) may impose limiting effects on the circuit underlying
supraspinal-motor integration by interfering with crucial information coding capacity.108 Disruption of this coordinated interplay in the transition period andlater during turning may hence lead to faulty cyclic ERD and render the STN susceptible to subsequent freezing while turning.
Differentiating between regular turning and freezing while turning using neuronal andkinematic features
Means to terminate pathological bursting behaviour using fast-acting aDBS paradigms have previously been tested in a pilot-study (n = 5).62 aDBS paradigms
intended to suppress turn-induced freezing, however, require stimulation thresholds tailored to patients’ speciﬁc neural signature of freezing.110,111 Ratherthan directly addressing pre-FoG detection, we here combined burst-information and kinematic features that reliably differentiate pathological fromphysiological turning states. This represents an important intermediate step before extending detection to pre-freezing transitions for predictive algorithms
suitable for closed-loop neuromodulation which will require larger data.112 Overall, our model showed good ﬁt with moderate classiﬁcation accuracy whichmay be due to an imbalanced set of freezing episodes across subjects. Our data hence serve as a starting point for future concept studies that involveaberrant gait kinematics and spectral signatures to inform aDBS speciﬁc to turning but will have to be tested in an independent cohort in the presence ofvarying medication and stimulation states.
While these ﬁndings provide new insights, a key limitation is the lack of cortical recordings. Although we interpret abnormalities in STN theta and betaactivity in the context of disrupted supraspinal-motor adaptation involving pre-frontal cortical areas, these conclusions are necessarily inferential and basedon prior literature. Future studies combining cortical and subcortical recordings will be essential to directly test these network-level mechanisms.
In summary, we demonstrate that freezing while turning is marked by frequency-speciﬁc abnormalities that emerge prior to freezing onset, most prominentlyimpaired low beta desynchronization that persists throughout freezing episodes and may be suggestive of impaired supraspinal-motor integration duringturning. We further demonstrate abnormalities in the modulation of theta power both before and during turning, suggestive of potential cognitivecontributions toward the allocation of attentional resources during gait adaptation. Overall, these ﬁndings differentiate turn-induced freezing from regular,unimpaired turning and highlight the transition period as a critical window of vulnerability. Our ﬁndings support potential future DBS paradigms targetingpathological spectral changes and further strengthen the pathophysiological foundation for understanding FoG.
