PMID:42968793 | Superiority of dual-target STN-SNr deep brain stimulation towards gait in Parkinson's disease: a meta-analysis and meta-regression of randomised trials. (2026)

# Superiority of dual-target STN-SNr deep brain stimulation towards gait in Parkinson’s disease: a meta-analysis and meta-regression of randomised trials

## Abstract
To evaluate the effectiveness of a dual-target deep brain stimulation (DBS) approach, stimulating both the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), and improvement of gait function and quality of life (QoL) in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). Systematic review and meta-analysis of crossover randomised controlled trials (RCTs). A systematic search was conducted across PubMed, ScienceDirect and Scopus databases up to October 2025. Adults with advanced PD (disease duration >5 years) experiencing clinically significant and refractory freezing of gait (FoG) despite optimal dopaminergic therapy and standard STN-DBS. Dual-target DBS stimulation of the STN and SNr compared with conventional STN-only stimulation. FoG and QoL. Eight crossover RCTs (n=97) were included. Combined STN+SNr resulted in a significant reduction in FoG compared with STN stimulation alone (standard mean difference=−0.39 (95% CI −0.73 to −0.04), I2=23.2%). No significant difference was observed in QoL (mean difference=−0.79 (95% CI −1.92 to 0.34), I2=0%). Meta regression revealed high-frequency stimulation in both targets was a significant moderator for gait improvement (95% CI −0.96 to −0.05, I2=39.2%). Adverse effects were generally transient and reversible. Combined STN and SNr stimulation provides significant clinical benefit for refractory FoG in advanced PD. However, this motor improvement did not translate into immediate QoL gains. Further investigation of RCTs and integrated rehabilitative management are essential to establish broader applicability and its long-term value. CRD42025632066. Keywords: Neurosurgery; Neurosurgical Procedures; Surgical Procedures, Operative; Surgical Equipment and Devices; Implantable Devices

## Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a type of neurodegenerative disease with rapidly increasing prevalence, morbidity and mortality rates worldwide.1 This disease causes the weakening and death of dopaminergic neurons in the basal ganglia area, leading to symptoms such as movement problems, tremors, stiffness and balance disorders.2 Based on the latest data from the WHO in 2023, this disease affects more than 8.5 million individuals globally.3 The most recent analysis from the Global Burden of Disease Study indicated that PD ranks as the 10th most prevalent neurodegenerative disease in Southeast Asia.4

One of the operative neurosurgical techniques for PD treatment is deep brain stimulation (DBS).5 This procedure has been known since the 1980s and was approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2003. Based on most of the published evidence, brain stimulation of both the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and globus pallidus internus (Gpi) has comparable effectiveness in improving motor symptoms in patients with PD.6 On the other hand, neurostimulation of the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) has a different focus, which primarily addresses freezing of gait (FoG) or postural instability during walking in patients with PD.7

Although DBS has been proven effective in alleviating motor symptoms in patients with PD, its effectiveness is limited in certain clinical aspects.8 Patients with advanced-stage PD reportedly continue to experience balance disturbances and walking difficulties despite undergoing DBS-STN procedures or using dopaminergic medications, indicating therapy resistance phenomenon.9 Additionally, some studies have reported that DBS-STN in patients with PD can lead to declines in specific cognitive functions, depression, dementia, hallucinations and axial symptoms such as FoG.10–12 Acute FoG occurring after a DBS-STN procedure is most likely caused by disruptions in the afferent fibres from the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) to the STN.13

DBS-SNr is considered a treatment for FoG in patients with PD because of its non-dopaminergic mechanism.14 This procedure has been reported to produce inhibitory effects that can reduce FoG symptoms. Based on the effects of both procedures, combined stimulation of the SNr and STN is suggested to modulate locomotor integration.7 Currently, several studies are exploring innovative approaches by targeting a combination of the STN and SNr stimulation in DBS procedures for patients with PD.9,15

In addition to the gap problem, the success of such interventions can also be assessed for FoG in patients with PD, which is suspected to correlate with quality of life (QoL). According to one of the largest and latest cross-sectional studies in patients with PD, the presence of FoG correlates with the poor aspect of QoL.16 This meta-analysis used a crossover design framework to synthesise evidence from the primary trials. In the included studies, each participant acted as their own control, which effectively eliminated among participant variation.17 This design allowed for the measurement of intervention preference across every target combination.18 By minimising the impact of individual differences, these crossover studies detected treatment effects with smaller sample sizes than traditional parallel-group studies.19 Therefore, this meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the synthesised effect of DBS to the STN and SNr in patients with PD, particularly concerning FoG and QoL.

## Methods

### Study design

This study design used a systematic review and meta-analysis approach of crossover randomised controlled trials (RCTs).20 The study protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database CRD42025632066.

### Search strategy

A computerised systematic literature search regarding relevant studies was carried out comprehensively through three different databases, including PubMed, Scopus and ScienceDirect. The literature search process began in October 2025, using the keywords ‘Parkinson’, ‘Deep Brain Stimulation’, ‘Combination’ and ‘Freezing of Gait’, along with MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terms in the PubMed database, and was adjusted for ScienceDirect and Scopus. The full keyword for each database combination is presented in the online supplemental material 1.

### Screening and data extraction

The article selection process was conducted with the assistance of the software Rayyan.ai (https://www.rayyan.ai/) and guided by the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 flowchart diagram as a reference framework.21 Duplicated articles were manually excluded, followed by a selection process based on titles and abstracts, as were full-text articles. Titles and abstracts were assessed independently by two authors (RAH and KNB), any discrepancies were resolved in consultation to third author (SI). All selected articles were successfully retrieved without exception, allowing for data extraction. The extracted data included information such as authors and year of publication, study location, study design, type of blinding, patient characteristics (age, gender, disease duration, baseline FoG severity), type of questionnaire, time of follow-up and measurement state. Data were extracted independently by two authors (RAH and FPSW) and third author (AS) verified the accuracy of the extracted data and performed statistical analysis.

### Study eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were predefined in advance of the literature search to ensure the homogeneity of included studies. The inclusion criteria were (1) patients with advanced-stage PD with a disease duration of more than 5 years, who exhibited clinically significant and refractory gait impairment, specifically FoG, that remained unresponsive to both optimal dopaminergic therapy and standard STN-DBS (Villadóniga et al,22 and Weiss et al, 201314), (2) crossover RCTs, (3) studies reporting specific outcome measures related to gait impairment (FoG) or QoL assessed through validated instruments and (4) articles providing electrode verification of the SNr position as a co-stimulation target. Studies involving patients with comorbidities other than PD, such as diabetes, heart disease, peripheral neuropathy (not related to diabetes), cerebrovascular disease (eg, history of stroke), severe orthopaedic conditions (like advanced arthritis or joint replacement), severe visual or vestibular disorders and cognitive impairment or dementia were excluded. There were no restrictions on language or publication date during the search process. Additionally, no patient or public involvement was sought during this study.

### Outcome measures

This study evaluated the comparisons of the DBS effect given to area in STN alone compared with combination area of STN and SNr to improve FoG and QoL score. These parameters aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of DBS in the treatment of PD. A quantitative analysis involved independent extraction of results from the included papers by each author, and any disparities were resolved through discussion.

### Quality assessment and certainty of the evidence

The assessment of bias risk in the included studies was conducted using the revised tool for Risk of Bias (RoB 2.0) for the eight crossover RCTs studies included in this study. Two reviewers (RAH and GZB) independently evaluated each study across all bias domains, and any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (FPSW). The assessment findings were recorded in Microsoft Excel 2021 and uploaded to the ROBVIS website (https://www.riskofbias.info/welcome/robvis-visualization-tool) to generate visual representations of the final assessments. The results were visually presented using a traffic light system.23 This structured methodology provided a thorough and transparent evaluation of the risk of bias across the included studies. The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method.24 The GRADE framework was applied to determine the level of confidence in the pooled effect estimates across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Two reviewers (RAH and AS) independently evaluated the certainty of evidence in each domain, with any disagreements resolved through discussion. The summary of findings table was created using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool. The decision to downgrade or retain the certainty level adhered to the GRADE Handbook recommendations and was guided by both quantitative indicators (eg, I² statistic, width of CIs) and qualitative judgments concerning clinical significance.

### Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted using RStudio V.4.4.1 with the ‘meta’ package. All outcomes were assessed with mean difference (MD)±SD variables which were subsequently presented in a forest plot using the standardised MD (SMD) for FoG score and MD for QoL score with 95% CIs and p<0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. All included trials employed crossover designs in which each participant received both stimulation conditions. However, most primary studies did not report paired summary statistics (eg, within-subject mean differences or SD of change), nor within-subject correlation coefficients. Therefore, pooled analyses were conducted using a parallel-group approximation based on reported condition-specific means and SD. For studies that did not report effect size estimates directly, study-level aggregate data (mean±SD) were extracted and incorporated into the meta-analysis using a two-stage individual participant data approach, consistent with established methodology.25,26 A random-effect model was applied to interpret the pooled effect size. Heterogeneity was considered present when Higgins’ I2 >50% or p-heterogeneity <0.1.17 Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Given the limited number of included trials, meta-regression analyses were conducted on an exploratory basis and interpreted with caution. To reduce the risk of spurious associations, meta-regression models were only performed when at least five studies (k≥5) contributed data to a given outcome–moderator combination. Accordingly, the results of these analyses were not interpreted as evidence of causality but rather as hypothesis-generating findings intended to inform future confirmatory trials.

## Results

### Study selection and identification

Database research has resulted in a total of 3157 studies from three databases which include PubMed, Scopus and ScienceDirect. Out of the 3157 initially identified documents, 866 were excluded due to duplicates and 2279 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Further assessment is done on 12 articles. No retrieved articles were found. Subsequently, four more articles were excluded due to wrong comparator, case-report study and no outcome of interest. Ultimately, eight studies were included for qualitative and quantitative analysis. ### Demography and clinical characteristics of the included studies

A quantitative analysis was conducted on eight RCTs, comprising a total of 97 participants who received DBS in STN, while the control group received DBS in area STN and SNr. All studies included were crossover RCTs and double-blinded. Qualitative analysis included all studies published between 2013 and 2025, conducted in China, Germany, Italy, France and Spain. The included studies enrolled patients with PD, with a minimum age of 57 years and who were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. All enrolled participants were at an advanced stage of PD. Stimulation frequencies used during the studies were categorised as low (30–63 Hz), moderate (71 Hz) and high (119–130 Hz). Follow-up duration in the studies was varied, ranging from 3 to 24 weeks. The efficacy of DBS was evaluated using a range of clinical outcomes, including FoG and QoL. ### Quality appraisal

The risk of bias assessment using RoB 2.0 is presented in figure 2. Five studies showed a moderate risk due to no description of the random sequence generation method. In domain 2, one study was moderate risk due to unspecified washout periods between intervention phases. In domain 3, all studies were low risk of bias. In domain 4, two studies were high risk due to unbalanced dropout rates to both groups. All studies were low risk in domain 5. In domain 6, six studies were moderate risk due to the absence of a preregistered protocol. Overall results showed that one study had low risk of bias, five had moderate risk and two had high risk across domains.

[fig F2] The assessment of bias risk in the included studies was conducted using Risk of Bias 2.0 for eight included crossover randomised controlled trials in this study.

### DBS effects on STN + SNr therapy versus STN alone on FoG score

The pooled analysis for the FoG outcome, which was reported by seven studies, is presented in figure 3. Only one study Hidding et al 2017 did not report FoG score. Combined STN and SNr stimulation resulted in a statistically significant reduction in FoG score compared with STN stimulation alone (SMD=−0.39, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.04; p=0.0293) with low heterogeneity observed (I2=23.2%).

### DBS effects on STN + SNr therapy versus STN alone on QoL score

The pooled analysis for the QoL outcome, which included three studies, is presented in figure 4. Combined STN and SNr stimulation showed no significant difference in QoL scores compared with STN stimulation alone (MD = –0.79, 95% CI –1.92 to 0.34; p=0.3950), with no heterogeneity observed (I2=0%). Several studies did not report any QoL outcomes,22,27–29 while one study30 did not provide separate QoL data for the intervention and control groups, therefore could not be included in the statistical pooled analysis.

### Adverse effects and tolerability

In Weiss et al9 transient dyskinesias, diplopia and mild visual hallucinations occurred in a few participants, all of which resolved after amplitude reduction.9 Similarly, Weiss et al30 reported short-lived diplopia in three patients during dual stimulation, which subsided following slight decreases in SNr voltage.30 A study by Hidding et al31 observed reversible episodes of confusion with hallucination, akathisia and transient motor worsening, leading three participants to discontinue the SNr phase early.31 Horn et al32 also described mild hallucinations and agitation in a subset of patients, likely due to current spread to adjacent dopaminergic regions.32 In contrast, Villadóniga et al and Artusi et al documented no major complications, with only transient paresthesia or lightheadedness during parameter titration.22,27 Additionally, Tan et al28 noted persistent dysarthria and chronic dizziness in a few participants, without psychiatric or neurological deterioration which eventually leads to withdrawal.28 Subsequently, Valldeoriola et al29 reported one case of postoperative confusion that resolved within a month, with few participants experiencing blurred vision (low-frequency SNr) and muscular twitching (high-frequency STN (HSTN)).29

### Meta-regression analysis

Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore potential moderators influencing the pooled effect on FoG outcome, as shown in the online supplemental table 1. The analysis indicated that age, sample size, male population, baseline FoG severity and disease duration did not significantly affect the estimated effect sizes for outcome. Similarly, type of study design and risk of bias studies were not associated with significant variations in the pooled estimates. Regarding stimulation frequency, HSTN+low-frequency SNr, HSTN+moderate SNr, low-frequency STN+high-frequency SNr (HSNr) showed no statistically significant effect on the overall estimate. In contrast, HSTN+HSNr was associated with a statistically significant reduction in the pooled effect size, (p=0.0287), suggesting a potential moderating effect of high-frequency stimulation (HFS).

### GRADE assessment

The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE assessment, as presented in online supplemental table 2. For the FoG outcome, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate, downgraded by one level due to methodological concerns. This downgrade reflected that several studies contributed multiple data sets to the pooled analysis, one study was judged to have a high risk of bias and five studies were judged to have a moderate risk of bias. For the QoL outcome, the certainty of evidence was rated as low, downgraded by two levels. This downgrade reflected the presence of two studies with a high risk of bias and one with a moderate risk of bias, along with the absence of statistically significant effects, although the overall direction of effect favoured the intervention group.

## Discussion

FoG plays a major contribution to falls, injury, poor QoL and loss of independence in PD.33–35 This meta-analysis demonstrated a statistical benefit of dual-target STN+SNr stimulation towards FoG compared with STN-only stimulation, with no substantial heterogeneity between studies. However, the magnitude of this effect was modest and should not be interpreted as evidence of a definitive clinical benefit. Despite statistical improvements in FoG, no meaningful improvement in QoL scores was observed across studies, with minimal heterogeneity between studies. The QoL was also not markedly improved by adding SNr stimulation even though FoG improved. This can be due to short follow-up time, suggesting that short-term gait improvements may be insufficient to translate into broader patient-perceived benefits, particularly given the multifactorial determinants of quality of life in PD. Meta regression analysis revealed that HSTN and HSNr resulted in significant FoG reductions compared with other frequencies. Given the small number of included trials, any observed moderator effects should be interpreted with caution. Meta-regression analyses were therefore conducted for exploratory purposes only. As such, these findings do not support causal inference and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating, warranting confirmation in future adequately powered trials.

All included studies performed crossover design, where each patient served as their own control while alternating between STN-only and STN+SNr settings. This minimises variability between patients and enables direct within-subject comparison of treatments.28 This design is also common among researchers in DBS due to some practical limitations. The number of patients with PD and bilateral STN leads that can effectively reach the SNr is limited, making it unrealistic to conduct large-scale parallel-group randomised trials. The effects of stimulation on gait are acute and reversible, allowing researchers to test multiple stimulation conditions within the same participant across short experimental blocks, typically around 20–60 min. In this study design, each participant serves as their own control.

To add, washout period plays a key role in crossover design trials as it reduces the odds of carryover effects. All included studies confirmed their presence of sufficient follow-up treatment with adequate justification of their washout period.9,22,27–30,32 Meanwhile, one study did not justify their washout, increasing risk of carry over effects in the next intervention.31 However, it is important to notice that in crossover trials evaluating DBS, extended washout period is often not required. The rationale is the limited carryover effect, as the clinical benefits of DBS recede within minutes to hours on cessation of stimulation, particularly in patients with a longer disease duration. This physiological basis provides justification for protocols that omit a formal washout period. Nevertheless, implementing a brief stabilisation period following reprogramming remains a prudent measure.36

The superior effects of STN+SNr dual stimulation on gait can be explained by the complementary roles of these targets in the basal ganglia motor circuitry. HSTN primarily modulates the cortico-thalamic loops, suppressing pathological beta oscillations in the STN and downstream targets.37 This disruption of abnormal STN activity improves classic PD motor symptoms, particularly bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor and contributes to some gait improvements such as increased stride length and more regular stepping.38 However, STN-DBS alone often has limited efficacy against FoG, especially in advanced PD. One reason is that FoG involves additional non-dopaminergic pathways and brainstem locomotor centres that STN stimulation may not adequately influence. The SNr is a basal ganglia output nucleus that projects inhibitory GABAergic neurons to the mesencephalic locomotor region, notably the PPN, which is crucial for gait initiation.39 In PD, loss of dopamine leads to overactivity of the STN due to disinhibition from the external pallidus, which in turn overdrives the SNr.40 Pathologically, hyperactive SNr neurons send excessive inhibition to the PPN and other brainstem locomotor centres. This overinhibition of the PPN is thought to underlie gait freezing and other axial motor deficits in PD.41 By adding SNr stimulation to STN-DBS, a synergistic modulation of distinct motor circuits can be achieved. While STN-DBS improves the supraspinal motor programme generation, SNr-DBS modulates the brainstem locomotor network. HFS of the SNr can suppress its pathological output, thereby disinhibiting the PPN and related locomotor pathways. This mechanistic synergy explained the observed reduction in freezing episodes with STN+SNr DBS.42

According to the synthesised evidence, a clear profile emerges for the patient who should be elected for dual-target STN+SNr stimulation. As demonstrated across the included trials, the ideal candidates are patients with advanced-stage PD for whom axial motor symptoms had become the primary limiting factor for functional independence.9,22,27–32 Specifically, this approach should be considered for patients with FoG is ‘off-state’ or had proven refractory to best-individualised STN-only stimulation and optimal dopaminergic therapy. While conventional STN-DBS remains the gold standard for managing cardinal motor symptoms, such as bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor, its efficacy against FoG is often limited because freezing involves non-dopaminergic pathways and brainstem locomotor centres that STN stimulation may not adequately influence.43–45 Thus, clinicians should elect the SNr as a supplementary target when gait initiation and postural instability remain the dominant source of disability, despite optimised subthalamic modulation. This dual target served as a specialised optimisation for a specific subset of patients who no longer derived sufficient axial benefit from standard therapeutic targets.

FoG judgement was performed by various questionnaires in included studies. One study used the HFPS (Heel–Flat–Push–Swing) parameter,22 while the rest used the FOG-Q (Freezing of Gait Questionnaire) parameter.29 In contrast, the other two studies used the Freezing of Gait Assessment Course (FOG-AC).9,32 These differences—patient-reported versus observed measures—may introduce bias and lead to differences in the interpretation of results. HFPS refers to the normal foot-floor contact sequence in walking.46 Gait cycles that deviate from this sequence are classified as atypical gait cycles.38 This test uses wearable sensors, often placed on the lower back or legs, to record motion data. On the other hand, FOG-Q is a subjective, patient-reported outcome measure consisting of six items that assess the severity and impact of FoG episodes in everyday life.47 Higher scores indicate more severe freezing episodes. However, due to its subjectivity, it is prone to bias. Patients with cognitive impairment may inconsistently report their freezing, while some patients might not recognise brief freezes.48 Additionally, FOG-AC is a structured clinical test in which a patient is observed navigating a course designed to provoke FoG. Patients walk through a standardised obstacle course with various freezing-prone tasks and clinicians observe and score the number, duration and severity of freezing episodes.37 This episode involves tasks such as starting to walk, walking through narrow doorways and turning around. These questionnaires provide direct observation of FoG in controlled but realistic settings. This test is a robust measurement of FoG severity. However, it requires enough physical space to set up the full course.49 Different from FoG, QoL is assessed using uniform parameters, the Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39), giving a robust result in QoL. The PDQ-39 is a validated, self-administered instrument specifically designed to assess health quality of PD. It is sensitive over time, making it suitable for monitoring disease progression and evaluating treatment outcomes. Additionally, the authors have removed patients comorbidities (eg, diabetes, stroke) as it gives bias and contraindicates the questionnaire application.50 These findings highlight the need for a tailored approach, combining DBS with gait rehabilitation strategies to maximise QoL benefits in patients with PD experiencing FoG.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis and meta-regression which analyses the dual-target stimulation, which covers the FoG and QoL aspect. Past-network meta-analyses compared STN, GPi, Vim (ventral intermediate nucleus) and PPN stimulation to improvement in PD motor symptoms. This study found that STN and GPi achieved the greatest overall motor benefit. Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that downstream modulation at SNr produced larger benefits. SNr was not included since it has only recently been explored in small studies.51 However, previous meta-analyses reported no improvement in balance and postural stability despite substantial motor benefits under DBS. This discrepancy highlights that while balance dysfunction is not affected by DBS, gait initiation and freezing episodes can respond favourably when STN and SNr locomotor circuits are coactivated.52

Another important insight from the analyses was the influence of frequency to effect size. Most studies employed HFS, ranging from 125 to 130 Hz and was proven to be superior compared with lower frequency stimulation (LFS, commonly 60 Hz).53 HFS modulates abnormal beta oscillations in the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuits. It effectively alleviates cardinal motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor. However, it has adverse effects on axial symptoms, including gait disturbances.54

On the other side, previous studies found that low-moderate frequency (60–80 Hz) has been reported in improving axial symptoms, particularly in stride length and trunk stability.55,56 However, not all patients can tolerate this. In some cases, it is less effective for tremor control and could lead to the re-emergence of tremor in some patients.56 Additionally, no difference of improvement was observed during high frequency versus low frequency in gait speed and step length.57,58 Meanwhile, one study reported acute disruptive conditions in axial and gait parameters during HFS.59 Thereby, future studies should focus on developing DBS that can optimise the balance between variability of HFS and LFS to manage both cardinal motor symptoms and axial symptoms effectively.

It must be noted that our meta-analysis did not find a significant average improvement in QoL. Additionally, most studies assessed QoL after weeks in each setting, which might be too short for patients to perceive more significant life changes. Thereby, longer stimulation is needed to validate this hypothesis. On the other hand, patients might have improved in walking yet still experience other deficits, such as speech, cognition and dexterity that dominate the QoL assessment. Additionally, it is important to notice that treatment of DBS has also found the influence of electrode placement towards the possibilities of complication, leading to worsening of QoL score. Complications found were diversified, with the most common adverse event being infection.60

Across the included trials, dual-target stimulation of STN and SNr was well tolerated, although several studies reported transient neuropsychiatric and stimulation-related side effects. The most frequent adverse events were confusion, agitation, hallucinations and emotional lability occurring shortly after activation of the SNr contact. The transient neuropsychiatric effects observed in several participants highlight the functional sensitivity of the nigral region, emphasising the importance of careful titration and specific parameter adjustment to patients. Importantly, the events were reversible and no patient required hospitalisation or permanent cessation of stimulation, reinforcing clinical safety and feasibility of STN and SNr stimulation.

There are several limitations of this study that should be considered. The total number of patients across the eight studies was relatively small due to the rarity of SNr stimulation in practice. Moreover, only one study was performed in Asia while the rest were from Europe. This limits the generalisability of our findings. In addition, although all included trials employed crossover designs, most primary studies did not report paired summary statistics or within-subject correlation coefficients. As a result, pooled analyses were conducted using a parallel-group approximation. This approach may have ignored within-subject correlations inherent to crossover designs, potentially leading to conservative or imprecise variance estimates; therefore, the pooled effect sizes should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, heterogeneity in gait assessment tools across studies may have contributed to variability in outcome measurement. Despite this, we mitigated this issue by analysing the mean change in FoG score at follow-up times. Lastly, we did not perform publication bias analysis due to limited included studies. Despite these limitations, our meta-analysis provides strong evidence that dual target stimulation improves gait freezing in PD. Future research should also evaluate the potential benefits of integrating neurostimulation with rehabilitative strategies. Ultimately, by addressing FoG more effectively, we aim to not only prevent falls and injuries but also to enhance the overall QoL for people with PD.

To achieve greater effect, the dual-target DBS may need to be complemented by gait rehabilitation therapies, such as physiotherapy, strategy training and exergaming to maximise functional mobility gains. Previous meta-analyses presented significant improvement of gait by conducting conventional physiotherapy, resistance training, treadmill and strategy training, while exergaming improved QoL.61 This finding suggests that translating a motor improvement into a meaningful change in daily function, confidence and participation requires a structured behavioural intervention. Gait rehabilitation may serve as the essential bridge, empowering patients to use their DBS-enhanced motor capacity in real-world settings, thereby addressing the psychosocial components of QoL that DBS alone may not.

## Conclusions

Combined STN and SNr stimulation resulted in significant improvement compared with STN alone towards FoG, while contrary in quality of life. This dissociation is most likely caused by the short follow-up durations in the trials, showing unaddressed impact. Although dual-targeted stimulations are essential for FoG, promising strategies are required for future large-scale and long-term RCTs are warranted. Subsequent research should explore not only its efficacy, but also integrate with comprehensive management and strategies, such as physiotherapy, treadmill training and exergaming in order to translate motor gains into meaningful improvement in quality of life.
