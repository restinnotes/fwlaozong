Abstract Background Freezing of gait (FoG) belongs to most pressing unmet symptoms in the treatment of Parkinson's disease. Objectives To explore whether combined subthalamic and nigral (STN + SNr) deep brain stimulation improves FoG compared with optimized STN stimulation alone; to identify clinical variables associated with therapeutic response. Methods In this exploratory multicenter randomized controlled double‐blind trial, we recruited patients from our existing cohorts and randomized them 1:1 to STN or STN + SNr . After baseline, patients were followed with close interim visits (visits 1–5) for 90 days (visit 6). The Freezing of Gait Assessment Course ( FoG ‐ AC ) was the primary endpoint and was evaluated at P < 0.2 with exploratory intent. Secondary endpoints assessed responder rates, treatment dynamics, predictors of response, and patient preferences. Results At V6, STN (n = 23) and STN + SNr (n = 22) FoG‐AC outcomes did not differ significantly. We identified responders with clinically meaningful FoG improvement in both the STN (n = 7) and STN + SNr (n = 8) groups. In STN + SNr responders, mean disease duration was ~12 years, which was ~4.5 years shorter than in non‐responders, but there were no differences in motor and cognitive functions. Treatment dynamics suggested that FoG responders improved slowly and gradually. The STN + SNr responder group showed numerically stronger FoG improvement compared with STN responders. Additionally, 15 STN compared to 10 STN + SNr patients wished to change treatment after the RCT phase and before unblinding. Both STN and STN + SNr treatment was safe. While in early disease stages FoG is either not present or effectively masked by dopamine replacement therapy, >60% of patients express FoG as the disease progresses. 1 This is relevant when counseling PD patients for subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) therapy, 2 or if FoG progresses after primarily effective STN‐DBS. 3 , 4 , 5 Effective neuromodulation therapy for FoG has only partly been achieved. The most remarkable benefit was attained in early motor fluctuations, when off‐related FoG is still responsive to dopamine replacement but better controlled for with STN‐DBS. 2 , 6 However, STN‐DBS at “higher” frequencies like 130 Hz is not always effective or may even worsen FoG 7 upon current spreading to the pallido‐thalamic tract, antero‐medial to the STN. 8 To overcome these limitations, several reprogramming strategies have been explored. “Low frequency” stimulation of the STN, likewise with 60 or 80Hz, 7 , 9 was effective in a proportion of patients while others showed worsening of segmental motor symptoms. Further, the effect may habituate weeks to months from reprogramming. 9 Interleave‐interlink programming of the STN—embedding low‐frequency stimulation interleaved with high‐frequency stimulation on a neighboring contact—showed improved axial motor outcomes based on clinical global impression‐severity ratings (CGI‐S). 10 We previously introduced the combined stimulation of STN and substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), 11 , 12 which is located at the interface to the descending locomotor brainstem pathways. 4 , 12 As advantage, the primarily effective STN parameters can often be maintained when adding nigral impulses with interleaving stimulation. However, a role of interindividual variation in functional anatomy, and hence electrode location as well as current spreading to adjacent structures like ventromedial STN or SN pars compacta (SNc) are possible in individual patients and may impact on the stimulation effects. While talking from “SNr” in this study, the label should be understood as encompassing these possibilities. In this framework, there was first indication that SNr stimulation modified clinical and kinematic gait‐ and balance‐related parameters. 4 , 13 Furthermore, STN + SNr showed variable effect on FoG in single case observations, 14 , 15 , 16 a small case series, 17 and several controlled studies. 10 , 12 , 18 , 19 , 20 STN + SNr was mostly safe while side effects occurred rarely 12 , 21 including on swallowing. 22 , 23 In this light, we studied the effect of STN + SNr on FoG on our existing cohorts in an exploratory multicenter randomized controlled study. We integrated narrow interim visits to monitor closely the treatment dynamics of STN + SNr. Furthermore, we explored the clinical characteristics of responders. Methods This investigator‐initiated, double‐blind, randomized, controlled multicenter trial ( ClinTrials.gov : NCT02588144 ) intended to enroll 54 patients from 12 expert centers. Importantly, patients were selected from the pre‐existing DBS cohorts of each center. We selected those, in whom the caudal most contacts likely reached the SNr according to pre‐defined standard coordinates. We did not prospectively implant patients with SNr, which was the main reason for restricting the sample size and for powering the primary endpoint to P < 0.2 while a confirmatory study would have needed substantially higher sample size. We closed recruitment prematurely once we could not identify more patients with FoG and electrodes reaching the caudal STN + SNr border zone. We screened 53 patients, 48 patients were enrolled. From these, one was randomized erroneously after initial screening failure and did not receive study‐related treatment. Two patients retracted informed consent during the active study phase. The remaining 45 patients (Table 1 ) were referred to statistical analyses as modified intention‐to‐treat‐cohort (mITT). The per‐protocol population consisted of 39 patients (20 standard STN, 19 STN + SNr), exclusive of 6 patients with major protocol deviations (Consort Chart). Patients We included patients with PD inclusive of genetic forms, disease duration >5 years at the time of study inclusion, age > 18 and <80 years, having received STN‐DBS for ≥6 months, impulse generator Medtronic Activa PC or RC capable of interleaving stimulation, motor improvement of at least 30% with STN stimulation (StimOFF vs. StimON, in MedOFF after overnight withdrawal of dopaminergic medication), Freezing of Gait Assessment Course (FoG‐AC) ≥10 and ≤33, dopaminergic medication constant ≥4 weeks prior to study enrolment, localization of the bilateral active electrodes contact in STN, and localization of the bilateral active electrode contacts in the SNr as expressed by standard coordinates relative to the midcommissural point (left SNr: −7 mm ≤ x ≤ −12 mm; −2 mm ≤ y ≤ −6 mm; −6 mm ≤ z ≤ −10 mm; right SNr: 7 mm ≤ x ≤ 12 mm; −2 mm ≤ y ≤ −6 mm; −6 mm ≤ z ≤ −10 mm). FoG‐AC boundaries were based on (i) clinical reasoning (i.e., we wished to include patients with a sufficient and reproducible amount of FoG (≥10 points) while excluding those who were mostly unable to walk [>33]), and (ii) based on planning data from our previous study, in which we found the strongest effects in patients showing FoG‐AC ≥10 points at baseline. 12 We excluded patients with MMSE <20 points, acute suicidality or psychosis, pregnancy, in case of comorbidities that interfered with proper conduct of the study or interpretation of the findings (e.g., depression, orthopedic comorbidity, inability to walk outside a freezing episode), presence of paradoxical on‐state freezing, 24 or participation in another clinical trial within the last 3 months. Study Design The screening visit (V0) included clinical testing in MedOFF to verify the inclusion criteria, but also to ensure optimal individual STN parameters and to allow for reprogramming if appropriate. Then, dopaminergic medication was re‐introduced and the FoG‐AC was assessed in MedON, STN StimON. Thereby, we ensured to study patients in whom FoG proved resistant to STN + medication therapy after having re‐visited and optimized STN parameters. A baseline assessment (Day 1, V1) was performed and patients were randomized in 1:1 ratio to either STN (alone) or STN + SNr using a computer‐generated randomization list. Assessment of the primary endpoint occurred at Day 90 (V6). We performed close interim visits at Day 2 (V2), Day 8 (V3), Day 21 (V4), and Day 42 (V5) to learn about the treatment dynamics of either stimulation paradigm for FoG, that may range from acute 7 to prolonged effects. 6 , 12 Further, tolerance was reported after initially successful programming. 9 Physician Roles and Therapy Adjustments Two DBS expert neurologists were assigned two different roles. The “rating physician” remained blinded to the allocated stimulation program of a patient. After final visit (V6), we asked both the patients and the rating physicians about their assumptions to which of the two treatment arms an individual patient had been randomized based on their personal impression. The answers indicated that blinding was effective, such that both patients’ and physicians’ assumptions were close to chance (patients: randomized to STN group: 12 STN, 11 STN + SNr; STN + SNr group: 12 STN, 10 STN + SNr; physicians: randomized to STN group: 13 STN, 10 STN + SNr; STN + SNr group: 7 STN, 15 STN + SNr). The second neurologist was the “treating physician,” when performing reprogramming STN parameters in the screening visit (details in Data S1 ). Further, this physician implemented stimulation parameters following randomization, while keeping the “rating physician” and patient/caregivers blinded. Therefore, the physician was advised to change several times between STN and STN + SNr to mask patients’ insight before maintaining the allocated stimulation program. In case of clinical worsening, adverse events, or safety issues, the treating physician was contacted primarily and decided about any therapy‐adjustments following pre‐specified standards (Data S1 ). Primary Endpoint The primary endpoint was the FoG‐AC 12 , 25 90 days (V6) after randomization. Clinical details on the FoG‐AC are given as Data S1 . It reflects the sum of 12 items ranging in total from 0 to 36 (36 = worst). In addition, we calculated the FoG‐AC change of FoG‐AC(V1) − FoG‐AC (V6); positive values indicated improvement. All assessments took place in MedON, StimON. Secondary Endpoints Secondary endpoints included MDS‐UPDRS II‐IV, CAPSIT‐PD, Berg Balance Scale, Parkinson's disease Questionnaire (PDQ‐39), Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG‐Q), Beck's Depression Inventory, Columbia‐Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C‐SSRS), Clinical global impression (both self‐reported and physician‐reported), MMSE, and falls diary. We asked patients after V6 (before unblinding) whether they wished to continue with their allocated stimulation program. Safety Endpoints The safety population included all patients from the mITT population. Safety variables of patients who withdrew their informed consent during the study were analyzed but not listed with individual data. We defined as safety endpoints: proportion of patients with adverse or serious adverse events (AE/SAE); increase in falls; increase in severity of gait disturbance/FoG over the study course; neuropsychiatric symptoms (i.e., clinically relevant depression, hallucination, psychosis, suicidality); worsening of segmental motor symptoms including akinesia, rigidity, tremor expressed as at least 20% worsening from baseline (arbitrary threshold); increase in dopaminergic on–off fluctuations >20% increase of MDS‐UPDRS IV from baseline. Statistical Analysis We hypothesized that stimulation with “STN+SNr has superior efficacy on FoG‐AC compared to STN alone.” The sample size calculation was based on an effect size of 0.47, a statistical power of 80% and a significance level α = 0.2 (one‐sided t‐test). To detect this improvement of the FoG‐AC (standard deviation of 10.07; mean improvement of 4.67 score points), a sample size of n = 27 patients per therapy group was required (overall n = 54). Primary Analysis We conducted the primary endpoint analysis via ANCOVA with FoG‐AC sum score values at baseline as co‐variable and the FoG‐AC sum score values after 90 days as dependent variable (refer Data S1 for full statistical rationale of primary endpoint analysis). The trial was planned as a non‐confirmatory exploratory study. P ‐values were not adjusted for multiple testing and used as descriptive measures. The primary analysis was performed for the patients of the modified intention‐to‐treat (mITT) population. The mITT population includes all randomized patients (intention‐to‐treat population (ITT)) without patients who withdraw their informed consent. The ANCOVA examined the null hypothesis that there was no difference in the FoG‐AC sum score after 90 days between the STN and STN + SNr groups: H0: μE = μC versus the alternative hypothesis: the mean FOG‐AC value for the STN + SNr group will be higher than for the STN group: H1: μE > μC. The null hypothesis would be rejected if the probability of error ( P ‐value) of the F‐test fell below the significance level α = 0.200. As sensitivity analysis, the per‐protocol population was analyzed, which included participants who had no major protocol violation. Major protocol deviations were pre‐defined as experiencing at least one of the following deviations: missing FoG‐AC values after 90 days, given STN‐SNr contacts for group A (STN) and B (STN + SNr) incorrect, coordinates of the active SNr contacts differ >1 mm from the given specifications in the inclusion criteria, malfunction of the stimulator (e.g., impulse generator not working, cable of active contacts broken), clinical factors which did not allow to carry out the study, therapy change for medication or stimulation not according to the protocol. Secondary Analyses For secondary endpoints, the change between V1 and V6 was calculated and the results were compared between the two groups via Mann–Whitney‐U test for quantitative parameters (ordinal data), and via Fisher's Exact test for qualitative parameters (dichotomous data) (all two‐sided significance level α = 0.05). We considered secondary endpoints purely hypothesis‐generating, and therefore did not correct for multiple testing. The exploratory statistical analyses of the secondary endpoints were performed for the mITT population and the per‐protocol population as well. Safety Analyses The safety population includes all patients of the ITT, who received treatment at least once. We analyzed: adverse events (AEs); serious adverse events (SAEs); increase in falls; increase in severity of gait disturbance (FoG); neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, hallucination/psychosis, suicidality) if judged clinically relevant; worsening of segmental motor symptoms (akinesia, rigidity, tremor >20% worsening from baseline); >20% increase of fluctuations. Responder Analyses Based on Minimal Clinically Relevant Change Further, we conducted responder analyses on the FoG‐AC on both STN and STN + SNr groups with exploratory intent. We defined patients (FoG‐AC, V6 vs V1) as (i) “responders” upon ≥ 3 points of clinical improvement of the FOG‐AC; (ii) “non‐responders” upon <3 points improvement, unchanged score, or worsening. ≥ 3 points FoG‐AC improvement reflect minimal clinically relevant change. 25 Missing Values Missing values were not replaced. Statistical testing procedures were run on predefined analysis populations. In case of a missing value the respective value was not analyzed for this specific parameter (“Complete Case Analysis”). Results Here, we report the findings from the mITT population. The sensitivity analysis, using the per‐protocol population, revealed no relevant deviation from the mITT analysis. Primary Endpoint The mITT population showed no relevant difference of the FoG‐AC 90 days after randomization (V6) between “STN” and “STN + SNr” group (unadjusted mean ± SD STN: V1 18.0 ± 7.1, V6 18.1 ± 8.9, STN + SNr: V1 14.3 ± 6.3, V6 14.9 ± 11.1; means adjusted for the baseline FOG‐AC value: STN: V6 17.10, STN + SNr: V6 15.94, adjusted Δ = −1.16, 95%CI [−7.03, 4.71], P = 0.692; Hedges g effect size 0.046 95%CI [−0.538, 0.631]) (Fig. 1A ). Unexpectedly, the STN + SNr non‐responders performed substantially better at baseline as compared to screening, keeping in mind that (i) both screening and baseline assessments had taken place with STN (alone) parameters and in medication ON before being randomized to STN + SNr after the baseline assessment. The STN + SNr non‐responder group gradually worsened from baseline to Day 90 back to the level of FoG expression that they had experienced during the screening session. Instead, the STN + SNr responders improved gradually. Secondary Endpoints There was no difference in change from V1 to V6 in most secondary endpoints. Number of steps from CAPSIT‐PD showed improvement in STN compared to STN + SNr. Clinical global impression differed between patients and physicians, such that the majority of patients judged their global impression rather worsened compared to baseline, whereas physicians judged it to be equal or slightly improved. Stimulation amplitudes were higher on STN than on SNr contacts based on our reprogramming policy, as expected (refer Table 2 and Data S1 for details). After final follow‐up, more patients in the STN (15/23) than STN + SNr (11/22) group wished not to continue with the stimulation parameters (all descriptive in Table 2 ). Discussion The aim of this exploratory study was to evaluate the treatment dynamics of STN + SNr and to characterize the clinical characteristics of treatment responders. Patients from pre‐existing DBS cohorts were followed closely over three months. Given the limited sample size and retrospective patient selection, the alpha for the primary endpoint was set to p < 0.2, and all statistical results were considered as descriptive rather than confirmatory measures. On the group‐level, FoG did not differ significantly between STN and STN + SNr, and both treatments were safe. In responder analyses, we identified that about one third of patients in both STN and STN + SNr showed clinically meaningful improvement. The main clinical characteristic was shorter disease duration of the STN + SNr responders. Our findings extend earlier controlled studies and case series, 12 , 17 , 19 , 26 by showing that, on the group level and in MedON, STN + SNr stimulation cannot be considered more effective than optimized STN for persistent FoG. However, individual patients may benefit from reprogramming both STN and STN + SNr. Responder Characteristics We identified clinically relevant FoG improvement in about one third of patients which is similar to a recent controlled study using STN + SNr showing similar response rate. 20 Our responder analysis provided several additional insights. Most importantly, responders in STN + SNr and overall responders showed shorter disease duration (STN showed similar numerical difference as statistical trend). However, responders did not differ from non‐responders in terms of STN‐DBS motor response on MDS‐UPDRS III, and measures of cognitive and postural functions. This said, the robust effect of STN‐DBS on motor signs according to the MDS‐UPDRS III widely ruled out the possibility, that STN‐DBS settings may have been suboptimal on motor symptoms. Thus, the presence of FoG in this cohort does not stem from ineffective STN‐DBS. Therefore, our findings prompt the view that both STN and STN + SNr DBS may modulate FoG presumably in disease stages that have not progressed too far, and when presumably the dopaminergic deficit (as expressed by residual dopaminergic fluctuations in both groups) may be the leading pathology. Instead, cognition and gait functions on the CAPSIT‐PD—which may indicate more severe cholinergic pathology 27 were preserved (refer Table 1 ). Further, the responder and non‐responder characteristics suggest that the differences in response are not to be explained by dopaminergic fluctuations; firstly, since the severity of fluctuations according to MDS UPDRS IV was similar in responder and non‐responder subgroups of both STN and STN + SNr; secondly, since the treatment dynamics showed a rather monotonous pattern of progressive improvement or worsening (Fig. 1B ), respectively. Treatment Dynamics Notably, most of the change in FoG severity in both STN and STN + SNr responder groups occurred with delay from re‐programming, developing constantly between Day 21 and Day 90 (Fig. 1B ). While this observation is intriguing and clinically relevant to facilitate long‐term success of any neurostimulation reprogramming for FoG, it also means “not to give up too early” on the potential effect of STN + SNr reprogramming in an individual patient. At the same time, optimal parameter selection upon initial programming seems inherently difficult, since the treating physician cannot necessarily derive immediate clinical readouts on FoG—i.e., (i) whether a patient will or will not respond, and (ii) which stimulation settings of a wide parameter space will provide the highest likelihood to be effective in the following weeks and months to come. Recent studies suggested that kinematic parameters—which were not recorded in this study—could provide an early indication about the individually effective parameters of nigral stimulation. 2 , 19 , 28 Instead, the STN + SNr non‐responder group showed a different pattern of quite profound early improvement of FoG when comparing screening visit and baseline (reflecting before and after STN reprogramming, still prior to randomization). However, this early improvement was gradually lost during the 90 days on STN + SNr and mirrors recent observations of a “vanishing effect” or “tolerance” with “low‐frequency” stimulation of the STN. 9 Safety The safety profile of stimulating SNr in addition to STN was favorable in this study like in previous studies. 10 , 12 , 20 SNr stimulation amplitudes needed to be balanced to avoid acute side effects like paresthesia, oculomotor, or capsular side effects. However, we observed no general worsening of segmental motor symptoms like akinesia or reversal of levodopa effect as was suggested from few anecdotal intraoperative observations. Dyskinesia has been reported only in a minority of patients. While neuropsychiatric side effects were reported exceptionally in other studies, 12 , 22 , 29 we did not observe depressive, hypomanic or hallucination‐psychosis symptoms. Methodological Considerations There are some limitations that could not be controlled for in this study. Disease Characteristics and Phenomenology of FoG FoG is heterogeneous regarding its clinical characteristics and phenomenology, and this may impact on its amenability to neurostimulation. FoG includes a set of diverse clinical presentations like starting hesitation, shuffling, festination, while passing obstacles, or during turning. 30 In particular, turning freezes (up to 80%) and freezing while walking straight ahead (up to 20%) 31 account for the majority of FoG episodes and are well‐represented in the FoG‐AC protocol. 32 Further, locomotor, cognitive, and emotional characteristics may impact on the circuit processes and amenability of any therapy. 33 , 34 Further, we were not able to report the evolution and characteristics of FoG prior to study enrolment including the freezing characteristics before regular surgery and from surgery to study enrolment. Thus, we cannot rule out the possibility that differences in individual disease trajectories may have impacted on our findings in the responder and non‐responder groups. Patient Recruitment Patients were recruited from pre‐existing cohorts, meaning electrodes were not prospectively implanted but localized retrospectively. Together, with further restrictive selection criteria this did not allow us to obtain the originally intended sample size of 54 patients. With 48 patients enrolled we were close to this number, and it is unlikely that six more patients would have changed the outcome of our primary analysis. Functional Neuroanatomy Electrode positions based on AC–PC coordinates did not differ between STN + SNr responders and nonresponders. However, detailed comparison of contact locations, tissue activation volumes, or connectomic profiles within a common anatomical space was not feasible because individual MRIs were unavailable under the data‐sharing agreement and intraoperative electrophysiologic data were lacking. Consequently, some caudal contacts may not have been optimally positioned within the SNr, underscoring broader uncertainty regarding its functional organization with diverse projections to brainstem and thalamocortical circuits. Likewise, outcome variability among STN responders may relate to electrode placement within the STN. 35 , 36 Nevertheless, evidence is growing in PD that the dorsolateral aspect of the SNr relates to integration of gait, balance, and FoG. 4 , 12 , 13 , 17 , 19 , 37 Intraoperative studies suggested that nigral stimulation resulted in modulation of inhibitory plasticity measures dependent on stimulation frequency and pulse width. 38 , 39 Further, nigral neurons may respond differently to motor and cognitive aspects of gait, 40 and this puts the SNr into an attractive position for tackling FoG which represents a dysexecutive‐motor disorder. Parameter Selection We optimized STN parameters in a standardized way during the screening visit. However, we could not systematically optimize nigral parameters in sequential clinical work‐up before randomization. Presently, knowledge on optimal nigral reprogramming is limited. Previously, higher or eventually lower frequencies were applied. 17 In a recent study, we found FoG outcome and “optimal” frequency selection highly variable across patients with most patients showing improvement at SNr frequencies of 71 Hz and 119 Hz, while 30 Hz stimulation was mostly ineffective. 28 A further unknown is, whether stimulation amplitudes should be provided symmetrically or asymmetrically. 41 In any case, early surrogates of clinical outcomes like kinematic gait parameters could help optimize the initial SNr programming. 28 In conclusion, both optimized STN and STN + SNr stimulation were safe and showed similar group‐level outcomes for FoG. Responder analyses indicated that patients with shorter disease duration achieved clinically meaningful improvement. Future effort should focus on (i) stratifying patients by disease characteristics, (ii) refining SNr targeting, and (iii) optimizing parameter selection for FoG.
