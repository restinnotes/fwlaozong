# Local Field Potentials in Movement Disorders: A Narrative Review

## Abstract
Local field potentials (LFPs) represent the summed electrical activity of neuronal populations and provide a critical window into synaptic and oscillatory dynamics across brain networks. In recent decades, advances in deep brain stimulation (DBS) and chronic sensing technology have established LFPs as essential biomarkers for understanding movement disorders and optimizing neuromodulation therapies. This review synthesizes evidence on LFPs in movement disorders, focusing on methodological aspects, functional characterization, disease-specific findings, and clinical applications. In Parkinson’s disease (PD), exaggerated beta-band activity in the subthalamic nucleus and globus pallidus internus (GPi) correlates with bradykinesia and rigidity, while dyskinesias are linked to broadband gamma activity and disrupted cross-frequency coupling. Essential tremor (ET) shows tremor-frequency synchronization in the thalamus and related networks. In dystonia, theta and alpha oscillations in the GPi correlate with symptom severity and guide DBS targeting. Tics in Tourette syndrome involve thalamic low-frequency oscillations, while chorea in Huntington’s disease is associated with elevated beta-gamma power. Clinically, LFPs are increasingly used for intraoperative targeting, postoperative programming, outpatient monitoring, and as biomarkers for adaptive DBS (aDBS). LFPs provide fundamental insights into disease-specific neural oscillations across PD, ET, dystonia, tics, and chorea. Pathological patterns serve as reliable biomarkers of symptom states. By leveraging these signals, DBS can be more accurately targeted, programming can be streamlined, and long-term outpatient monitoring can be enhanced. aDBS based on LFP biomarkers is already in clinical use for PD, offering superior motor control and fewer side effects than conventional stimulation. Keywords: Local field potential, Deep brain stimulation, Movement disorders, Programming, Adaptive deep brain stimulation

## Introduction

The cumulative body of knowledge on local field potentials (LFPs) marks an essential development in the understanding of various aspects of brain function and a crucial window into the coordinated behavior of neurons. LFPs define the electrical signals generated by the activity of neuronal clusters, typically identified using electrodes placed in brain tissue, reflecting synaptic activity and membrane oscillations [1]. Notably, similar recording patterns can be associated with different functions depending on the brain network involved, varying according to disease and individual variables, such as motor and behavioral states.

The earliest studies involving human LFPs can be traced back to Hans Berger’s introduction of the electroencephalogram in the 1920s. Although electroencephalogram records large-scale brain activity from the scalp, historically, it showed evidence of rhythmic electrical brain patterns, hinting at the underlying localized activity later studied through LFPs [2]. In the mid-20th century, groundbreaking studies by Herbert Jasper, Wilder Penfield, and others led to insights into cortical oscillations using direct cortical recordings in patients with epilepsy [2]. The first descriptions of LFPs as mesoscopic signals reflecting synchronized population activity emerged soon after, with seminal work by Jasper and Andrews [3] and by Hubel [4] demonstrating that low-frequency potentials recorded from the cortex captured population-level synaptic activity distinct from single-unit discharges.

Between the 1960s and 1970s, in vivo recordings were refined by the use of tungsten microelectrodes, offering high mechanical resistance, sharp tips, and good electrical conductivity, which allow chronic implantation in animals [5]. In recent decades, the resurgence of interest in neural oscillations, especially in the beta and gamma frequency ranges, has elevated LFPs as critical tools in understanding motor control, cognition, and disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and epilepsy. Techniques like deep brain stimulation (DBS) have incorporated LFP recordings to optimize therapy, exemplifying the translational relevance of LFPs in modern neurology [1].

Building upon these technological advancements, pivotal studies have demonstrated how LFP recordings from implanted DBS electrodes can directly link neural oscillatory activity with clinical states in movement disorders. Landmark studies by Brown [6] and Priori et al. [7] were among the first to systematically demonstrate that LFPs recorded from the subthalamic nucleus (STN) in PD patients reveal distinct oscillatory patterns that correlate with motor symptom severity and are modulated by both voluntary movement and dopaminergic therapies. Brown [6] highlighted two principal modes of synchronized activity within the subthalamo-pallidal-thalamo-cortical circuitry, one in the beta range (<30 Hz), which is pathologically increased in PD and reduced by dopaminergic therapy, and the other in the gamma range (>60 Hz), which is associated with normal movement and restored by treatment [6]. Priori and colleagues provided compelling evidence that the amplitude of beta-band oscillations in the STN is specifically suppressed following administration of dopaminergic agents, whereas other frequency bands show distinct, rhythm-specific responses to pharmacological modulation, supporting the concept of multiple autonomous oscillatory systems within the human basal ganglia, each linked to different aspects of motor function and dysfunction [7]. Collectively, these pioneering studies established LFPs recorded from DBS electrodes as robust biomarkers linking basal ganglia network oscillations to the pathophysiology and treatment response in PD, thereby catalyzing subsequent advances in adaptive DBS (aDBS) and biomarker-driven neuromodulation strategies.

## Methods

We conducted a narrative review of the literature on movement disorders and LFPs until October 2025. Searches were conducted in the PubMed and Embase databases. Combinations of LFP with each of the following conditions were included in the search terms: PD (or Parkinson), tremor, dystonia, Tourette syndrome (TS) (or Tourette), tics, and chorea. Title and abstract were used to filter the first search results. When abstracts indicated possible relevance, full texts were examined. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines were included for their comprehensive and evaluative synthesis of the field. Original research articles were defined as investigations reporting new data in human subjects distinct from experimental or translational studies conducted primarily in animal models or in vitro. Experimental and translational studies were included only if their mechanistic findings were explicitly linked to the pathophysiology, biomarkers, or clinical features of movement disorders in humans. We integrated systematic reviews and meta-analyses by extracting their major thematic syntheses, using them to contextualize and compare individual study findings. Original research articles were used to illustrate specific new discoveries, while experimental and translational studies contributed mechanistic insights where directly relevant. Clinical guidelines were used to highlight consensus or standards of care when indicated.

The evidence that was extracted was compiled narratively and arranged into the following thematic sections: (i) techniques for recording and processing LFPs; (ii) event-related dynamics and functional characterization; (iii) disease-specific findings across movement disorders; and (iv) clinical and translational applications such as aDBS. Studies were included if they provided original or review data on human or translational models relevant to movement disorders and employed LFP recordings or analyses from subcortical or cortical structures. Exclusion criteria comprised articles not involving LFPs, studies limited to non-motor disorders, animal-only studies without human relevance, and publications not available in English. Although this was a narrative rather than systematic review, we aimed for transparent and reproducible screening. The initial search retrieved 914 records across databases. After title and abstract screening, 175 full-text articles were reviewed in detail, and 72 publications were ultimately retained for synthesis, including key original studies, meta-analyses, and recent reviews. Reference lists of these papers were further examined to identify additional relevant sources. Frequency-specific oscillatory signatures, their associations with clinical features, and potential use as biomarkers for neuromodulation were given particular emphasis.

## Results

### Recording Methods

In humans, LFPs are recorded using electrodes designed to capture low-frequency population activity, typically in the range of 0.5–250 Hz. These signals can be obtained during invasive procedures such as epilepsy monitoring (using subdural strips, grids, or depth electrodes) or during DBS surgery [8] (Table 1).

[table-wrap T1] Applications of LFPs in DBS and clinical practice

During DBS implantation, microelectrode recording (MER) is used for high-resolution mapping of neuronal activity to localize the target nucleus [i.e., STN or globus pallidus internus (GPi)]. MER electrodes, which are high-impedance tungsten or platinum-iridium microelectrodes with fine tips (3–25 µm), are designed to record single- and multiunit activity, not LFPs, due to their geometry and impedance characteristics [9]. LFPs can, however, be captured either from the reference ring of the MER electrode, where impedance is lower, or, more commonly, from the macroelectrode contacts of the permanent DBS lead once it is implanted. From the microelectrode tip, only multiunit burst activity can be detected, which may correlate with LFP dynamics but does not represent LFPs per se [8–10].

LFPs may be recorded under several conditions following DBS electrode implantation. Acute recordings are typically obtained from externalized leads in the immediate postoperative period, before the implantable pulse generator (IPG) is placed, using external cables that connect the intracranial electrodes to a digital signal processor; this setup enables recording during movement tasks and under various medication and stimulation states [7, 11]. Intraoperative recordings can also be conducted before the IPG is connected, utilizing external amplifiers and processors [8, 9]. With recent advancements, chronic recordings are now possible using modern bidirectional DBS systems capable of sensing and storing LFPs, allowing data acquisition both in clinic (streaming mode) and at home (chronic mode) for long-term monitoring [12]. These systems further enable outpatient analysis of brain LFP activity using the clinician’s programmer tablet.

In the context of recordings done acutely after DBS lead implantation, local peri-lead edema formation and progression alter the surrounding tissue matrix and LFP signal characteristics. A few studies demonstrate that edema volume can significantly correlate with low beta-band power and may contribute to transient fluctuations in impedance and LFP stability [13, 14]. These effects must be considered when interpreting immediate postoperative LFP recordings as they may confound assessments of disease-specific oscillatory activity and biomarker validity. Accordingly, studies should document recording timing, use imaging to quantify edema, and incorporate methodological caveats regarding the acute phase after electrode placement in both research and clinical settings (Table 2) [14].

### Functional Characterization of LFPs

LFPs are analyzed using a spectral analysis framework, primarily reflecting synchronized synaptic inputs, subthreshold membrane oscillations, and slow ionic currents, rather than the spiking of individual neurons. These are complex signals composed of multiple frequency bands – delta (0–3 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), gamma (31–200 Hz), and high frequency (>200 Hz), each associated with distinct neural processes [10, 15]. These frequency bands are defined arbitrarily and may exhibit overlapping boundaries, contributing to instances in which findings are not uniformly documented in the literature. Higher frequency oscillations extending up to 350–400 Hz have been reported, encompassing ripple-like activity relevant in particular neurological conditions, such as epilepsy where they have been identified as biomarkers of epileptogenic tissue in the mesial temporal lobe and neocortex [16].

The amplitude and power of LFPs reflect the degree of neuronal synchronization near the electrode; these frequency bands are not inherently pathological as their functional relevance depends on spatial and contextual factors. For instance, normal LFP activity in the GPi features low-amplitude, transient beta oscillations and increased gamma activity during movement, reflecting healthy motor processing. In contrast, in PD, GPi LFPs show abnormally elevated and sustained beta activity, particularly in the low-beta range correlating with motor symptoms like rigidity and bradykinesia [1, 10, 15]. These distinct oscillatory patterns help differentiate normal from pathological states and guide DBS strategies. As such, functional characterization of LFPs involves analyzing their spectral content, coherence, and phase relationships with behavior or external stimuli. Importantly, oscillatory signatures are not static but dynamically modulated by behavioral, motor, and cognitive states. The spectral profile of LFPs can vary substantially depending on whether recordings are obtained during rest, voluntary movement, or under different medication and vigilance conditions [17, 18]. For example, beta desynchronization typically accompanies movement execution, while re-emergent beta synchrony is observed during rest or postural maintenance [18, 19]. Likewise, attention, task engagement, and sleep-wake transitions modulate both oscillatory power and coupling patterns across basal ganglia-cortical circuits. These state-dependent dynamics emphasize that the validity and interpretation of LFP biomarkers are contingent on recording conditions and that variability across studies may often reflect differences in behavioral or cognitive context rather than inconsistent physiology [17–19].

In addition to the oscillatory components typically analyzed in LFP studies, the aperiodic or non-oscillatory component of neural power spectra, often described by a 1/f-like decay, encodes crucial information about the underlying synaptic and circuit dynamics. The slope and offset of this aperiodic component reflect the excitation-inhibition (E/I) balance and the scale of neuronal population activity, providing complementary physiological insights beyond oscillatory rhythms [20]. Methodologically, spectral parameterization techniques now allow the decomposition of LFP power spectra into periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic (1/f) components, enabling more accurate quantification of both rhythmic and broadband activity. In PD, the aperiodic exponent has been shown to vary with dopaminergic state, decreasing after medication and during effective DBS, consistent with restoration of physiological E/I balance [20, 21]. These findings suggest that aperiodic metrics may serve as additional biomarkers of disease state and treatment response, enhancing the interpretive precision of LFP analyses. Integrating aperiodic measures with traditional oscillatory features may therefore provide a more complete representation of neural network dynamics in movement disorders.

Beyond spectral power analysis, contemporary approaches increasingly emphasize temporal and coupling-based metrics to capture the dynamic organization of oscillatory activity. Amplitude-amplitude and phase-phase coupling analyses assess synchrony across frequency bands or spatially distinct regions, providing insight into functional connectivity within and between basal ganglia-cortical networks [22]. Phase-amplitude coupling, in particular, quantifies how the phase of a low-frequency oscillation (e.g., beta) modulates the amplitude of a higher-frequency rhythm (e.g., gamma), reflecting cross-scale coordination relevant to motor control and disease states. Abnormal beta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling in PD and dystonia has been linked to pathological synchronization and impaired movement execution [23]. In the time domain, transient oscillatory bursts, especially beta bursts, are now recognized as physiologically meaningful events rather than sustained rhythms. Their rate, duration, and timing correlate with motor performance and dopaminergic modulation [24]. These complementary analytical dimensions expand traditional spectral power frameworks, allowing a richer characterization of network dynamics in movement disorders.

While frequency-specific power changes are valuable for distinguishing pathological from physiological brain states, several methodological factors must be considered when interpreting these findings. Band-power metrics are highly dependent on referencing schemes, filtering choices, and behavioral or medication states, all of which can influence both spectral amplitude and peak frequency estimation [17, 25]. Furthermore, frequency bands represent heuristic divisions of a continuous spectrum rather than fixed physiological boundaries, and their optimal limits may vary across individuals, brain regions, and disease states [25]. Recent work by Bronte-Stewart and colleagues has demonstrated that spectral peaks can shift toward lower or higher frequency ranges depending on dopaminergic state and cortical involvement, with exaggerated alpha rhythms emerging in some patients with PD [26]. Such inter- and intra-individual variability underscores the importance of adaptive or data-driven approaches to spectral analysis rather than relying solely on canonical frequency-band definitions. Acknowledging these dependencies is crucial to ensure that band-power features are interpreted as context-sensitive markers rather than static, universally applicable biomarkers.

Finally, although the present review focuses on motor aspects of movement disorders, it is important to note that LFP recordings have also been extensively applied to study non-motor processes such as emotional regulation, reward processing, moral decision-making, and impulsivity. Recordings from the STN, nucleus accumbens, and anterior cingulate cortex have revealed frequency-specific modulations linked to affective valence, risk-taking, and cognitive control, indicating that these circuits contribute to both motor and limbic functions [19, 21]. For example, STN low-frequency oscillations (4–12 Hz) have been shown to encode conflict and emotional salience, while beta and gamma activity in limbic territories reflect decision uncertainty and reward expectancy [27]. These findings highlight that LFPs provide a valuable window into the broader functional integration of motor, cognitive, and affective networks, even though non-motor domains fall beyond the central scope of this review.

### Event-Related Synchronization and Desynchronization

Changes in power in response to specific events – such as movement, sensory input, or cognitive tasks – are referred to as event-related desynchronization (ERD) and event-related synchronization (ERS) [17]. These patterns, observed in LFP recordings, reflect dynamic shifts in neural activity. ERD typically indicates a transient decrease in power within a given frequency band and is interpreted as a sign of increased neuronal activation and reduced synchrony, allowing for more flexible information processing. It commonly occurs in the alpha and beta bands during motor preparation or execution [1, 17]. In contrast, ERS reflects a transient power increase whose functional significance varies with frequency and behavioral context. For example, beta/alpha rebounds are often linked to inhibitory or resetting processes, whereas gamma-band ERS is typically associated with local activation and information processing [18, 19]. The interpretation of ERD/ERS therefore depends strongly on the specific frequency band, anatomical locus, and task conditions. Together, these phenomena provide important insights into neural dynamics and are widely used in both basic neuroscience and clinical applications, including brain-computer interfaces and aDBS (Tables 2 and 3) [17–19].

### LFPs in PD

Abnormal neural oscillation synchronization in the basal ganglia, particularly within the GPi and STN, is an important trait of PD pathophysiology. One of the most robust and recurring observed features in the assessment of LFPs in PD patients is augmented beta frequency power, as mentioned previously in this review [28]. Importantly, beta oscillations can be further subdivided into two canonical spectral bands: low-beta (13–20 Hz) and high-beta (21–35 Hz). The relevance of this discrimination relies on the fact that their functions differ: low-beta activity is dominant in the STN, considered pathological, closely linked to disease severity, and sensitive to dopaminergic or DBS therapies [29]. This activity band is tightly correlated with certain motor symptoms, such as bradykinesia and rigidity. Conversely, high beta is thought to support physiological, prokinetic functions, probably playing a role in motor control and recovery during effective treatment [28, 29]. Tremor is less consistently correlated with beta power, tending to occur with oscillations synchronized at or near the tremor frequency. These phenomena correlate better with theta and may involve distinct circuits, such as the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit [30].

Postural instability and gait disorder (PIGD), which is typically more resistant to levodopa therapy, is more heterogeneous in terms of LFP recordings. In this scenario, beta oscillations are less directly correlated to PIGD symptoms and decreased beta power and lower frequency oscillations in the theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) bands have been reported in the STN and GPi [31]. Recordings of PIGD cases also show disrupted cross-frequency coupling (CFC) – defined as the structured, non-random interaction between different brain frequency bands – among beta and either higher (gamma) and lower frequency bands [32]. Furthermore, physiologic decrease in beta activity during movement (desynchronization) is blunted in PIGD patients, indicating reduced neural flexibility that might impair postural responses and gait initiation [31]. As a final point, freezing of gait (FOG) has also been correlated with brief beta bursts just before or during episodes. However, given the complexity of FOG, this finding is not consistent across patients [33]. Of importance, other frequency bands may be equally or more relevant, especially in those cases with a marked cognitive component.

Conversely, levodopa and effective DBS alter LFPs by reducing beta power, restoring more normal patterns of neural activity, and potentially increasing gamma and, to a lower extent, theta and alpha oscillations. As such, this pattern is considered prokinetic, closely linked to clinical motor improvement and the occurrence of peak-dose dyskinesias. On the flipside, re-emergence of beta oscillations, either upon levodopa wearing off or by switching DBS off, correlates with more severe signs of parkinsonism [28, 29].

As already pointed out, dopaminergic medication suppresses beta activity and improves motor symptoms. However, when this suppression becomes excessive, it can be associated with the emergence of dyskinesias, suggesting that while some beta reduction is therapeutic, undue suppression can lead to hyperkinetic side effects [28–30]. In contrast, STN and GPi gamma band activity, particularly in the low to mid-gamma range (60–90 Hz), often increases during dyskinesias. Some patients also exhibit broader increases in power across the gamma range, sometimes referred to as broadband gamma [28]. This increase is believed to reflect the abnormal, involuntary movements characteristic of dyskinesias and may involve both subcortical and cortical motor circuits. Additionally, theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–12 Hz) activity may also increase in some patients, although the significance of these changes is less clearly defined [15]. These oscillations may contribute to the pathological network synchrony that underlies dyskinetic states. Finally, during dyskinesias, CFC between low-frequency phases (theta or alpha) and gamma can be disrupted or disorganized, reflecting impaired information processing and abnormal motor control [23]. Together, these changes (suppressed beta activity, increased gamma power, and disrupted CFC) represent a candidate neurophysiological fingerprint of dyskinetic states in PD. While increased broadband gamma power and altered CFC during dyskinesias are consistent findings across multiple studies, it is important to acknowledge that such signals can be influenced by movement-related artifacts [28]. Dyskinesias often involve vigorous, repetitive muscle contractions capable of introducing high-frequency components that may overlap with true neural gamma activity. To mitigate this confound, most studies apply rigorous preprocessing, including high-pass filtering, notch and harmonic artifact removal, and the use of electromyographic or accelerometry signals for artifact detection and exclusion [18, 23, 28]. Furthermore, simultaneous cortical and subcortical recordings, as well as recordings during both medication- and stimulation-induced dyskinesias, have provided converging evidence that at least part of the broadband gamma enhancement reflects genuine neural activity rather than muscular contamination [18, 23]. Nonetheless, the potential contribution of peripheral artifacts cannot be completely ruled out and should be explicitly considered when interpreting gamma- and coupling-based biomarkers. This methodological consideration is particularly relevant for aDBS paradigms that rely on high-frequency features for real-time control, underscoring the need for artifact-aware signal processing and multimodal validation [23, 34].

Finally, while gamma-band activity during dyskinesias is a consistent finding in several studies, its pathophysiological role remains under discussion. Some authors have reported a predominance of broadband gamma activity during levodopa-induced dyskinesia and during aDBS paradigms that use gamma-based control signals [35], whereas others have questioned whether this represents a truly pathological oscillation or a physiological correlate of movement and cortical excitation [9, 17, 18]. In parallel, theta and alpha oscillations have been more consistently observed in both motor and non-motor states, including emotional and cognitive processing [36, 37]. Moreover, high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) (250–330 Hz) recorded in the STN have been linked to the dopaminergic ON state, supporting their potential as markers of prokinetic network engagement [38].

As mentioned previously, an extensive body of literature has explored non-motor correlates of LFPs in PD, emphasizing that basal ganglia oscillatory activity extends beyond motor control, reflecting the integration of motor, limbic, and associative circuits [19, 21, 27].

LFP recordings of HFOs in distinct anatomical areas of the STN have also been associated with different clinical PD phenotypes. HFOs include part of the gamma spectrum (>60 Hz), high gamma (90–200 Hz), and faster ripple-like activity (200–450 Hz). While slow HFOs tend to occur in the dorsal STN and are associated with the tremor-dominant subtype of PD, fast HFOs above 90 Hz are often recorded in the ventral STN and are linked to PIGD. These distinctive anatomical and clinical features are more variable and less pronounced for the GPi recordings [15, 30–32]. The applications of LFP monitoring in PD and its integration into closed-loop or aDBS systems will be further discussed in a separate section of this review (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in ET

Essential tremor (ET) is increasingly recognized as a network disorder involving abnormal oscillatory activity within the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have demonstrated tremor-related activity and heightened coherence across the cerebellum, thalamus, and motor cortex [39]. This pathological synchronization correlates with tremor severity, underscoring the circuit’s central role in ET pathophysiology [39].

LFPs recorded from the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus consistently show prominent oscillatory activity at tremor frequency (4–12 Hz), with amplitude increases during tremor episodes. These oscillations are coherent with electromyographic and accelerometric signals from affected limbs, both at tremor frequency and at its harmonics, and are spatially confined to specific VIM subregions [40]. Furthermore, MER from motor thalamic nuclei (VIM and ventral oral posterior) reveals strong coherence with pathological tremor frequencies (4–7 Hz), whereas physiological tremor-related oscillations (8–12 Hz) are distributed across motor and somatosensory thalamic regions. CFC analyses indicate a hierarchical organization where low-frequency theta rhythms modulate beta and gamma band activity [40].

Patients with ET also exhibit excessive synchronization in the theta and beta frequency bands. During voluntary motor tasks, beta-band activity (13–30 Hz) typically decreases while gamma oscillations (55–80 Hz) increase, a dynamic that may serve as a physiological biomarker for aDBS. Notably, thalamic beta oscillations – especially those localized along the dentato-rubro-thalamic tract – have been associated with sustained tremor suppression, highlighting their potential as a therapeutic target [41]. Emerging evidence also suggests that beta oscillations in the VIM are modulated by task complexity. Specifically, during incongruent visuomotor center-out tasks, VIM beta-band power is significantly reduced compared to congruent tasks, while neuronal firing rates increase during periods of low beta power, particularly as targets are approached [42]. In contrast, patients with PD show no comparable modulation of beta power in the STN under similar conditions [17]. These findings imply that beta suppression may facilitate thalamocortical information transfer via enhanced neuronal firing, underscoring the task-dependent dynamics of thalamic oscillations in ET [40–42].

Complementing these observations, both voluntary movements and postural tremor can be reliably detected using thalamic LFPs recorded from the same electrodes used for therapeutic stimulation. Detection reached a mean sensitivity of 0.8 with a false-positive rate of 0.2. Low-beta (13–22 Hz) and -theta (4–7 Hz) oscillations were the primary features for identifying movement and tremor, respectively [43]. Importantly, movement detection often preceded the actual onset of movement. Classifiers trained on specific, cue-based tasks were able to successfully generalize to self-initiated movements that resemble everyday activities. However, distinct models appear necessary to optimize detection of voluntary movement versus postural tremor [41, 43].

Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of thalamic oscillatory dynamics in the pathophysiology of ET and support the use of LFP features as reliable biomarkers to detect tremor and guide neuromodulation. Incorporating these biomarkers into fully implantable, closed-loop DBS systems holds significant promise for advancing personalized, adaptive stimulation strategies aimed at improving clinical outcomes in ET (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Dystonia

In dystonia, LFPs recorded from the GPi – the primary DBS target – consistently show increased power in the theta range (4–12 Hz). This low-frequency activity has been correlated with EMG signal and symptom severity, reinforcing its pathological relevance [15, 30]. Directional coupling analyses reveal a bidirectional relationship between the GPi and affected muscles, with a dominant influence exerted by the GPi. Notably, this low-frequency activity is responsive to peripheral inputs, as demonstrated by its modulation during “geste antagoniste” in cervical dystonia [15].

This low-frequency oscillatory pattern appears to be distinct from those observed in other movement disorders, such as PD and Huntington’s disease (HD), further supporting its specificity to dystonia. Moreover, recordings from the GPi reveal greater low-frequency power than in the external pallidum (globus pallidus externus [GPe]), emphasizing its spatial specificity [10, 44]. The stability of this signal over time, confirmed through chronic recordings conducted months after DBS implantation, further supports its reliability as a biomarker for both disease monitoring and therapeutic targeting [44]. The therapeutic effects of DBS in dystonia may involve the suppression of abnormally synchronized low-frequency oscillations between the GPi and cortex, particularly in patients with phasic dystonia [45, 46]. Of note, the temporal dynamics of these changes differ substantially from those observed in PD, where modulation of beta activity by DBS or dopaminergic medication typically occurs within seconds, reflecting rapid circuit-level adjustments. In contrast, in dystonia, the reduction in pathological low-frequency power and the normalization of cortical coherence often develop gradually, over hours to days or even weeks, paralleling the delayed clinical response characteristic of this condition [45–47]. This slower timescale likely reflects plasticity-driven reorganization rather than acute suppression of pathological synchronization. Targeting regions within the GPi that exhibit maximal low-frequency power has been associated with better clinical outcomes, suggesting a functional relevance of this biomarker in guiding electrode placement [48]. Furthermore, recordings from patients with long-term DBS show that low-frequency activity continues to correlate with dystonia severity, reinforcing its enduring relevance for adaptive neuromodulation. Subtype-specific differences have also been reported, reflecting distinct underlying network dynamics. Phasic dystonia is associated with alpha-band oscillations (8–13 Hz) and functional coupling between GPi, GPe, and motor cortex, whereas tonic dystonia shows delta oscillations (2–4 Hz) and predominant coupling between the GPi and GPe [45, 48]. These patterns suggest phenotype-dependent variations in circuit dysfunction. Additionally, cervical dystonia has been shown to exhibit interhemispheric asymmetries in GPi activity across frequency bands, further highlighting the heterogeneity of oscillatory signatures across dystonia phenotypes [49].

Beyond low-frequency oscillations, a broader frequency spectrum has implications for both pathological and physiological motor control. For instance, walking has been shown to increase theta-alpha activity and reduce beta power, indicating that some oscillatory components may reflect normal motor function. Gamma-band activity is modulated during both movement and cognitive engagement and may contribute to motor learning and adaptation [44, 45]. These findings caution against indiscriminate suppression of neural oscillations during DBS as targeting pathological activity too broadly could interfere with essential physiological processes, as appears to be the case in dystonia patients with deterioration of gait after GPi DBS [47].

Finally, studies of cortico-subcortical coherence have revealed three main oscillatory networks: theta (pallido-temporal), alpha (pallido-cerebellar), and beta (cortico-pallidal) [44, 49]. Among these, only the alpha pallido-cerebellar network shows an inverse correlation with dystonia severity, emphasizing the importance of target modulation. Supporting this, both GPi and STN DBS were shown to reduce pathological alpha oscillations and interhemispheric motor cortex coherence, suggesting that network desynchronization is a core therapeutic mechanism [46]. Taken together, these findings support the pathophysiological relevance of low-frequency oscillations in dystonia and its potential as a biomarker for aDBS techniques (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Tics

Tics in the context of TS present as a childhood-onset disorder characterized by motor and phonic tics, often accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities. While many respond to pharmacological and behavioral therapies, a subset remains refractory. During tic episodes, the LFPs recorded in the thalamus, particularly in the centromedian (CM) nucleus, consistently show low-frequency oscillations in the theta-alpha/low-beta range (5–15 Hz), encompassing the theta (2–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and lower beta (13–20 Hz) bands [50]. These oscillations have been identified as biomarkers of TS, with their presence strongly correlating with tic severity and treatment responsiveness, particularly in cases with co-morbid obsessive-compulsive behaviors [50, 51]. This activity is also considered a general signature of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loop implicated in hyperkinesias, such as levodopa-induced dyskinesias and dystonia [15]. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that delta-theta (2–10 Hz) bands power within the CM nucleus are correlated with tic onset but not with voluntary movement, while reductions in cortical beta activity may precede premonitory urges [52].

In chronically implanted cases of TS, dynamic spectral changes in LFP patterns have been observed: while initial recordings at rest, conducted 3–5 days post-implantation, typically show prominent theta-alpha activity (5–15 Hz), chronic monitoring at rest, often performed with externalized leads during battery replacement, reveals a gradual shift toward both lower (theta, 5–7 Hz) and higher (beta, 13–35 Hz) frequency bands. The observed increase in the beta power may reflect the long-term DBS effects. Notably, in the context of obsessive-compulsive behavior as a comorbidity, greater LFP spectral power in the 5–15 Hz range shortly after implantation is associated with a better symptom control [53].

These findings place the CM nucleus as a primary DBS target for TS and highlight potential strategies for aDBS strategies. In this context, additional motor cortex (M1) strips have been used to detect tic-related activity. Newer devices utilize a single CM nucleus of the thalamus lead to both sense tics and trigger bilateral stimulation, demonstrating the feasibility of using low-frequency power increases (1–10 Hz) as a stimulation trigger [54]. However, several challenges remain. These include slow ramp times (gradual increase or decrease in stimulation intensity), which may hinder real-time tic suppression due to the rapid nature of frequency changes. Additionally, significant inter-subject variability requires the development of personalized algorithms to ensure effective stimulation and clinical outcomes (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Chorea

The use of DBS in HD and other hyperkinetic syndromes has underscored the utility of LFPs in optimizing therapeutic strategies for chorea. In these conditions, recordings of the GPi reveal distinct oscillatory patterns that aid in target location and serve as potential biomarkers for aDBS. In HD, LFPs commonly reveal elevated high-beta to low-gamma activity (26–43 Hz), correlating with chorea severity [55]. In contrast, dystonia is marked by increased low-frequency power (6–14 Hz), a spectral signature that may aid in distinguishing hyperkinetic disorders with overlapping clinical features [15]. As such, the ratio of low-frequency to high-beta/-gamma power provides a useful spectral power for differentiating these hyperkinetic movements and tailoring stimulation settings accordingly [33]. Furthermore, nonlinear analysis using Lempel-Ziv complexity reveals greater regularity in dystonic oscillations and increased irregularity in HD at higher frequencies, offering further refinement for aDBS strategies [54, 55].

Stimulation at the lateral border of the GPi and GPe has been linked to significant chorea reduction, highlighting this region as a potential DBS target [56]. Intraoperative LFP recordings in juvenile HD reveal alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) oscillations, similar to patterns seen in akinetic-rigid syndromes [15, 56]. Furthermore, resting-state LFPs correlate with clinical metrics, such as the Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS), supporting their potential use in ambulatory monitoring [55, 56]. The emergence of closed-loop DBS systems enables real-time adaptation based on LFP signatures – particularly high-beta and low-gamma activity, which track chorea severity in HD. However, long-term signal stability, particularly in the context of progressive brain atrophy, remains a critical limitation for implementing aDBS in this clinical practice context [56].

### Clinical Applications of LFP Recordings

#### Intraoperative Target Localization and Postsurgical Programming

Different from MERs that are capable of capturing high-frequency action potentials from individual neurons, LFPs offer wider spatial and temporal profiles of neural dynamics, allowing real-time physiological characterization of target areas during implantation [1, 10, 15, 17]. In this setting, LFPs are registered when the electrode reaches the planned target, and spectral analyses are performed to assess frequency-specific activity that supports accurate localization [10]. As previously described, in PD, beta-band oscillations (13–30 Hz) at rest can be detected across multiple nodes of the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical network but are typically more meaningful in the dorsolateral STN and the GPi, the primary surgical targets [31, 33]. In this context, intraoperative LFP recordings from DBS leads allow identification of an electrophysiological signature, providing a functional marker that complements imaging and anatomical landmarks, particularly in asleep DBS procedures in which intraoperative patient feedback is unavailable (Table 1) [17, 28].

Similarly, in other movement disorders such as ET and dystonia, LFP recordings can be used to verify the engagement of a target within the motor network. In the case of dystonia, increased power in the theta-alpha range (4–12 Hz) can be recorded from the motor subregion of the GPi, and in ET detection of movement- or posture-related oscillations at tremor frequency may help target location in the VIM [39, 40, 45, 49].

Beyond intraoperative targeting, postoperative LFP recordings of DBS-implanted patients may identify potential “hotspots” along the electrode axis for optimal stimulation guiding electrode contact selection and initial programming [57]. In this context, contacts exhibiting the highest amplitude beta oscillations should correlate with greater therapeutic efficacy, providing an objective physiological marker for stimulation settings. This approach has been studied comparing various outcome measures with standard monopolar review, revealing similar efficiency in terms of motor improvement and total electrical energy delivered but much shorter programming time and inter-rater variability (Table 1) [57–59].

#### LFPs as Biomarkers of Disease State: Outpatient Monitoring

LFPs represent a paradigm-shifting tool for monitoring symptom fluctuations and disease progression in complex movement disorders such as PD. While traditional clinical assessments – motor exams or patient diaries – provide only an interpretive snapshot of the patient’s condition and often fail to capture the dynamic, day-to-day fluctuations in symptom severity, LFPs provide objective and continuous physiologically grounded data that directly reflect the real-time activity of neural circuits involved in motor control [34]. As such, recordings over longer timeframes allow the detection of not only the presence of abnormal activity (i.e., elevated beta power) but also of how a parameter may change in response to medication, DBS therapy, or daily activities.

The development of newer generation implantable DBS devices with chronic sensing capabilities marks a significant advancement in this realm as they can record LFPs continuously from hours to weeks without requiring hospitalization or special equipment [34, 60]. These data could transform personalized neurology by enabling fine-tuning of DBS or the medication regimen based on real-time physiological feedback rather than on clinical guesswork. Integrating LFP monitoring into routine care may allow earlier symptom detection, more targeted treatment, and improved long-term outcomes [60].

The process of event recording for outpatient monitoring of DBS patients using LFPs is based on capturing neural activity in relation to specific clinical events (i.e., medication intake, motor tasks, symptom fluctuations, etc.) [61]. Recordings can be continuous, scheduled, or triggered, and events are marked manually or automatically using synced external devices, mobile apps, or clinician input. A critical element is time-locking neural data to these events, allowing the analysis of how LFP patterns change surrounding the specific trigger. For example, a drop in beta activity following medication or the occurrence of brief beta bursts just before or during episodes of freezing can help link brain signals to symptoms. The data are then stored in the implant or transferred to external devices for analysis (Table 1; Fig. 1) [60, 61].

#### aDBS and Personalized Therapy

The identification of reliable physiological biomarkers – particularly LFPs – has catalyzed a new era in neuromodulation, enabling the development of closed-loop systems such as aDBS. In contrast to conventional DBS, which delivers continuous stimulation using fixed parameters regardless of clinical states, aDBS employs real-time neurophysiological feedback to dynamically adjust stimulation in response to fluctuating symptom severity. This personalized, demand-driven approach optimizes therapeutic precision, improves symptom control, and significantly reduces stimulation-induced side effects and energy consumption [61, 62]. aDBS systems typically utilize LFPs recorded from the STN or GPi to detect pathological beta-band oscillations. When elevated beta power is recorded, stimulation is increased to suppress these oscillations. Conversely, when beta activity subsides due to treatment response, stimulation is reduced. This closed-loop mechanism allows for a more physiologically attuned modulation of neural activity compared to continuous DBS, which delivers fixed-parameter stimulation irrespective of symptom variability [61–63].

To be able to provide this technique, the system must integrate sensing, processing, and stimulation functions. Hardware requires an IPG model with built-in sensing capability and electrodes capable of delivering stimulation and record LFPs [34]. The recorded signals are then processed in real time by onboard or external software that runs signal analysis algorithms. Minimum and maximum thresholds must be predefined to allow the software to adjust dynamically the stimulation parameters (more often amplitude but also frequency or pulse width) [34, 60–63].

The control strategies underlying aDBS encompass a range of algorithmic approaches that differ in complexity, adaptability, and computational demands. Early implementations employed single-threshold controllers, in which stimulation is activated when a pathological oscillatory feature exceeds a predefined level and is deactivated when it drops below that threshold [34, 60, 61]. While effective in proof-of-concept studies, these designs can produce frequent switching and stimulation instability when signal fluctuations hover near the threshold. To mitigate this, more recent systems have introduced dual-threshold or hysteresis-based architectures, providing a buffer zone that minimizes rapid toggling and enhances temporal stability [61, 62]. Importantly, prior to the availability of fully implantable aDBS systems, several early proof-of-concept studies employed a fundamentally different control strategy based on a linear proportional algorithm [63–65]. In these paradigms, stimulation amplitude was continuously scaled in proportion to the magnitude of the pathological biomarker (most commonly beta-band power), rather than being governed by discrete on/off thresholds. This approach was tested over substantially longer time windows, ranging from approximately 2 h to 24 h, and demonstrated stable symptom control with reduced stimulation delivery compared to conventional DBS [64]. Linear proportional control differs conceptually from dual-threshold or hysteresis-based architectures in that it provides a continuous, graded modulation of stimulation rather than binary or state-dependent switching. Notably, this algorithm has since been incorporated into commercially available fully implantable aDBS devices, underscoring its translational relevance and ongoing clinical applicability [63–65].

Beyond amplitude-triggered control, more sophisticated models have emerged, including reinforcement learning and model-predictive algorithms that continuously optimize stimulation parameters (e.g., amplitude, frequency, pulse width) based on probabilistic estimates of symptom state and network response [62, 66]. These adaptive schemes aim to personalize therapy to the patient’s evolving neural and behavioral context, paving the way for context-aware and multimodal control strategies that integrate cortical, peripheral, or wearable sensor inputs. Despite these advances, several technical and translational challenges remain. A major obstacle is the latency between biomarker detection and stimulation adjustment. Even brief delays can compromise efficacy for transient phenomena such as tremor bursts, dyskinesia onset, or tic expression. Artifact handling remains a central limitation: stimulation artifacts, movement-related noise, and electromagnetic interference often contaminate the recorded LFP, requiring complex real-time filtering and blanking strategies that may inadvertently remove physiologically relevant data [34, 60–62]. Cross-channel interference, arising from concurrent stimulation and sensing through neighboring contacts, can distort signal fidelity, particularly in multipolar and directional electrodes.

Additional technical constraints include limited bandwidth and on-board computational power of implantable devices, which restrict the implementation of high-resolution spectral analyses and multivariate models. Long-term signal stability is another unresolved issue; impedance drift, encapsulation tissue growth, and progressive neurodegeneration can alter signal amplitude and spectral composition, challenging longitudinal calibration of adaptive algorithms [34, 60–62, 66]. Moreover, the generalizability of biomarkers across behavioral states and disease stages remains uncertain as in the case of beta power, for instance, which may not consistently predict motor impairment during complex or cognitive tasks [28, 31, 34]. Finally, data privacy, cybersecurity, and regulatory validation of closed-loop algorithms represent emerging frontiers that will require rigorous oversight as adaptive systems transition from research to routine clinical practice [34, 67]. Ongoing work aims to address these challenges through advances in amplifier design, adaptive noise suppression, multi-site sensing (including cortico-subcortical coupling metrics), and machine learning (ML) frameworks that combine physiological and behavioral data streams to deliver more reliable, individualized neuromodulation [67].

Historically, the pivotal study by Little et al. [61] published in 2013 is widely credited as the first in-human proof of concept for aDBS in PD, showing that, compared to conventional DBS, aDBS provided greater motor improvement, reduced stimulation time, and lowered energy consumption. Since then, several other groups have significantly advanced the field, particularly through the development of fully implantable bidirectional systems capable of chronic sensing and adaptive control. The first study demonstrating stable long-term recordings of beta-band activity in freely moving patients was published in 2021 using the Summit RC + S platform, establishing the feasibility of continuous biomarker-based modulation in real-world conditions [65]. Earlier work from one of the groups involved in this study had already shown the utility of motor cortex signals for aDBS control, demonstrating that cortical gamma activity and phase-amplitude coupling can serve as reliable, movement-related control signals for real-time modulation [35]. Together, these studies illustrate a coherent trajectory from chronic biomarker streaming to multimodal network-level sensing, providing the physiological basis for increasingly personalized closed-loop neuromodulation. Likewise, recent work has highlighted the importance of movement-state specificity and physiological context for next-generation aDBS strategies. Busch and colleagues demonstrated that the performance of single-threshold adaptive stimulation critically depends on parameter choice, the patient’s motor state, and the intrinsic controllability of STN beta activity, underscoring the need for context-aware control policies [66]. Complementing these findings, Farokhniaee et al. [32] provided direct evidence that gait elicits distinct beta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling patterns within the STN of PD patients, reinforcing the potential for gait-informed or task-specific adaptive stimulation frameworks [32]. Also, foundation-laying work has established biomarker-driven control frameworks and charted a path for the translation of fully implantable aDBS systems, including detailed reviews of feedback control architectures and clinical considerations [68, 69]. Building upon these developments, a large retrospective analysis of 62 PD patients treated with bilateral STN DBS found that both conventional DBS and aDBS improved motor symptoms and quality of life; however, aDBS provided additional long-term advantages, including a reduced levodopa equivalent daily dose and greater improvements in MDS-UPDRS II, IV, and PDQ-39 scores [70]. Supporting these findings, a pilot study of 4 PD patients reported more than 50% reduction in the duration of their most bothersome motor symptoms when using personalized aDBS protocols. The benefits were especially notable in tremor-dominant patients, who also experienced improved quality of life due to more consistent symptom control throughout daily activities [71]. Further reinforcing the clinical relevance of aDBS, the 2025 Delphi consensus found that 90–95% of movement disorder specialists acknowledged the therapeutic value of aDBS, particularly for patients experiencing motor fluctuations and dyskinesias. In this consensus, experts highlight its capacity to deliver personalized, adaptive therapy while reducing the burden of frequent reprogramming [72].

A recent multicenter pivotal study, the ADAPT-PD trial, provided the strongest clinical validation to date for LFP-guided aDBS. Using individualized alpha-beta band power as the control signal, both single- and dual-threshold aDBS modes produced motor outcomes comparable to continuous DBS while significantly reducing energy delivery. Importantly, chronic sensing and at-home adaptive stimulation were feasible and well tolerated, with most participants preferring aDBS [73]. These findings reinforce the translational relevance of LFP biomarkers and support their expanding role in personalized neuromodulation.

Collectively, these findings establish aDBS as a clinically relevant advancement in the treatment of PD, offering a tailored, patient-specific approach that adapts to each patient’s evolving neurophysiological needs in both clinical and real-world settings. As of 2025, aDBS has moved from experimental trials to early clinical use with formal regulatory agency approval in Japan, North America, and Europe, allowing commercial rollout in these regions. Early clinical adoption is underway at specialized centers, supported by positive outcomes from previous trials. Broader access and long-term efficacy data are expected to expand use over the next few years.

aDBS for ET, dystonia, and tics remains largely investigational and not yet used in routine clinical practice. Although preliminary findings show comparable or better symptom control than conventional DBS, potential lower battery consumption and fewer stimulation-induced side effects, specific challenges are significant [34]. For example, in the case of dystonia, lack of a robust, fast-responding biomarker (unlike the beta-band signal used in PD), delayed clinical response over days or weeks complicates the real-time feedback needed for adaptive control [46, 49]. For tics in TS, there are additional issues related to the nature of the disease that add layers of complexity. These include the fact that tics can vary in frequency, intensity, and anatomical distribution and also the burden of psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., OCD, mood disorders), which significantly affect overall quality of life outcomes [52, 54]. Similarly, ET lacks a consistent, tremor-specific biomarker, making it difficult to trigger stimulation accurately [43]. Tremor-related signals in the 4–12 Hz range are often unstable, vary across activities, and overlap with normal movement, complicating signal detection. Rapid and episodic tremor onset also poses a problem as current aDBS systems may not respond quickly enough to suppress symptoms effectively [42–44].

### Limitations of Current LFP Paradigms

Despite their growing clinical and research relevance, current LFP paradigms face several important limitations that constrain their generalizability and translational impact: (i) LFPs recording methods have limited spatial resolution as they represent the summed activity of neuronal populations within a few millimeters of the recording site, impeding clear isolation of specific circuit elements or the discrimination of overlapping signals from nearby nuclei or white matter tracts. (ii) Signal interpretation remains complex as oscillatory activity in a given frequency band may have different functional or pathological meanings depending on the recording site, disease state, and behavioral context. (iii) Recording conditions often differ across studies and patients, for example, intraoperative LFPs are typically recorded under anesthesia or off-medication states, misrepresenting physiological activity during daily life [8, 10, 28, 34]. Chronic sensing systems now allow outpatient recordings, but signal quality degrades over time due to impedance changes, encapsulation tissue growth, or device-related artifacts. (iv) Standardization and reproducibility remain challenges once there is no universally accepted framework for preprocessing, filtering, or defining oscillatory bands, leading to inconsistencies across studies and limiting comparisons. (v) Biomarker specificity is imperfect: while beta activity is well established in PD, biomarkers for dystonia, tremor, and tics are more variable, context dependent, and slower to change, limiting their use for aDBS. Finally, (vi) technological constraints such as limited sampling bandwidth, on-board storage, and computational capacity of implantable devices restrict the complexity of analyses and the implementation of advanced adaptive algorithms [8, 10, 28, 44].

Another major limitation concerns the reproducibility and generalizability of spectral biomarkers across studies, centers, and recording platforms. Differences in acquisition hardware, referencing schemes, electrode geometry, and signal preprocessing can significantly alter power spectra, coupling metrics, and inferred physiological relationships [8, 10, 28]. Furthermore, variability in behavioral paradigms, medication states, and patient selection criteria complicates direct comparisons and may contribute to inconsistent findings across the literature. The lack of standardized pipelines for data acquisition, artifact rejection, and spectral decomposition remains a critical barrier to biomarker validation and large-scale meta-analytic synthesis [8, 10]. Multicenter initiatives and open data frameworks are increasingly being developed to address these challenges, aiming to harmonize methodology and improve cross-study reproducibility, an essential step toward establishing robust, generalizable LFP biomarkers suitable for clinical translation.

In parallel, the integration of ML, deep learning (DL), and advanced signal processing techniques has emerged as a powerful approach to extract meaningful patterns from complex LFP datasets. These data-driven frameworks enable automated feature selection, motor- and behavioral-state decoding, and prediction of clinical fluctuations with higher precision than conventional spectral metrics [31, 34]. Dimensionality-reduction methods such as principal component analysis, independent component analysis, and manifold learning further enhance interpretability by identifying latent neural states across recording sites and time scales. In the context of aDBS, ML and deep learning algorithms are increasingly used to personalize stimulation parameters, optimize control policies, and improve artifact rejection through real-time pattern recognition. Although still at early translational stages, these computational approaches represent a major step toward intelligent, self-adjusting neuromodulation systems capable of continuously adapting to each patient’s evolving neural dynamics [34, 35, 74]. Future paradigms integrating LFPs with cortical recordings, wearable sensors, and ML-based analytics may overcome many of these challenges, enabling more precise and robust biomarker-driven neuromodulation.

## Conclusion

LFPs currently represent a reliable neurophysiological method for mapping brain network activity in movement disorders. Across PD, tremor, dystonia, tics, and chorea, specific oscillatory patterns recorded from stereotactic surgery targets provide critical insight into disease mechanisms, symptom expression, and treatment response [44, 57]. In PD, the pathological role of beta oscillations, particularly low-beta activity in the STN and GPi, is well established and tightly linked to bradykinesia and rigidity, whereas dyskinesias are characterized by broadband gamma activity and disrupted CFC [31]. Other phenotypes such as tremor, PIGD, and FOG exhibit distinct LFP signatures involving theta, alpha, or burst-like activity, reflecting circuit-level heterogeneity [29, 31]. In ET and dystonia, pathological synchronization in the theta, alpha, and beta ranges – modulated by task, posture, or symptom severity – supports their role as state-dependent biomarkers [39–45, 48]. Similarly, LFPs in tics and chorea reveal low-frequency and beta-gamma abnormalities, respectively, with disease- and phenotype-specific patterns that inform both diagnosis and DBS targeting [54, 55]. Beyond their diagnostic and pathophysiological value, LFPs have transformed DBS into a more precise, responsive therapy. From guiding intraoperative targeting and electrode programming to enabling real-time outpatient monitoring, LFPs offer continuous, objective insight into neural dynamics. Most notably, they form the backbone of aDBS, which dynamically adjusts stimulation in response to physiological feedback. In PD, aDBS has moved from experimental validation to early clinical implementation, demonstrating improved motor control, reduced side effects, and enhanced quality of life [8, 69, 70]. However, in other disorders like dystonia, ET, and TS, technical challenges – such as biomarker variability, delayed clinical responses, or comorbidities – still limit routine use.

Taken together, LFPs not only deepen our understanding of the oscillatory underpinnings of movement disorders but also enable a paradigm shift toward biomarker-driven, patient-specific neuromodulation. As sensing technologies, algorithm development, and clinical integration continue to evolve, LFPs are poised to play a central role in the next generation of personalized neurotherapeutics.

## Abstract
Objectives: With the development of advanced neuroimaging techniques, including resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging and diffusion tensor imaging, Parkinson’s disease (PD) has increasingly been recognized as a complex brain network disorder. ## 1. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by impaired motor function with resting tremors, bradykinesia, akinetic-rigidity (AR), postural instability, and gait disturbance. In addition, most patients experience a range of non-motor dysfunctions that are heterogeneous in nature. The stages at which motor symptoms appear vary during the progression of PD [1,2]. Traditionally, PD has been attributed to dopaminergic neuronal degeneration within the substantia nigra (SN) pars compacta and consequent basal ganglia (BG) dysfunction [2,3,4]. However, emerging evidence substantiates a broader conceptualization of PD as a brain network disorder, characterized by aberrant functional and structural connectivity across distributed cortical and subcortical systems. The advent and maturation of sophisticated neuroimaging techniques, particularly resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), have enabled in vivo characterization of distributed network abnormalities in PD and facilitated connectome-level investigations [5,6,7,8].

Key implicated networks include the sensorimotor network (SMN), default mode network (DMN), frontoparietal network (FPN), striatal-thalamo-cortical (STC) and cerebello-thalamo-cortical (CTC) circuitry. Dysregulation within and between these networks contributes to distinct clinical phenotypes such as tremor-dominant, postural instability and gait disorder (PIGD), freezing of gait (FoG), and AR subtypes. Network-level analyses afford refined diagnostic and prognostic markers beyond traditional clinical assessments. In particular, deep brain stimulation (DBS), targeting principally the subthalamic nucleus (STN) and globus pallidus internus (GPi), constitutes a cornerstone of advanced PD treatment. Accumulating evidence indicates that DBS-mediated symptomatic improvement arises from modulation of large-scale brain networks, rather than localized effects alone [9]. This review synthesizes contemporary findings on PD-related brain network alterations, delineates symptom-specific network patterns, and characterizes DBS-modulated networks, underscoring the translational potential of symptom-targeted neuromodulation to optimize therapeutic outcomes.

## 2. Materials and Methods

The literature search was conducted using the PubMed and Web of Science databases, covering publications from 2015 to 2025. The search included the following keywords and their combinations: “Parkinson’s disease”, “PD”, “tremor”, “rigidity”, “akinetic” “PIGD”, “FoG”, “functional connectivity”, “structural connectivity”, “deep brain stimulation”, and “brain networks”. This study focused on previously published original research articles related to brain networks in PD. As the aim of the review was to construct symptom-specific functional connectivity networks and DBS modulation networks in PD, the following inclusion criteria were applied: 1) The research explicitly addressed PD and its core motor symptoms (e.g., tremor, rigidity, PIGD, and FG), or clearly examined the network effects of DBS. 2) Studies needed to employ functional or structural connectivity methods to investigate brain network characteristics in PD. 3) Studies further need to report statistically significant region-to-region connections, specifying the anatomical regions involved. To ensure anatomical interpretability and cross-study comparability, only connectivity approaches that permitted explicit mapping between brain regions, such as seed-based functional connectivity and diffusion tractography, were included.

Consequently, studies that did not report anatomically defined connections, such as ICA-only analyses, global graph-theoretical metrics without region-specific edges, or whole-network summaries lacking node-to-node detail, were excluded because these methods do not allow direct construction of symptom-specific anatomical networks.

In addition, given the narrative and connectivity-focused nature of this review, we did not apply a formal risk-of-bias assessment tool. However, a qualitative appraisal of included studies was performed, considering participant characterization and PD diagnostic criteria, sample size and subgroup definition, imaging modality and analytical approach, statistical thresholding and correction methods, and the clarity of anatomically defined region-to-region connectivity reporting. Figure 1 outlines our study and search approach.

The original article data for constructing both our PD general network and symptom-specific network come from brain region coordinates with significant connectivity changes compared to healthy controls. Most of the included studies used correlation coefficient methods for constructing brain networks by employing rs-fMRI. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined as seed regions for functional connectivity, after which voxel-wise correlation analysis was conducted. Next Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated the between the average time series within the ROIs and the time series from other brain regions. Research on DBS-modulated networks incorporated rs-fMRI, as well as DTI to construct structural connectivity. White matter fiber tracking or DBS fiber-filtering techniques were used to precisely locate DBS related connections. Imaging modalities employed in the studies included in this review and the visualization approach used for network construction in the present work are provided in Figure 2. The figure also summarizes the number of studies reporting significant findings within the PD general network, tremor network, PIGD network, FOG network, AR network, and DBS-modulated network.

Cortical parcellation was based on Thomas Yeo’s 7-network cortical atlas, which divides the cerebral cortex into seven networks: the DMN, FPN, limbic network, ventral attention network, dorsal attention network, SMN, and visual network [10]. Similarly, cerebellar parcellation was defined according to Buckner’s 7-network cerebellar atlas [11]. In this way, the major resting-state networks of the nodes were determined. Using BrainNet Viewer [12], we visualized the nodes and their connections to provide a clearer representation of the network-level connectivity within the brain.

## 3. General Brain Network Abnormalities in PD

Functional connectivity (FC) in PD patients as revealed by rs-fMRI studies, has provided a comprehensive understanding of general network abnormalities in PD and has been compared with symptom-specific network abnormalities in subsequent sections.

The role of the cerebellum in PD has been highlighted in recent studies. For example, Palmer et al. found widespread alterations in cerebello-cortical FC, changes that were associated with PD-related motor and cognitive impairments [13]. In addition, increased cerebellar local activity and enhanced FC among cerebellar structures have been reported; these findings have been interpreted as potentially reflecting a compensatory mechanism [14,15].

The SMN encompasses the primary sensorimotor, premotor, and supplementary motor area (SMA), and is crucial for voluntary movements [16]. In PD, the SMN exhibits reduced functional connectivity, both internally within the network and between interactions with other networks [17]. Wei et al. found enhanced FC between the SN and sensorimotor regions, suggesting that alterations in the nigro-striatal pathway may be related to motor deficits in PD [18]. Shima et al. focused on striatal dopamine-depleted areas, and found decreased FC with the motor cortex and SN. However, the STN exhibited enhanced FC with the lateral premotor cortex and primary motor cortex (M1) [19].

Cognitive impairment is a critical non-motor symptom of PD. Indeed, with disease progression, the majority of patients eventually develop dementia [20]. Importantly, the DMN is critically involved in cognitive impairment in PD [21,22]. Fathy et al. found reduced FC between the dorsal anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex in PD. This reduction may lead to destabilization of the DMN and FPN, thereby affecting patients’ cognitive function [23]. Llewelyn et al. discovered that the hippocampus of PD patients showed dysconnectivity with the paracingulate gyri [24]. Chen et al. found that, in the DMN, FC between the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and right precuneus, left cuneus, and right angular gyrus was significantly increased [22]. Thibes et al. further showed widespread reduction in FC between the PCC and motor cortex [25]. Wang and colleagues determined that enhanced FC between the ventromedial putamen and left paracentral lobule, as well as multiple nodes of the DMN in PD patients [26].

The PD general network exhibits extensive FC alterations between the basal ganglia, SMN, and DMN. These findings underscore that PD reflects widespread large-scale network disruptions rather than pathology restricted to the SN. A total of 10 studies were included in this research, and the relevant results are summarized and visualized in Figure 3a.

## 4. Symptom-Specific Brain Network Abnormalities in PD

### 4.1. The Tremor Network

Tremor is typically characterized as an involuntary, rhythmic shaking that primarily occurs at rest and is therefore referred to as resting tremor. It is often one of the earliest symptoms observed in patients with PD. The tremor-dominant subtype of PD (PD-TD) is distinguished by resting tremor as its primary feature [27]. Compared to other subtypes, PD-TD progresses more slowly, poses a lower risk of cognitive impairment, and responds more favorably to DBS therapy [28].

Helmich et al. proposed a model in which, in PD-TD, the basal ganglia trigger tremor episodes, whereas the CTC circuit is associated with producing the tremor [29]. The cerebellum and the STN are crucial hubs of these two loops [30,31,32]. In PD-TD, functional coupling within and between these hubs is markedly altered. Multiple studies have demonstrated abnormal bidirectional cerebello-cortical coupling in PD-TD, particularly between the cerebellum and motor cortex [33,34,35]. In addition, cerebello-prefrontal FC has also been reported to be disrupted, indicating that tremor-related cerebellar dysfunction extends beyond the motor loop [36,37]. Similarly, FC between the STN and the motor cortex, as well as between the STN and the cerebellum, is disturbed in tremor patients [34,38,39]. Another key node shared by these two circuits is the primary motor cortex. In PD-TD, M1 shows not only abnormal FC with the basal ganglia and the cerebellum [40], but also increased connectivity with the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN) and the inferior parietal lobule [41]. These convergent alterations at the main nodes of the basal ganglia and the CTC circuit support the view that network-level dysfunction within these loops plays a central role in the generation and modulation of Parkinsonian tremor.

In addition, PD patients have been found to increasingly rely on visual input to control their movements. The occipital lobe is an important component of the visual network [42], and studies in PD-TD have reported reduced functional connectivity between the occipital gyrus and other cortical regions [43] suggesting that visual network alterations may accompany tremor network reorganization. However, this feature is more pronounced in PIGD patients.

The tremor network is characterized by FC alterations in the CTC circuit and basal ganglia. In particular, the FC among the cerebellum, STN, and motor cortex shows notably prominent alterations. It is noteworthy that the DMN shows relatively limited FC alterations within the tremor network. This finding may be associated with the lower risk of cognitive impairment in the PD-TD subtype. Overall, these findings are consistent with abnormal oscillatory interactions within coupled the CTC and basal ganglia circuits. In total, 13 studies were included in the analysis of the tremor network. These observations are collectively summarized and illustrated in Figure 4a.

### 4.2. The PIGD Network

The PIGD subtype (PD-PIGD) is characterized primarily by impaired postural control and gait disturbances, including slow walking, difficulty in initiating movement, and balance instability [27]. It is more commonly observed in the later stages of the disease and has a direct impact on patients’ daily functioning and quality of life. Compared to PD-TD, PD-PIGD is often associated with more rapid disease progression, more severe cognitive impairment, and poorer responsiveness to treatment [44,45].

Across resting-state fMRI studies, PD-PIGD shows predominantly reduced FC within the STC circuitry, including decreased intrinsic coupling between the caudate and putamen and weakened connectivity between the STN and putamen and pons [39,41]. In addition, PD-PIGD exhibits reduced FC across multiple cortical regions, including the dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, SMA, and M1, with the occipital cortex showing particularly widespread decreases in connectivity [40,43,46].

Both PD-TD and PD-PIGD share several compensatory mechanisms, including increased cerebellum–SMN FC and increased STN connectivity with the sensorimotor cortex and visual cortex [35,38,47]. However, hyperconnectivity between the PPN and the sensorimotor cortex has been reported exclusively in PD-PIGD [41].

A total of nine studies were included in the analysis of the PIGD network. The summarized findings suggest that the PIGD and TD networks share some common patterns of injury and compensatory mechanisms. However, the PIGD subtype appears to exhibit more severe FC disruptions within the striatum and visual cortex, potentially reflecting impaired visuomotor integration underlying balance deficits and fall risk in PD-PIGD patients. A schematic summary of these findings is illustrated in Figure 4b.

### 4.3. The FoG Network

FoG is characterized by brief episodes of an inability to step or by extremely short steps. These episodes typically occur at gait initiation or when turning during walking [48]. Although FoG is considered a specific motor manifestation within the PIGD subtype, it is closely associated with disease progression and non-motor symptoms in all PD patients [49]. Unlike other symptoms of PD, FoG shows limited responsiveness to conventional medical and surgical treatments [50].

Both structural damage to the midbrain and a marked reduction in midbrain–cortical connectivity have been established in PD-FoG patients [51]. The PPN, a major nucleus of the mesencephalic locomotor region, plays a crucial role in FoG pathogenesis, and PD-FoG patients exhibit abnormal PPN FC with corticopontine–cerebellar pathways [52,53]. The dorsal raphe nucleus, the principal serotonergic nucleus in the brainstem, also shows significantly reduced FC with the SMA, left superior frontal gyrus and left median cingulate cortex [54,55].

In PD-FoG patients, widespread reduced FC has been observed within the SMN, DMN, and visual network [56]. These include impaired FC between the left parietal opercular cortex and primary somatosensory and auditory areas [57], between the right middle frontal gyrus and DMN nodes [58] and between the right lateral posterior thalamic nuclei and the right inferior parietal lobule [59], as well as disrupted connectivity within frontoparietal–limbic–striatal circuits and decreased cerebellar–prefrontal FC [60,61].

A total of nine studies were included in the analysis of the FoG network. Compared to other networks, this network appears to exhibit more pronounced structural and functional alterations in the midbrain, indicating that midbrain structures, notably the PPN, likely play a pivotal role in FoG pathophysiology. These abnormalities are proposed to disrupt the interaction between the mesencephalic locomotor region and cortical executive and SMN, leading to impaired automatic gait control and increased reliance on ineffective cortical compensation during gait initiation and turning [51,62]. In addition, widespread FC alterations are also observed in the SMN, DMN, visual network, and cerebellum. The involved brain structures within the FoG network are illustrated in Figure 4c.

### 4.4. The AR Network

PD-AR is primarily characterized by akinesia, bradykinesia, and rigidity. PD-AR patients are more prone to developing depressive symptoms and cognitive decline than other subtypes [63]. Previous studies have also indicated that these patients are particularly vulnerable to reductions in procedural learning and memory [64]. Depression and cognitive impairment also significantly impact the quality of life in PD-AR patients.

The right fronto-insular cortex (rFIC) is a critical node of cognitive control shows reduced FC with the salience network [65,66]. The DMN is critically involved in cognitive processes [67]. In PD-AR, a decline in FC within the posterior DMN, accompanied by enhanced compensatory FC in the anterior DMN [68]. Hu et al. further reported distinct FC patterns between the PD-TD and PD-AR subtypes. Specifically, PD-TD patients engaged greater cerebellar resources, whereas those with the AR subtype predominantly recruited frontal lobe resources [69].

A total of three studies investigating the AR network were included. Summarizing the findings, limited evidence suggests that PD-AR patients may show alterations in cognition-related networks, including the DMN, which is implicated in multiple cognitive processes. However, because only three studies were included, these observations should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further validation. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that PD-AR patients are more prone to cognitive decline. The summarized results are illustrated in Figure 4d. ### 4.5. DBS-Modulated Network

DBS is a well-established treatment for PD, of which the STN and GPi are the two major targets [70]. DBS modulated multi-network abnormalities and the resulting FC changes have been shown to be associated with improvements in motor and psychiatric symptoms in PD patients [71]. Different network nodes may be modulated by different stimulation targets, potentially resulting in similar clinical outcomes [72]. Therefore, we summarized the structural and functional connectivity features of the volume of tissue activated (VTA) in both STN-DBS and GPi-DBS from previous studies to identify the modulated network of DBS in PD.

STN-DBS may exert its effects not only by directly stimulating the STN but also by modulating distant brain regions via white matter fiber pathways [73]. Studies have shown that the STN-VTA connects to the brainstem, cerebellum, and motor and premotor cortices [9,72,74,75,76,77]. The hyperdirect pathway (HDP), which connects the STN and motor cortex, has been confirmed in multiple studies and may represent a potential therapeutic target in PD [9,74]. Indeed, Chen et al. revealed that STN-DBS may deactivate the motor cortex [78].

Furthermore, Accolla et al. suggested that the STN is also connected with limbic and associative areas, as well as medial temporal regions [75]. Calvano et al. further showed connections of the STN with the prefrontal cortex [77]. Bai et al. demonstrated that DBS modulates FC within the SMN and across multiple networks centered on the FPN. The authors further indicated that such effects are symptom-specific [71]. Shen et al. identified two distinct circuits: the GPi circuit that selectively responded to high-frequency stimulation and was associated with overall motor improvement; and the M1 circuit that exhibited progressive deactivation over time and was linked to reduced bradykinesia [79].

DBS may alleviate specific motor symptoms through modulation of distinct white matter tracts. For example, tremor improvement has been associated with stimulation of pathways connected to the M1 and cerebellum, whereas axial symptom improvement has been associated with pathways connected to the SMA and brainstem. Bradykinesia and rigidity improvements have been associated with pathways connected to the supplementary motor and premotor cortices, respectively [80]. In addition, improvement in FoG has been linked to stimulation volumes structurally connected to motor areas, the prefrontal cortex, and the GPi, whereas stimulation of the lenticular fasciculus may be associated with worsening of FoG [81].

Moreover, the functional activity of the motor cortex induced by DBS varies across different motor phenotypes. DiMarzio et al. observed that DBS led to deactivation of the M1 in TD-PD patients but activation of both the M1 and SMA in the PD-AR subtype. In addition, STN-DBS resulted in M1 activation in PD-PIGD patients, whereas GPi-DBS resulted in M1 deactivation [82].

In the classical model of the BG, under Parkinsonian conditions, the loss of dopamine in the SN leads to hypoactivity in the direct pathway and hyperactivity in the indirect pathway. These alterations result in reduced inhibition from the striatum to the GPi and increased excitation from the STN, ultimately causing excessive output from the GPi, leading to excessive inhibition of the thalamus and cortex, which results in motor suppression [32].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that both STN-DBS and GPi-DBS improve motor symptoms and functioning in PD patients. Although STN-DBS has been reported to produce greater motor improvements, no significant differences between the two targets have been observed in cognition, mood, or behavior [83,84]. These overlapping clinical effects are consistent with the fact that both STN-DBS and GPi-DBS engage motor-related CTC and basal ganglia circuitry, showing partially overlapping modulation of SMN. However, STN-DBS tends to exert stronger effects on SMA connectivity and more consistently modulates connections with cerebellum pathways and motor thalamus, whereas GPi-DBS shows relatively stronger links with primary motor and sensory cortex [85,86].

The DBS-modulated network included 14 articles. Overall, DBS stimulation was found to exert significant effects on the motor cortex, particularly the M1 and SMA, which likely underlie its therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, compared with GPi-DBS, STN-DBS affected a broader range of brain structures and exerted stronger modulatory effects on the motor cortex. Importantly, the modulatory effects of DBS also differ significantly across distinct motor symptoms. We have illustrated the modulate network of DBS in Figure 3b.

## 5. Discussion

With advances in research on brain network disorders and the development of advanced imaging techniques, such as fMRI and DTI, it has become possible to trace and analyze abnormal signal transmission within brain networks [87]. PD is not merely the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN, but it also involves dysregulation across multiple brain regions. Overall, these findings provide deeper insight into PD-related network disturbances and the phenotype-specific effects of DBS.

The construction of PD general brain network abnormalities is based on fMRI studies comparing PD patients with healthy controls. For this reason, the PD general network encompasses broader alterations in resting-state networks and brain structures compared to symptom-specific brain network abnormalities. For instance, network-level and cross-network alterations involving the SMN, DMN, FPN, cerebellum, brainstem, and BG have been consistently demonstrated. Dysfunction within the CTC circuit is thought to be strongly associated with the core motor manifestations of PD, while the DMN predominantly contributes to non-motor symptoms. FC disruptions have been identified both within the cerebellum and between the cerebellum and cortical regions, and such cerebello–cortical alterations have been associated with impairments in motor coordination, balance control, and also cognitive performance in PD [13,14]. Consistent with this, increased cerebellar FC in patients with frequent falls has been interpreted as reflecting a compensatory recruitment additional network resources to counteract gait and postural instability [14]. At the large-scale network level, altered FC within the DMN and between DMN hubs, such as the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex, and motor systems has been associated with cognitive impairment and with reduced efficiency of cognitive–motor integration in PD [22,23,25].

In the context of PD symptom-specific brain network abnormalities, each symptom is characterized by distinctive functional network alterations, yet overlapping patterns can also be observed across symptoms.

The tremor network has received the most extensive investigation. Connectivity changes centered on the cerebellum, BG, and motor cortex are particularly prominent in the tremor subtype, which are consistently interpreted as reflecting abnormal rhythm generation and amplification within coupled CTC and basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical circuits [33,36,38,39]. In particular, abnormal coupling between the dentate nucleus and the primary motor cortex has been directly associated with tremor severity, supporting a central role of cerebellar output in tremor maintenance [36]. Consistent with this oscillatory network architecture, DBS targeting the CTC circuitry appears to be more effective compared with stimulation of other networks [28].

The PIGD network shares certain similarities with the tremor network; however, PD-PIGD patients exhibit more pronounced FC impairments within the visual cortex and visuospatial associative regions [37,43]. These alterations are likely associated with the increased risk of falls and balance deficits observed in these patients, as impaired visual–motor integration and reduced efficiency of using visuospatial cues can weaken postural control during gait and turning [41,58]. The emergence of FoG indicates that PD has progressed to an advanced stage, where both pharmacological therapy and DBS show limited efficacy. Widespread reductions in functional connectivity have been observed across the whole brain within the FoG networks, including midbrain, frontal, parietal, and cerebellar regions [53,57]. Notably, alterations in midbrain connectivity seem to play a critical role [52,63], and have been proposed to disrupt interactions between brainstem locomotor circuits and frontal executive network and SMN, leading to loss of automatic gait control and increased reliance on vulnerable executive compensation during gait initiation and turning [51,61].

PD-AR is frequently accompanied by depression and cognitive impairment, both of which significantly compromise patients’ quality of life. Within the AR network, the DMN, which is closely linked to cognitive processing, exhibits marked FC disruptions [68], suggesting early vulnerability of large-scale cognitive networks in rigidity-dominant PD and providing a network basis for depressive and cognitive symptoms. The SMN encompasses the somatosensory and motor cortices and extends into the SMA, playing a crucial role in the initiation of voluntary movements. In AR patients, reduced connectivity and altered fronto-insular coupling have been shown to impair the switching between executive and motor networks [66,69], thereby elevating the threshold for voluntary movement initiation and contributing to bradykinesia and rigidity.

Alterations in the SMN are consistently observed across all four symptom-specific networks and are also prominent in the PD general network, underscoring its critical role. Correcting or mitigating these abnormalities may represent an important network-level mechanism contributing to motor symptom improvement in PD.

DBS delivers electrical pulses to specific targets, such as the STN and GPi. Through connected white matter tracts and neural circuits, particularly the HDP linking the STN with the (pre)motor cortex, DBS may modulate large-scale distributed networks to achieve therapeutic effects [9]. A sophisticated understanding of how the brain responds to DBS is a prerequisite for enhancing its therapeutic efficacy and minimizing potential side effects [79]. A synthesis of previous research indicates that the effects of DBS are not limited to motor-related regions such as the SMN and cerebellum, but also extend to networks associated with non-motor symptoms. Although this review focuses on motor networks, non-motor circuits, including limbic and associative networks, may also be engaged by DBS-related modulation and warrant further investigation. While DBS can provide substantial motor improvements, optimizing each motor symptom may require stimulation of distinct white matter tracts. If electrodes are not ideally placed or if postoperative programming of DBS parameters, which still largely relies on subjective experience, is suboptimal, motor symptom control may be inadequate, and side effects may occur [80,88]. DBS is the most effective surgical treatment for PD, with its efficacy validated over the past three decades. A thorough understanding of symptom-specific network abnormalities in PD and the neural mechanisms underlying DBS treatment effects is essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and guiding clinicians in surgical planning and postoperative programming. Emerging DBS technologies, such as directional and adaptive DBS, may offer hardware-based solutions, yet targeting symptom-specific networks and understanding the symptom-specific DBS modulation remain pivotal for advancing PD DBS therapy.

## 6. Limitations

An important methodological limitation of the current literature is that symptom-specific networks in Parkinson’s disease are predominantly inferred from resting-state fMRI, which captures correlational relationships rather than causal interactions. Furthermore, because many network studies rely on ICA or graph-theoretical metrics, our edge-based synthesis (requiring anatomically defined node-to-node connections) may under-represent data-driven whole-brain network components and topological reorganization, thereby affecting the completeness of symptom-specific network mapping. By contrast, DBS-modulated networks are commonly derived by combining diffusion-based tractography with functional imaging, where DTI delineates structural constraints between brain regions and fMRI reflects stimulation-associated network patterns. While these approaches provide valuable anatomical and network-level insights, they do not permit direct inference of causal or dynamic network interactions.

Regarding DBS connectomics, findings may vary across studies due to methodological differences in electrode localization and VTA modeling assumptions, which can limit comparability across studies. Additional heterogeneity may arise from DBS target (STN vs GPi), medication state, and stimulation parameters; although our included DBS studies predominantly reflect chronic postoperative imaging, variability in study protocols may still affect reported connectivity patterns. In addition, tract and network inferences can be influenced by atlas or parcellation choices, which may affect the definition of nodes and pathways across studies. Finally, patient heterogeneity (e.g., disease stage, symptom profile, and individual anatomy or connectivity) may confound group-level inferences. Consequently, associations between network alterations and symptom improvement should be interpreted with caution.

## 7. Future Implications

DBS remains the most effective surgical treatment for advanced stages of PD. Although it may be accompanied by side effects, its efficacy has been validated through decades of clinical practice and research. With the growing understanding of PD, accumulating evidence from brain network studies suggests that motor improvements are closely linked to the modulation of symptom-specific network abnormalities.

Thus, tailoring DBS strategies to individual patients, by selecting the most appropriate targets and optimizing stimulation parameters to precisely modulate symptom-specific networks, represents a promising direction for enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. In recent years, technological advances such as directional DBS and adaptive DBS have enabled more precise modulation of one or multiple symptom-specific networks. However, this also imposes a substantial burden on clinicians in terms of surgical planning and postoperative programming. The investigation of symptom-specific DBS-modulated networks is therefore essential to future progress. Importantly, these efforts should not be limited to the classical STN and GPi targets; novel targets, such as the pedunculopontine nucleus and cerebellar regions, also warrant further exploration. Yet, current research in these areas remains limited. Emerging evidence consistently implicates cerebellar circuits in tremor network and the PPN within locomotor network relevant to PIGD and FoG. However, both targets still lack large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies to rigorously validate their clinical efficacy and safety. Moreover, recent work suggests that optimal improvement of different motor symptoms may correspond to distinct white-matter connectivity patterns [80]. Nevertheless, how stimulation of these symptom-relevant pathways translates into symptom-specific functional network dynamics remains insufficiently characterized.

The convergence of innovative DBS technologies with advancing knowledge of symptom-specific brain networks is expected to drive significant breakthroughs in PD therapy, while simultaneously presenting new challenges for clinical and research scientist.

## 8. Conclusions

In this review, we discussed the symptom-specific brain network abnormalities in PD and the DBS-modulated network. The major symptoms of PD not only exhibit distinct network features but also share common alterations in the functional connectivity of the SMN. By stimulating specific targets, DBS modulates large-scale brain networks, particularly the SMN, thereby alleviating motor symptoms of PD. Furthermore, the differential responses of various symptoms to DBS underscore the need for further investigation into symptom-specific DBS-modulated networks.

# Deep neurobehavioral phenotyping uncovers neural fingerprints of locomotor deficits in Parkinson’s disease

## Abstract
Gait deficits present an unresolved therapeutic challenge in Parkinson’s disease. At the behavioral level, symptoms exhibit heterogeneity, including bradykinesia and hypokinesia during cyclical limb movements, and sudden, involuntary interruptions in the gait sequence, known as freezing of gait. The neural activities driving these various deficits remain largely unknown. Here, we investigated the neural correlates of gait sequence interruptions with a deep phenotyping approach. For this, we transformed kinematic trajectories and cortical oscillations into continuous time series of neurobehavioral features. Next, we combined low-dimensional embedding with supervised classification to identify cortical oscillation features that drive gait deficits. In a rodent Parkinson’s disease model, our approach revealed that gait, akinesia, and stationary movements occupy distinct regions in the low-dimensional embedding space. Among the predominant features separating the states, Hjorth complexity and mobility modulated at akinesia onset. Additionally, we validated our findings in two Parkinson’s patients with freezing of gait, where neural features in STN recordings partially reflected the results in rodents. The presented neurobehavioral phenotyping approach is translational and can easily be generalized to the analysis of other complex movement disorders. Together, our results highlight specific neural features as potential biomarkers that may support the development of adaptive closed-loop algorithms for gait therapy in PD. Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Neurology, Neuroscience

## Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by diverse neural and behavioral deficits, ranging from emotional components to movement1,2. Motor impairments include an array of symptoms such as hypo- and bradykinesia, tremor, and Freezing of Gait (FoG). FoG has been formalized as “brief, episodic absence or marked reduction of forward progression of the feet despite the intention to walk” 3–6. Current PD-therapies include dopaminergic medication, eventually combined with deep-brain stimulation (DBS), primarily of the subthalamic nucleus (STN). These interventions are effective at addressing tremor and rigidity but have moderate or no efficacy on gait impairments7,8. The limitations of pharmaceutical and open-loop DBS, led to the exploration of adaptive DBS protocols to provide enhanced treatment effects9–11. Effective stimulation protocols hinge on identifying suitable biomarkers that correlate with symptoms and can be monitored to guide the intervention11,12. Yet, these biomarkers have remained poorly characterized for gait impairments, and the heterogeneous nature of gait symptoms has further hindered discovery.

Beta-band oscillations (13–35 Hz in humans) are a common target for adaptive DBS12 as they have been repeatedly shown to correlate with bradykinesia13,14, rigidity14, and tremor15. Indeed, algorithms locking to beta bursts showed promising results addressing hemibody subscores (bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor) of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS)11,12. Beta phase-locked stimulation16 has been proposed as a further closed-loop stimulation refinement. Moreover, gamma power entrainment with standard open-loop DBS has also been shown to effectively ameliorate a diverse set of motor symptoms (most prominently bradykinesia and dystonia)9. For gait in Parkinson’s patients, first insights have been generated on the correlation of FoG with high beta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling17, high beta cortico-STN coherence18, and abnormal patterns in the theta band19. Moreover, the combination of multiple neural markers into a multi-input model has been proposed as a promising strategy to address complex and heterogeneous symptom manifestations at multiple scales20,21. Yet, a detailed characterization of neural oscillation features that drive gait impairments remains to be achieved.

Here, we addressed the neural correlates of locomotor deficits using deep neurobehavioral phenotyping in the unilateral 6-OHDA rodent model of Parkinson’s disease. This model offers a well-established platform to explore gait impairments and inform the next generation of neuromodulation therapies22. A typical locomotor sequence in rodents consists of gait, which is the cyclical limb movements that propel the animal forward23, and can be preceded, interrupted, or followed by active or inactive behaviors24. Active motor behaviors include grooming, sniffing, and scanning the environment - here collectively referred to as stationary movement. Inactive motor behaviors include freezing, a state of akinesia that can occur as an innate response to acute stressors25, or as a pathological symptom in the Parkinsonian condition3. In this study, we used deep neurobehavioral phenotyping to selectively analyze the neural correlates of distinct movement states in the locomotor sequence.

We computed neurobehavioral feature vectors that segment the locomotion sequence into discrete time windows. Next, we used machine-learning models to uncover the salient features that best characterize deficits in the locomotor sequences. With this strategy, we identified Hjorth complexity (deviation from a sinusoid) and mobility (frequency variance)26 as novel parameters that correlate with the onset of akinetic episodes. These parameters are computationally efficient and low-latency, making them attractive target candidates for closed-loop neuromodulation. Finally, we underscored the potential utility of the identified neural features in rats by confirming the same pattern of modulation in STN neural recording from a Parkinson’s patient with FoG episodes.

## Results

### Basic processing of locomotor deficits confirms established neural biomarkers in 6-OHDA rats

To analyze neurobehavioral correlates of motor deficits, we employed neurokin, a self-developed toolbox (https://neurokin.readthedocs.io) to integrate multimodal data. We worked with the unilateral 6-OHDA model as a standard for locomotor deficits22. The left Medial Forebrain Bundle (MFB) was injected with 8 μg of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) in N = 6 Sprague Dawley rats, resulting in the degeneration of dopaminergic innervation (Fig. 1a). We quantified the degeneration through tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) staining and signal intensity measurements in striatal brain sections (Fig. 1b), the neurotoxin led to a signal intensity loss of >85% (mean TH signal, left: 4.78 ± 0.51 A.U., right: 30.41 ± 4.22 A.U., P < 0.001) (Fig. 1c). Sham animals (N = 8) underwent the same procedure, but were injected with saline. After manifestation of deficits, rats were allowed to behave freely on the runway. To investigate the neurobehavioral correlates, we collected primary motor cortex (M1) ElectroCorticoGraphy (ECoG) signals (at a sampling rate of 24 kHz) and video-recorded the experiments (at a 50 Hz frame rate). From the videos, we manually labeled the three locomotor states: (i) gait, (ii) stationary movement, and (iii) akinesia—thus establishing ground truth labels (Fig. 1d). A representative labeling of a runway trial in a 6-OHDA lesioned rat is displayed in Supplementary Video 1.

Next, we used neurokin to process manual labels and synchronize the events to the neural data (Fig. 1e). We then built a pipeline to compute the percentage of time spent in each state. The data showed that rats injected with 6-OHDA spent more time in akinetic states (P < 0.01, t-test with Bonferroni–Holm correction) and less time in gait than sham rats (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1f). Next, we calculated the corresponding power spectral density (PSD) between 5 and 100 Hz from M1 ECoG signals in the 6-OHDA model animals (Fig. 1g). We extracted the mean value of the frequency range 20–42 Hz, corresponding to the high beta–low gamma band (HB–LG), which has been established as the pathological neural correlate in hemiparkinsonian rats27. We modeled the group comparison using a linear mixed model, setting the event type as the fixed effect and the day of recording nested within the subject variable as the random effect. We identified a distinct modulation of the HB-LG band across the locomotor states, highlighting an increased activity specifically during states of akinesia, compared to stationary movement (P < 0.001) and gait (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1h).

These results confirmed that 6-OHDA rats exhibited a prolonged period of akinesia compared to sham28, and that HB–LG band activity correlates with motor symptoms29. The presence of the akinetic state in the PD rats, together with the increase in HB–LG band power during akinetic episodes, indicated the pathogenic characteristics of this state.

### Linear deep phenotyping analysis expands neurokinematic features, separating locomotor states

To expand our understanding of the neural correlates of locomotor deficits, we created a multimodal dataset for deep neurobehavioral phenotyping. We synchronized 36 kinematic features (from 3D coordinates recorded at 200 Hz) and 108 neural features sampled at 200 Hz (from neural data sampled at 24 kHz, please refer to Supplementary Table 1 for the full list of features used). The identical 5 ms interval was examined from both a kinematic and a neural perspective, creating a vector of features that is shifting in time. We used neurokin to handle the extraction of kinematic features from 3D hindlimb coordinates and synchronize neural features from ECoG data (extracted using py_neuromodulation30) (Fig. 2a).

After the extraction of the neurobehavioral feature vectors, we set out to investigate specific features separating locomotor states. We created two linear feature spaces (i) utilizing the kinematic portion and (ii) utilizing the neural portion of the multimodal dataset. In the kinematic space, the model could separate the gait state, but confounded the stationary movement with the akinetic state (Fig. 2b). To understand which features contributed the most to the state separation, we inspected the model with a permutation feature importance test. We repeated the test in a cross-validation design, leading to the identification of the top 5 features (speed of: right shoulder, left shoulder, right crest, right hip, and right knee) (Fig. 2c). We visually inspected the directionality of these features by plotting the corresponding scaling on the LDA space. This confirmed that high values in speed-related variables intuitively correlated with the direction of separation of the gait state (Fig. 2d), thus validating our pipeline. Finally, we quantified the performance of the model in predicting the state values of unseen data, within a cross-validation design. We then computed the f1-score (a metric combining precision and recall), which returned values of f1: 0.69, precision: 0.79, recall: 0.62, and calculated the average confusion matrix (Fig. 2e). Because the kinematic model fails to separate akinesia from stationary movement, we grouped the two categories (Supplementary Fig. 1a displays the expanded 3 × 3 confusion matrix). In line with the projection, the LDA model could separate akinesia and stationary movement (97% ± 3% true positive rate) from the gait state (62% ± 14% true positive rate).

Conversely, the LDA model based on neural data only improved the separation of akinesia from stationary movement and gait, but confounded gait and stationary movement. (Fig. 2f). We computed the most relevant neural features for state separation (Fig. 2g), and identified Hjorth complexity and mobility26 as relevant features, while also confirming modulation of HB–LG and gamma oscillations. Projecting the loadings in the LDA space (Fig. 2h), suggested that Hjorth complexity in the left hemisphere (6-OHDA injected) correlated with akinetic episodes, while Hjorth mobility in the right hemisphere correlated with movement. Finally, we quantified the performance of the model in predicting the state of unseen data (f1-score: 0.64, precision: 0.50, recall: 0.72), and computed the average confusion matrix (Fig. 2i). Here, we grouped the categories of stationary movement and gait as our focus was the neural distinction between the akinetic state versus active states (Supplementary Fig. 1b displays the expanded 3 × 3 confusion matrix). Indeed, the model returned a complementary result to the kinematic model: the akinetic state was distinguished above chance level (56% ± 11%) from the active states of gait and stationary movement (82% ± 6%).

### Deep phenotyping with neural networks refines locomotor state characterizations

To account for possible limitations of linear models, such as LDA, for non-linear data31, we additionally performed a non-linear analysis to find the neural features that best discriminate locomotor states. We used the same multimodal dataset and the previously annotated locomotor state labels as ground truth. We employed the toolbox CEBRA32, a deep neural network trained with contrastive learning. We computed the low-dimensional embeddings using supervised training for the selection of the contrastive learning sampling and then trained a K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier to estimate the performance of the model.

We first evaluated the kinematic portion of the features dataset. We projected the kinematic data in a low-dimensional embedding to visualize the distribution (Fig. 3a). Next, we performed the same non-linear analysis, using only the neural portion of the multimodal dataset. After supervised training, we transformed the data onto a 3D embedding (Fig. 3e), which visually indicated a better separation of the states’ clusters than achieved by the linear analysis with LDA (Fig. 2f). To confirm the functional validity of the embedding, we trained a CEBRA model with shuffled labels, and retrieved a collapsed manifold (Supplementary Fig. 7).

### Identified neural features modulate during akinetic state

Next, we set out to characterize the identified neural features relevant for the separation of locomotor states and their relationship to akinetic episodes. Plotting each feature as a function of its importance score for the linear and deep-learning models, allowed us to identify consistently high-scoring features: Hjorth complexity (affected left hemisphere), gamma amplitude (non-affected right hemisphere), Hjorth mobility (right hemisphere), and HB–LG amplitude (left hemisphere) (Fig. 4a). Hjorth complexity and mobility reflect, respectively, the deviation from a pure sine wave, and the average frequency content26 as graphically represented in Fig. 4b. Plotting the average temporal modulation of these four features around the onset of (N = 75) episodes of akinesia revealed common patterns. Specifically, Hjorth complexity underwent a gradual increase shortly before the beginning of the akinetic episode, and again at the onset (Fig. 4c). Conversely, Hjorth mobility displayed a gradual decline preceding and during the akinetic episode onset (Fig. 4d). The HB–LG band showed a similar modulation profile as Hjorth complexity in the affected hemisphere, whereas gamma showed a similar modulation as Hjorth mobility in the healthy hemisphere. We then explored the group-level modulation of these features across locomotor states. We calculated significance with a related t-test (α < 0.05), adjusting for multiple comparisons using a Bonferroni–Holm correction (12 tests). Hjorth Complexity was significantly up-modulated specifically during states of akinesia, whereas gamma amplitude demonstrated a down-modulation during episodes of akinesia, in comparison to the gait state (Fig. 4e). Finally, we qualitatively inspected the ability of the HB–LG amplitude or Hjorth complexity to segregate the locomotor states. We computed two embeddings based only on one of the two neural features. Both the data projection in the embedding and the f1-scores (calculated in a cross-validation design) show comparable separation, with a slight advantage of the Hjorth complexity over the HB–LG band-based embedding (Fig. 4f). Together, our results highlight Hjorth complexity and mobility as possible real-time markers for state and state transition detection.

### Modulation of identified neural correlates is confirmed during freezing of gait in a Parkinson’s patient

Finally, we investigated whether the identified features (Hjorth complexity and mobility, beta (13–20 Hz) and gamma amplitude) could translate to the characterization of locomotor deficits in Parkinson’s patients. For this, we analyzed kinematic and bilateral STN data synchronized with manual gait and freezing of gait (FoG) labels from video recordings. The patients were instructed to walk in a straight line for 10 m, turn and walk back (Fig. 5a). An exemplary episode of FoG is depicted in (Fig. 5b). We binned the data in 1 s bins and selected lower-body markers: lateral condyles, shank, fifth metatarsal bone, and toe. Using neurokin, we computed a set of 292 kinematic features from the following categories: linear velocity, linear speed, linear acceleration, tangential acceleration, angle, angle velocity, angle acceleration, toe height, and toe forward movement. Next, we performed a PCA to quantitatively assess the distinct kinematic features of FoG to normal gait (Fig. 5c). Picking the top 10 correlated features returned a selection of minimum acceleration features which negatively correlated with gait, and maximum velocity or speed which positively correlated with the gait state (Fig. 5d). We then analyzed the STN neural data, segmenting episodes of gait and FoG. To assess clinical translation of the neural features identified in the 6-OHDA model, we computed the amplitude of Hjorth complexity, Hjorth mobility, beta band, and gamma band. We analyzed a total of 6 STN channels (3 left hemisphere, 3 right hemisphere) and for each participant displayed an exemplary channel, which showed the maximum beta modulation during FoG. While one subject did not display any neural modulation, the second subject showed the same pattern of modulation of Hjorth complexity and mobility during FoG, as observed for M1 ECoG during akinesia in our rodent model (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 4). Specifically, Hjorth complexity was up-modulated (P = 0.004), Hjorth mobility was downregulated (P = 0.004) the beta band was up-modulated (P = 0.006), following the same modulation as for 6-OHDA rats. However in contrast to rats, the gamma band was up-modulated (P = 0.004). We used a within-patient, trial-constrained permutation test, with Bonferroni correction for 4 tests. Finally, we confirmed that the modulation in subject 2 was not due to a different neural signature of turns, during which the majority of FOG episodes happened (Supplementary Fig. 6). These observations establish the capability of deep neurobehavioral phenotyping to identify features for locomotor states separation that translate to clinical experimental settings.

## Discussion

In this study, we set out to discover novel neural fingerprints of akinetic states by analyzing kinematic and ECoG multimodal data from a Parkinson’s rodent model. We created a neurobehavioral dataset, capturing both the kinematic and neural aspects of locomotor states. To identify critical neural features characterizing akinetic states, we trained a linear model and a neural network on the neural and kinematic portions of the dataset. Specifically, our results linked Hjorth complexity and Hjorth mobility to akinetic episodes. Further, we confirmed the same pattern of modulation in one of two Parkinson’s patients, indicating a potential direction for future translational studies. We propose these novel neural fingerprints as potential biomarkers of akinetic states, which could be further explored as targets for precision interventions such as closed-loop stimulation.

Designing algorithms to detect FoG is an ongoing effort, even inspiring international machine learning challenges33. Previous literature focused on classifying FoG computing features based on the gait cycles34–36, which is well-suited for classifying gait abnormalities but entails intrinsic delays, as detection can only occur after the completion of a step. Moreover, gait-cycle-based approaches cannot easily distinguish akinesia from voluntary resting states, as both lack stepping events. To allow features calculation for all motor states, we employed a rolling time-window approach37,38, in line with recently developed motion sequencing algorithms39. This enabled continuous monitoring of locomotor states, including completely akinetic episodes. Moreover, leveraging a neurobehavioral dataset, presented two different lenses (neural and kinematic) on the same phenomena.

Parkinson’s disease is characterized by a heterogeneous symptom distribution40–42. Historically, the most explored feature has been the beta band, which has shown robust correlation with bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor13–15. However, a single feature might not account for the complexity of different symptoms distribution43. To address this, we computed a broad set of neural and kinematic features and employed a data-driven approach to select emerging salient features characterizing pathological locomotor states. We first employed the linear dimensionality-reduction algorithm LDA, which is commonly used in neuroscience and is a reference algorithm for both offline and online decoding setups. Then, we compared the results to the deep-learning model CEBRA, which accounts for temporal context and non-linearities in the data. We scored high-performing features and retrieved known correlates of akinesia and bradykinesia, such as beta band amplitude44,45, as well as known correlates of prokinetic activity, such as gamma band amplitude46, thereby corroborating the validity of our method. Interestingly, less explored but promising47 features, such as Hjorth complexity and mobility, were also significantly modulated. To probe the translational value of our findings, we also explored a small human dataset. While one subject did not show any pattern of modulation (neither in the Hjorth parameters, nor in the hallmark beta band), the second highlighted a modulation pattern consistent with our findings. The subject who did not display modulation has a suboptimal localization of the right electrode, which results in a position lateral to the STN (Supplementary Fig. 8). The right hemisphere was also the most affected, corresponding to the left hemibody being more impacted. Although this suboptimal positioning still provided acceptable clinical benefits, as reflected in the UPDRS improvement, it may have led to less optimal electrophysiological recordings. This is also reflected in the postoperative medication adjustments, where subject 2 achieved a superior reduction. Together, these results support our pipeline as a data-driven approach for identifying salient neural features, revealing both established and novel markers of pathological locomotor states across species.

Hjorth parameters were first established to quantify EEG traces, and they provide a bridge between the time domain (the signal change over time) and the conventionally analyzed frequency domain (the signal composition over frequencies)26. Since then, Hjorth parameters have been explored as biomarkers to classify Alzheimer’s disease patients48,49, classify Parkinson’s disease patients50, and to predict tremor in Parkinson’s patients51. Here, we established that Hjorth parameters correlated with a pathological locomotor state and uncovered the specific modulation of the complexity and mobility parameters at the onset of akinesia, both in a PD rodent model and in a patient. Time-domain biomarkers like Hjorth parameters are appealing for real-time closed-loop stimulation because they bypass the added computational cost of frequency-domain analyses and the latency inherent to causal filters14.

Freezing of gait (FoG) in Parkinson’s disease remains a poorly understood phenomenon52,53. Whether the proposed mechanisms of FoG in humans translate to rodent models requires consideration at multiple levels, including neuroanatomy, motor behavior, and network activity. Several circuit pathologies are hypothesized to play a role in the manifestation of FoG, including distortions in locomotor networks (i.e., cortex-basal ganglia loops and brainstem MLR), their cognitive regulation (i.e., frontal cortex and visual cortex), and their emotional control (i.e., amygdala)52–56. Rodent models of PD typically rely on dopaminergic depletions in the nigrostriatal pathway22. The resulting circuit deficits in rodents do reflect the progressive dopaminergic degeneration in patients that correlates with the occurrence of FoG57. The neuroanatomical comparability of our study is therefore established predominantly at the level of dopaminergic deficiencies acting on cortex-basal ganglia loops. At the behavioral level, there are four major motor expressions of FoG in patients, namely: leg trembling, shuffling, festination, and akinesia3. In our rodent experiments, we only observed akinesia as a distortion to the natural gait sequence on the runway. These observations are consistent with an in-depth literature review that found no clear evidence for gait interruption patterns in rodent models of PD other than akinesia22. There has been one report in the non-human primate MPTP model, where leg trembling occurred in the hindlimbs during quadrupedal locomotion58. This observation indicates that other movement patterns - resembling human FoG - can occur during quadrupedal gait. As a conservative interpretation of our study, we conclude that rodent models of PD and human patients are comparable in the sense that they spend excessive amounts of time in pathological motor states during gait execution (i.e., akinesia in rodents and multiple patterns of FoG in patients). Within these pathological motor states, we observed several similarities in the network oscillations of the cortex in rodents and the STN in one human patient. Namely, beta oscillations, as well as Hjorth complexity and mobility, modulated in the same direction in rodents and one Parkinson's patient during pathological gait states. For beta oscillations, these findings have already been substantiated by fitting clinical evidence in a group of 18 Parkinson's patients59. Yet, future research will need to address the reproducibility of the modulation of Hjorth parameter modulations during gait in larger patient cohorts. Establishing translationally robust neural biomarkers will be pivotal when developing next-generation neuromodulation technologies.

The ideal therapy should be tailored to the specific subset of symptoms that a patient displays, maximizing the benefits while minimizing adverse effects. Thus, finding a range of computationally lightweight features, that can be combined in a multi-input closed-loop control21 and can reliably detect a pathological state will be crucial to develop personalized stimulation strategies to mitigate, or avoid altogether a pathological motor state. A feature vector that allows to identify a pathological state, can be conceived as discovering its fingerprint, which could eventually be targeted with a timely neuromodulation. Recent examples of model-based control are offered in the field of Spinal Cord Injury rehabilitation, where a context-aware algorithm could significantly improve locomotor performance60,61. The practical need of a state-based stimulation is illustrated, for example, by the impairment of movement stopping caused by a state-naive open-loop STN stimulation to address Parkinson’s symptoms62. Ultimately, we envision a closed-loop algorithm based on real-time mapping of the neural state projected to a patient-specific embedding. This tailored neural projection would reveal the transitions into pathological states and guide targeted interventions to relieve or avoid pathological manifestations. As a proof of concept, we tested the monitoring abilities of our neural embedding in an offline implementation. Supplementary Video 2 illustrates a successful neural state tracking during a runway trial.

The analysis presented in this study was focused on three locomotor behaviors (akinesia, stationary movement, and gait) that were clearly recognizable during the runway task. It is notable that both the linear and non-linear kinematic models could not perfectly separate the akinetic state from the stationary movement. However, this is explainable as no markers were placed on the forelimb or on the head of the animals, which often remained the only body parts active during stationary movement. So, while the akinetic state was apparent from the video recording (used to create the ground-truth labels), it was challenging to distinguish from the available 3D coordinates. Further analyses of this Parkinson’s model could include other behavioral states, and potentially lead to the uncovering of additional neural correlates of the disease. Nevertheless, the pipeline established here could serve as a template to explore other states, as well as the effects of treatments (e.g., pharmaceuticals, electro-, or optostimulation) on neural correlates. The modulation of Hjorth parameters was reproduced in a single patient, although consistent and promising, more data needs to be collected.

In conclusion, this study employed a data-driven approach to uncover unexplored neural correlates of pathological locomotor states in Parkinson’s disease across species. In the future, these biomarkers may prove to be novel targets for a closed-loop stimulation that adapts in real-time to the current locomotor state, providing an on-demand delivery method.

## Methods

### Neurokin’s modular and open-source design

To conduct tasks of synchronization, data parsing, and kinematic feature extraction, we made use of the self-developed package neurokin, which streamlines the integration of three distinct types of data: neural local field potentials, kinematic recordings, and event labels. The package is primarily object-oriented to ensure a coherent interface across data types. The underlying principle of neurokin is modularity, both enabling the use of any of the classes independently (to facilitate data import and management) and supporting the assembling of project-specific pipelines.

We sought to adhere to the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (FAIR) principles63. Specifically, neurokin is openly available on GitHub (neurokin) and pip installable (pip install neurokin) ("F” principle), it includes independent documentation (neurokin documentation) ("A” principle), it uses standard domain-relevant file types (e.g., .c3d or .csv) ("I” principle), it builds upon qualified software (e.g., numpy, pandas) and details a permissive MIT License ("R” principle). Building on these principles, we encourage community-wide code review and participation for further development.

#### KinematicDataRun

This class was used for importing and processing kinematic data. It handles 2D and 3D kinematic data originating from optoelectrical gait systems such as Vicon (.c3d file format), or files from computer vision toolboxes (e.g., DeepLabCut). Upon importing the data into the corresponding kinematic object, the user can make use of the kinematic-features extraction pipeline. This pipeline was designed to maximize flexibility. To this end, the user can define a custom skeleton via a configuration file and select standard features (e.g., speed, acceleration, height). Additionally, the framework supports the straightforward addition of custom features.

#### NeuralData

This class operates on neural recordings, such as ECoG and local field potentials (LFPs), standardizing data from different recording systems in a common format to interface with fundamental methods (e.g., timestamps-parsing, PSD calculation). It is currently compatible with the Tucker-Davis Technologies apparatus and OpenEphys data, with the possibility of being expanded to other hardware. It was used for importing and processing the collected neural data from rats.

#### NeuralCorrelatesStates

We used this class to tailor a pipeline to retrieve Vicon labels and align them with kinematic and neural features. This can easily be expanded to support any time-stamped, user-defined events. Users can define the events by providing labels. These are used to retrieve corresponding kinematic and neural data, and can eventually be used as inputs for machine learning applications.

### Animal model and surgical procedures

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the German regulatory body (LaGeSo), specifically under the animal licence number G0206/19. Experiments were performed on male Sprague Dawley rats with initial weights of 200–250 g, ordered from Janvier Labs. Animals were kept in a 12-h light cycle with ad libitum access to food and water.

All the interventions were performed under full general anesthesia with isoflurane (1–2%) in oxygen-enriched air. After surgery, the rats were placed in an incubator for optimized recovery from anesthesia as described in ref. 60.

PD models were injected with 6-OHDA (Sigma: H4381) dissolved immediately before use in a buffer solution of 0.02% w/v ascorbic acid, achieving a final concentration of 8 mg/mL. Of the 6-OHDA solution, 1.2 μL was injected at a rate of 0.04 mL/min through a pipette according to McNamara et al. 16. Sham animals were injected with the same solution except for 6-OHDA. The injection was targeted to the Medial Forebrain Bundle (coordinates from bregma: AP: −2.6 mm, ML: 1.6 mm, DV: −8.4 mm from skull surface). The success of the 6-OHDA model was verified with the antibodies anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (abcam: ab112) and anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (Thermofisher: A-11034).

Following a recovery period of 2 weeks, the rats were implanted with 2 stainless steel ECoG screws (2 mm) over the motor cortex (left and right hemisphere), one ground screw over the cerebellum, and 2 LFP probes targeted to the STN area (left and right hemisphere). No data from the LFP electrodes were analyzed in this study.

To guarantee the protection of the implant, we designed a three-component cap that can be 3D-printed and assembled during surgery. The 3D-printing files are openly available (headstage caps).

### Runway task

We trained the rats to run from one end of a 15 × 140 cm runway to the other. We placed a cage with at least one additional rat at the end of the runway to further motivate the tested rat. At the beginning of each recording, the rat was placed at the starting end of the runway, then given up to a minute to spontaneously initiate walking. If, by the end of the given time, the rat had not run the whole length, it was gently brought to the ending side for a few seconds while the recording setup was prepared for the next recording. This ensured exposure to the additional rats and minimized idle time at the starting point. We repeated the task until 15 successful crossings were achieved, or the total recording time per animal reached 20 min. To ensure consistency across sessions, we marked with a permanent marker the joint points (shoulder, iliac crest, hip, knee, ankle, and metatarsophalangeal joint) on the skin. Then, at the start of each session, 4 mm reflective markers were glued with double-sided tape on the marked spots.

### Kinematic data recording

To capture the movements of the rats, we used an infrared camera-based motion capture system (Vicon). We equipped a 2.5 m2 room with 10 infrared and 2 HD wall-mounted cameras. The infrared cameras were positioned around three sides of the runway, covering different heights. Data from the infrared cameras was recorded at a sampling rate of 200 Hz, and videos from the HD cameras were recorded at 50 Hz. The raw data were then processed using the Nexus software (v2.12.1). Each recording was cropped from the earliest point where all the markers were visible, and no contact between the experimenter and the animal was observed, to the last point where the markers were visible, and no contact with the experimenter was observed, or to the end of the run. The 3D position of each joint was manually reconstructed using a custom-defined bipedal skeleton (as no markers were placed on the forepaws). The data was then exported in the .c3d format.

### Neural data recording

To record ECoG neural signals, we used stainless steel screws (DIN 84 A2 M1.2 × 2 from MaxWitte GmbH), laser-soldered to a 10T stainless steel PFA-coated wire (SS-10T/A from Science Products). The wires, soldered to Omnetics 32-channel connectors, were coated with Epoxy resin to grant stability, and all the unused channels were shorted to the ground. During experiments, we plugged a digitizing headstage (Intan) to minimize movement artifact and used a TDT recording apparatus. The signal pipeline was then controlled through the software Synapse. We recorded all raw data at a sampling frequency of 24,414.1 Hz; data from implanted LFP electrodes were not analyzed.

### Locomotor states labeling

We defined akinesia on a phenotypic level, observing an almost complete lack of any type of movement. This was to be contrasted with stationary movement, where the rat was in an engaged state and performing an array of behaviors (e.g., grooming or sniffing) and gait, when the rat was walking. The events were manually labeled in the Nexus software. The data was then exported in the ASCII format to a .csv file.

### Locomotor states distribution analysis

We used neurokin to process the .csv files generated by the Nexus software, and created a dataset summarizing all the event timestamps in each run. For each run, we computed the percentage of time spent in each locomotor state. Next, we computed subject-level averages and quantified the differences in locomotor state distribution between the sham control and the PD animals. We performed an independent t-test with a Bonferroni–Holm correction for multiple comparisons (three comparisons). We assumed an α < 0.05.

### Kinematic features dataset creation

To create the neural and kinematic dataset, we relied on a consistent folder structure that was then automatically processed. We used neurokin to import and process the .c3d and .csv files generated by Vicon. We selected kinematic features representing speed, acceleration, angle variation for the various markers, forward movement, and height for the metatarsophalangeal joint. We based our feature extraction on a sliding-window strategy. The kinematic features were computed at a 200 Hz rate (i.e., 5 ms window with a 2.5 ms overlap with the following window). This corresponds to a sliding window of 2 frames, with 1 frame of overlap, allowing for the highest precision of feature computation.

### Neural features dataset creation

Using neurokin, we selected the neural data corresponding to the valid portion of the recording (all markers visible, no contact with the experimenter). To extract neural features, we relied on the package py_neuromodulation30, and used a feature sampling rate of 200 Hz, thus aligning with the kinematic features framerate. We computed 108 parameters, among which were temporal features (e.g., Hjorth parameters), oscillatory features (e.g., power of the beta band and gamma band), and waveform shapes (e.g., sharp wave intervals). On rare occasions, we encountered a length mismatch between the kinematic and the neural files. Upon reviewing the original files, we discovered that occasionally a few additional frames were recorded at the beginning of the kinematic file; we discarded files with excessive length mismatch and trimmed those with minimal mismatch (typically 1–2 frames).

### Multimodal dataset creation and testing parameters

For each feature, we filtered for outliers (set to the median value) and normalized by Z-scoring. Finally, we concatenated all runs in a multimodal (kinematics, neural, and labels) dataset. A schematic flowchart of the data processing is displayed in (Supplementary Fig. 9). To validate the models, we performed an 11-fold cross-validation with a Group-K-Fold approach—as 55 runs were present, at each iteration we trained on 50 runs and tested on 5 unseen ones. We used random seeds to ensure all analyses were done on the same splits.

### LDA analysis

The visualization of the entire model was achieved by training and transforming the whole dataset and computing kernel density estimation marginal distributions. Importance scores were computed for each feature, for each fold of the cross-validation, using the sklearn package64, specifically calculating the balanced accuracy. The final confusion matrix was calculated as the average of the 11 folds.

### CEBRA analysis

We used a CEBRA model32, based on contrastive learning and a generalized InfoNCE loss function, with the following parameters: model_architecture = “offset200-model”, batch_size = 512, temperature = 0.1 (value set after temperature optimization scan), learning_rate = 0.0005, max_iterations = 50,000, time_offsets = 200, output_dimension = 3, conditional = “time_delta”. This allowed us to use the state labels as the categorical variable used to optimize the contrastive learning steps. The “offset200-model" was custom-made to have a receptive field of 200 samples, corresponding to 1 s of data, 6 convolutional layers with GELU activation functions, and input and output dimensions corresponding to the number of features (36 for the kinematic model and 108 for the neural model) and the number of output dimensions (3), respectively. We confirmed a progressive decay in the loss-function, which plateaued before the end of the max_iteration number (Supplementary Fig. 5). To evaluate the performance of the model, we used a KNN decoder with n_neighbors = 3 and metric = “cosine", on test data projected in the trained embedding. Importance scores were computed for each feature, for each fold of the cross-validation, using the f1_score calculation from the sklearn package. The final confusion matrix was calculated as the average of the 11 folds.

### Statistical analysis of selected features alignment

The significance of each selected feature was computed as a related t-test between the compared states. To correct for multiple comparisons, we used a Bonferroni–Holm correction, ranking 12 t-test (3 states, 4 features). We assumed an α < 0.05.

### Human data collection

We analyzed the data of two male Parkinsonian patients (age: 68 and 70 years) implanted bilaterally in the subthalamic nucleus (STN-DBS) with Sensight B33005 electrodes and the Percept PC implantable pulse generators (Medtronic, PLC, USA). Subject 1 had a suboptimal localization of the right electrode, which resulted in a position lateral to the STN. In the axial 2D slice, the deepest contact of the right electrode lies laterally to the STN. The 3D reconstruction shows that all contacts lie laterally. The electrodes of Subject 2 are accurately placed within the STN (Supplementary Fig. 8). Subject 1 achieved a 78% reduction in the Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) one year after implantation (from 2050 to 452.5), Subject 2 achieved an 87% reduction (from 1113.4 to 150). Subject 1’s right hemisphere was the most affected, corresponding to the left hemibody being most affected clinically. Subject 2’s left hemisphere was the most affected, corresponding to right hemibody dominance in symptoms. Subject 1 received a 4 on the Hoehn and Yahr scale, and a 25 on the Montréal Cognitive Assessment test; Subject 2 scored 3 and 26, respectively. Patients showed gait disturbances and freezing of gait in the meds-off/stim-off condition (i.e., after overnight suspension of all dopaminergic drugs and after switching off the stimulation for at least one hour), remarkably improved by both medication and stimulation (Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale, UPDRS-III65), gait item: (i) meds-off/stim-off: 50 and 78 and (ii) meds-off/stim-on: 31 and 45.

Data were collected in the context of the project 24778381 within the Collaborative Research Centre TRR 295 ReTune (Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft [DFG]), which received ethical approval from the institutional review board at Würzburg University (no. 103/20-am) on 22.03.2021. The project conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from the participants at recruitment.

Patients performed the experiment in a meds-off/stim-off condition. They were instructed to walk barefoot back and forth for 10 times along a walkway of about 10 m, at their preferred speed. Kinematic data were recorded using a six-camera optoelectronic system (SMART DX-400, BTS Bioengineering, Italy) with a sampling rate of 100 Hz and 29 markers placed on anatomical landmarks according to a published full-body protocol. An in-built visible-light camera (VIXTA, BTS Bioengineering, Italy) allowed for automatically synchronized videos of the experiment. LFPs were collected bilaterally and synchronously from all non-adjacent contact pairs (i.e., [0–2], [1–3], [0–3], with 0 being the lowermost and 3 the uppermost contact, recorded with the indefinite streaming modality) with a sampling frequency of 250 Hz. Cortical activity was recorded with a 64-channel portable EEG device (Sessantaquattro, OTBioelettronica, Italy), which was used just for synchronization purposes. The kinematic and cortical recordings were synchronized using a transistor-transistor logic (TTL) reference signal, while cortical and LFP recordings were synchronized by means of an electrical artifact produced by a transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), as described in detail elsewhere. In this way, we could align kinematic data and labels from videos with the LFP time series.

Videos were inspected independently by two clinicians with experience in movement disorders. FOG episodes were recognized when patients either failed to start walking within one second after being instructed to do so, or showed visible signs of attempting, but being unable, to continue walking. Additionally, any sudden and noticeable interruption in walking without an obvious external cause was also classified as a FOG event4. Beginning and end times of each FOG episode were marked independently, and any discrepancies between the two experienced clinicians were resolved through mutual discussion. Subject 1 had 20 freezing episodes, during turning and continuous walking (median duration: 7 s, range 1–33 s). Subject 2 had 8 freezing episodes, mainly during turning (median duration: 3.5 s, range 2–8 s).

### Human data analysis

We processed the 3D kinematic data by parsing the moments of straight walk (as the markers were not detected during the turns) and inverted the direction of the backwards walks (x coordinate) to be able to compute equal features. We selected markers that had at least 70% of the points and interpolated the missing values. We chose a subset of markers in the lower body and computed a set of 292 linear and angular features using neurokin. We filtered for outliers (set median value) and Z-scored column-wise. We split each run into portions of 1 s and computed the mean for each portion. We performed a dimensionality reduction step using PCA and finally color-coded depending on the freezing of gait label (if the portion of data spanned a transition, the color was decided by the majority of the data).

We processed the STN data by selecting the valid part of the data (exclusion of the flanking zero-padding) and computed neural features using py_neuromodulation resampled at 100 Hz. For visualization purposes, we selected the channel that had the highest beta band modulation and was coherent with the trend (see Supplementary Fig. 4 for the complete set). We excluded a short segment of the data (6 s) around a strong STN artifact from session 2 of Subject 2. We parsed the data in episodes of FoG and of gait (Subj. 1—gait episodes: 20, FOG episodes: 19; Subj. 2—gait episodes: 9, FOG episodes: 8). Finally, the mean of each feature per episode was computed.

# Effects of different types of deep brain stimulation on gait disorders in patients with Parkinson’s disease: a network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials

## Abstract
To systematically compare and rank the relative efficacy of different types of deep brain stimulation (DBS) for gait disorders in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), with particular emphasis on three outcomes: motor function (MDS-UPDRS III), Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q), and normal gait velocity (cm/s). The goal is to provide evidence-based guidance for individualized neuromodulation strategies. Following a preregistered protocol (PROSPERO CRD420251074368), a comprehensive literature search was conducted from January 1, 2000, to June 15, 2025, across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CNKI. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) meeting predefined PICOS criteria were included, and continuous outcome data for MDS-UPDRS III, FOG-Q and normal gait velocity in the Off-Dopa state were extracted. A frequentist network meta-analysis framework (R, netmeta package) was employed, with mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) as effect estimates. Heterogeneity was quantified using τ2 and I2 statistics. Global and local inconsistency tests were applied, and treatment ranking was performed using P-scores. Robustness was examined through fixed- vs. random-effects comparisons, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, and, where feasible, meta-regression and subgroup analyses. Twenty-five RCTs were included, involving 324 patients in intervention groups and 324 in control groups. (1) MDS-UPDRS III: Network meta-analysis showed that Low-PPNa-DBS produced the greatest improvement in motor function (MD = −31.20, 95% CI –53.25 to −9.15, p = 0.0055), followed by PPN-DBS (MD = −26.00, 95% CI –38.92 to −13.08, p ≤ 0.0001) and CuN-DBS (MD = −23.00, 95% CI –42.65 to −3.35, p = 0.0218). Additional significant but moderate effects were observed for Posterior-STN-dDBS (MD = −16.55, 95% CI –28.43 to −4.68, p = 0.0063), IL-IL-DBS, 60 Hz-STN-DBS, 80 Hz-STN-DBS, STN-DBS, STN + SNr HF-DBS, and STN + SNr LF-DBS. Overall heterogeneity was moderate to high (τ2 = 12.1151, I2 = 68.7%), and heterogeneity testing indicated significant heterogeneity (Q total = 14.48, df = 5, p = 0.0128). (2) FOG-Q: None of the DBS modalities yielded statistically significant improvements in FOG-Q scores (all 95% CIs crossed zero, p > 0.05). This outcome exhibited high heterogeneity (τ2 = 1.3074, I2 = 88.3%) and significant inconsistency (Q total = 25.61, p < 0.0001), suggesting that current evidence is insufficient to confirm a definitive DBS benefit for freezing of gait. (3) Gait velocity (cm/s): Network analysis demonstrated that spDBS [MD = 18.06, 95% CI (10.42, 25.70), p < 0.0001] and STN-DBS [MD = 17.58, 95% CI (13.20, 21.95), p < 0.0001] provided the most pronounced improvements. Conventional aDBS [MD = 14.80, 95% CI (7.71, 21.89)] and GPi-DBS [MD = 13.91, 95% CI (2.40, 25.42)] were associated with moderate benefits. Heterogeneity for this outcome was moderate to high (τ2 = 12.1151, I2 = 65.5%), indicating persistent between-study variability. This network meta-analysis systematically compared the effects of different DBS modalities on gait disorders in PD. For MDS-UPDRS Part III, Low-PPNa-DBS, PPN-DBS, and CuN-DBS yielded the most pronounced and statistically robust improvements. In contrast, analysis of FOG-Q scores revealed no statistically significant effects for any DBS intervention. Regarding gait speed, spDBS and STN-DBS showed clear and significant enhancements. https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251074368, Identifier: CRD420251074368.

## Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common progressive neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterized by core motor symptoms including bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor (Lees et al., 2009). Among these, gait disorders represent one of the most challenging manifestations to manage in the mid-to-late stages of the disease. These gait disturbances substantially reduce functional mobility and independence and are strongly associated with increased fall risk, progressive disability, and heightened caregiving burden (Bloem et al., 2021; Tolosa et al., 2021).

In Parkinson’s disease, gait disorders are an important component of motor dysfunction. Motor function in PD is typically evaluated through motor signs, mobility and balance performance, and quantitative gait parameters (Yin et al., 2025). Gait disorder in PD is broadly characterized as a multi-dimensional locomotor impairment resulting not only from basal ganglia dysfunction but also from degenerative or dysregulated processes involving extra–basal ganglia structures—including the brainstem, cerebellum, cortical regions, and multimodal sensory integration systems—which collectively compromise the automaticity, rhythmicity, and adaptability of walking (Gao et al., 2020; Jankovic, 2008). Clinically, PD-related gait disorder manifests through phenomena such as shuffling steps, reduced stride length, diminished gait speed, increased step-to-step variability, freezing of gait (FOG), and impaired postural control (Mirelman et al., 2019). From a quantitative standpoint, these manifestations can be operationalized through specific spatiotemporal gait metrics, including stride length, gait speed, cadence, swing and stance phase proportions, step variability indices, and turning performance, each of which reflects distinct pathophysiological substrates of the disease (Guo et al., 2022; Nutt et al., 2011; Johansson et al., 2023).

Although pharmacological therapies—such as levodopa (Turcano et al., 2018), dopamine receptor agonists (Perez-Lloret and Rascol, 2010), amantadine (Inzelberg et al., 2006), and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) inhibitors (Fabbri et al., 2022)—can alleviate symptoms during the early stages, disease progression is typically accompanied by motor fluctuations and dyskinesias (Aradi and Hauser, 2020; Cenci et al., 2022; Rascol et al., 2003). These treatments provide only limited benefit for gait disorders, and some agents may induce addictive behaviors or mood disturbances (Debove et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2016), emphasizing the potential clinical utility of other therapeutic options.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has emerged as a major surgical intervention for managing motor complications in advanced PD. DBS can be tailored to individual symptom profiles and targets distinct nodes within the basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuits that mediate diverse PD manifestations (Hariz and Blomstedt, 2022). The subthalamic nucleus (STN-DBS) (Onder and Comoglu, 2024) and the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi-DBS) (Vitek, 2002) remain the most widely applied targets. In recent years, technological advances such as adaptive DBS (aDBS) (Beudel and Brown, 2016), device-assisted neuromodulation therapies (Rajan et al., 2022), directional electrodes, and personalized stimulation strategies (Schroter et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2021) have been developed, aiming to achieve more precise modulation of abnormal neural activity through closed- or semi-closed-loop systems. However, the efficacy of different DBS targets and stimulation parameters for gait disorders remains controversial (Cury et al., 2022), and evidence regarding invasive interventions for PD is inconsistent (Deuschl et al., 2022), highlighting the need for more systematic comparative evaluations.

Existing studies on STN-DBS and GPi-DBS largely address overall motor symptoms and are limited by small samples, heterogeneous designs, and reliance on pairwise analyses, leaving the comparative effects of different DBS modalities on gait disorders insufficiently defined (Deuschl et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021). Evidence for emerging stimulation paradigms is particularly sparse. Network meta-analysis (NMA) (Hoaglin et al., 2011), by integrating direct and indirect comparisons, enables simultaneous evaluation and ranking of multiple DBS targets and paradigms, thus overcoming the lack of head-to-head trials and providing a more comprehensive framework for gait-specific assessment. Using validated gait-related measures, the present study applies an NMA to compare the efficacy of established and emerging DBS strategies—including DBS-off or sham controls—while examining heterogeneity through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

## Materials and methods

### Study design

This study was conducted as a network meta-analysis aimed at systematically evaluating the effects of different types of deep brain stimulation (DBS) on gait disorders in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). The study population consisted of patients with clinically confirmed PD presenting with gait impairments, and only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, and these studies required clear outcome data. The study was designed and implemented in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Shamseer et al., 2015). Based on the principles of population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study design (PICOS) (Methley et al., 2014), a set of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria was established to determine study eligibility. The protocol was registered on June 15, 2025, in the PROSPERO database (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/; registration ID: CRD420251074368) to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

### Eligibility criteria based on PICOS

#### Population

Included: Adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (PD) according to established diagnostic criteria, presenting clinically relevant gait disturbances that impair mobility. Gait disturbances may manifest as reduced gait speed, shortened step length, increased stride-to-stride variability, freezing of gait (FOG), or other observable walking dysfunctions.

Excluded: Patients with secondary or atypical parkinsonism [e.g., multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)] or those undergoing surgical interventions other than DBS.

#### Intervention

Included: Various types of DBS targeting key brain regions such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN-DBS) and globus pallidus internus (GPi-DBS), as well as advanced modalities including adaptive DBS (aDBS). Stimulation parameters (site, frequency, mode) were analyzed to explore their effects on gait.

Excluded: Non-DBS neuromodulation techniques (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation, spinal cord stimulation) or non-neuromodulatory interventions.

#### Comparator

Included: Other DBS types, sham stimulation, off DBS. Head-to-head comparisons of different DBS targets (e.g., STN vs. GPi, STN vs. aDBS, GPi vs. PPN) were eligible, allowing evaluation of differential effects on gait outcomes.

#### Outcomes

Primary outcomes: Quantitative gait-related measures, including MDS-UPDRS Part III (motor examination), gait speed, stride time variability, and Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q) scores. Only studies reporting clearly defined DBS interventions with these measurable gait outcomes were included. Furthermore, eligible studies were restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including crossover designs, that explicitly reported blinded assessments.

#### Study design & context

Eligible studies were restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including crossover designs, that explicitly reported blinded assessments (Savovic et al., 2012). Studies conducted in hospital-based neurology or neurosurgery departments or movement disorder centers were considered. Observational studies, case reports, narrative reviews, and studies without full-text availability were excluded.

### Literature search strategy

Because early studies may have methodological limitations in sample size estimation and outcome measurement, the search period was set from January 1, 2000, to June 15, 2025, to enhance the accuracy and credibility of this network meta-analysis. Database search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S3. The databases searched included PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CNKI.

Our stringent search and criteria were essential for maintaining internal validity by reducing methodological heterogeneity, though such rigor may inevitably restrict the generalizability of the findings (Turner et al., 2012).

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria

#### Inclusion criteria

(1) Participants: Adult patients (≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of idiopathic PD, regardless of sex, disease duration, or stage, with clinical manifestations of gait impairment (e.g., reduced gait velocity, shortened step length, freezing of gait [FOG], or other gait-related dysfunctions). (2) Intervention: DBS treatment, without restriction on target site or stimulation paradigm. (3) Comparator: Other DBS modalities, DBS-off, or sham stimulation. (4) Study design: RCTs, including parallel-group and crossover designs. Crossover trials were eligible if random allocation to intervention sequences was explicitly described. No restriction was imposed on the duration of the washout period; however, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the potential impact of carryover effects in crossover trials at risk, ensuring the robustness of the results. (5) Outcomes: Studies were required to report at least one extractable quantitative gait-related outcome (gait velocity, FOG-Q score, or MDS-UPDRS III score) along with corresponding effect size and variance estimates (standard error or confidence interval).

#### Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients with secondary or atypical parkinsonism [e.g., multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP)] or patients undergoing surgical interventions other than DBS. Because such procedures may have distinct mechanisms, targets, and clinical effects, which could introduce heterogeneity and confound the evaluation of DBS-specific outcomes. (2) Non-PD populations or mixed cohorts where PD-specific data could not be extracted. (3) Interventions not involving DBS [e.g., pharmacological therapy alone, rehabilitation, or other neuromodulation techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), or spinal cord stimulation (SCS)]. Studies using TMS, tDCS, or other non-invasive neuromodulation techniques were excluded because these interventions differ fundamentally from DBS with respect to invasiveness, target sites, mechanisms of action, and clinical effects. Including such studies could introduce heterogeneity and reduce the specificity of the analysis. (4) Studies not reporting gait-related outcomes. (5) Non-RCT study designs (e.g., observational studies, case series, case reports, reviews, or expert opinions). (6) Incomplete or unextractable data, with no supplementary information available after contacting the authors. (7) Duplicate publications or repeated use of the same cohort (only the study with the largest sample size or highest methodological quality was retained) (8) Animal or in vitro studies.

### Data extraction

Search results from each database were imported into EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, United States) for reference management and duplicate removal. Extracted study characteristics included first author, publication year, intervention type, sample sizes of intervention and control groups, and gait-related outcomes with effect size data. Quality assessment of the included studies was performed using Review Manager 5.4.1 (The Cochrane Collaboration, London, United Kingdom). Two independent reviewers (YG and YH) used customized Excel worksheets to extract data, including author, country, demographic characteristics, and stimulation type. Extraction consistency was assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) across variables (Stoll et al., 2019); the initial overall mean r was 0.81, indicating high reliability. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer (ZZ) when necessary. Studies for which the requisite outcome data could not be extracted, and for which relevant information could not be obtained from the original authors despite attempts to contact them, were excluded from the analysis. If unsuccessful, data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer v4.7.1 Final analyses were conducted using the effect-size data extracted or derived from each study, including outcome means, measures of variability (e.g., standard deviations or standard errors), and sample sizes, following standard procedures for network meta-analysis (Sutton et al., 1999; Baird, 2018).

To assess the potential influence of carryover effects in crossover trials, we conducted a structured sensitivity analysis focusing on studies with unreported or insufficient washout periods (Mathur and VanderWeele, 2020; Mathur, 2024). All crossover studies lacking explicit washout information or reporting durations considered clinically inadequate (e.g., <24 h) were temporarily excluded, after which the network meta-analysis was re-estimated to evaluate changes in treatment ranking and effect-size estimates relative to the primary analysis. Additionally, crossover trials were stratified into those with reported versus unreported washout periods, and a node-splitting approach was applied to detect any subgroup-specific inconsistencies. This combined strategy allowed us to identify potential hidden carryover effects and ensured the robustness of the overall treatment estimates. When only the mean and range are reported, the standard deviation was estimated according to the method described by Hozo et al. (2005).

Gait-speed values reported in different units (e.g., m/s, cm/s) were manually converted to a uniform scale (cm/s) before analysis, enabling the use of mean differences (MDs) rather than standardized mean differences (SMDs). MDS-UPDRS Part III scores were assessed using standardized and widely adopted scoring systems, ensuring consistency across studies. Therefore, no additional standardization of effect sizes was required, and each outcome was synthesized using either mean differences (MDs) or the original scale, preserving clinical interpretability. The FOG-Q and NFOG-Q questionnaires were used, the outcome measure for FOG-Q was analyzed using the standardized mean difference (SMD) (Kwon and Reis, 2015; Weir et al., 2018), the conversion formulas are provided in Supplementary Note S2. To enhance data comparability and ensure consistency in pooled analyses, all extracted means and standard deviations were uniformly retained to one decimal place using the rounding method, ensuring the robustness. ### Assessment of risk of bias and methodological quality

This study employed a frequentist framework-based network meta-analysis, with all statistical analyses performed using the network-meta package in R (Daly and Soobiah, 2022). Data integration and management were conducted with Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, United States). For all study outcomes, mean difference (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was used as the effect size. Overall heterogeneity was assessed by the I2 statistic (Ades et al., 2024), categorized as low (0–40%), moderate (40–70%), or high (70–100%). Initially, a network evidence plot was generated to illustrate the comparative relationships among interventions. A fixed-effects model was applied to pool effect sizes when heterogeneity was low (0–40%); otherwise, a random-effects model was used., and both global and local inconsistency (node-splitting method) tests were performed to evaluate agreement between direct and indirect evidence. When significant inconsistency was detected (p < 0.10), finally, the relative efficacy of interventions was ranked using P-score (Rucker and Schwarzer, 2015).

To systematically evaluate the robustness of the network meta-analysis results, a multi-level sensitivity analysis strategy was implemented. First, results obtained from random-effects and fixed-effect models were compared to assess the potential impact of model selection on effect estimation (Rucker et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2017), the fixed-effect model assumes a common true effect across studies and estimates pooled effects purely based on within-study variance. Consistency between fixed- and random-effects estimates was taken as evidence of robustness to model assumptions. Second, analyses were repeated after excluding studies at high risk of bias to minimize the influence of study quality. Third, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially omitting each individual study to examine its influence on the overall effect estimate. In addition, potential sources of heterogeneity were explored through meta-regression and prespecified subgroup analyses (Venekamp et al., 2014), stratified by clinical characteristics such as mean disease duration, mean age, and washout period, thereby providing a scientific basis for interpreting the findings. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, <0.01 as highly significant, and <0.001 as extremely significant.

### MDS-UPDRS Part III scores

The network evidence diagram (Figure 3A) demonstrated that 24 randomized controlled trials (1–6, 8–25) evaluated 23 types of deep brain stimulation (DBS), namely: 60 HZ-STN-DBS; 60 μs-STN-DBS; 80 HZ-STN-DBS; aDBS; Central-STN-dDBS; CuN-DBS; DS-STN-DBS; GPi-DBS; IL-IL-DBS; Left-STN-DBS; Low-PPNa-DBS; OS-STN-DBS; Posterior-STN-dDBS; PPN-DBS; PPNa-DBS; rDBS; Right-STN-DBS; SNr LF-DBS; spDBS; STN-DBS; STN + SNr HF-DBS; STN + SNr LF-DBS; TBS-DBS. ## Discussion

### Principal findings and clinical implications

This systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) provides a comprehensive evaluation of the comparative efficacy of various deep brain stimulation (DBS) modalities on motor and gait function in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Following the PICOS framework and PRISMA guidelines, 25 studies were included. Future research may consider revising certain exclusion criteria with minimal impact on study validity to enhance the comprehensiveness of evidence synthesis. Our findings indicate differential efficacy across DBS targets and parameters: Low-PPNa-DBS, PPN-DBS, and CuN-DBS emerged as potentially beneficial for general motor impairment (MDS-UPDRS Part III), though evidence remains limited due to small sample sizes. This further supports that, in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), postoperative quality of life and motor and non-motor symptoms largely depend on DBS electrode implantation location and stimulation parameters (Moro et al., 2002; Horn et al., 2017; Petry-Schmelzer et al., 2019; Anderson et al., 2020; Anderson et al., 2021). Short-pulse DBS (spDBS) showed the most promising effects for improving gait speed among included studies, followed by conventional STN-DBS, this finding provides supporting evidence that shorter pulse widths broaden the therapeutic window of subthalamic nucleus stimulation (Reich et al., 2015; Bouthour et al., 2018; Dayal et al., 2020).

Notably, none of the DBS modalities yielded statistically significant improvements in Freezing of Gait (FOG) as measured by the FOG-Q, with all confidence intervals crossing zero. This finding reflects the multifactorial and episodic nature of FOG (Ehgoetz Martens et al., 2018; Weiss et al., 2020), which is likely mediated by extranigral circuits such as the pedunculopontine nucleus and mesencephalic locomotor region (Wojcik et al., 2025; Factor et al., 2025; Camastra et al., 2025), a meta-analyses (Schlenstedt et al., 2017) suggest that STN-DBS improves gait and FOG in the medication-off/stimulation-on state, suggesting that the observed lack of significant effect in this study may be influenced by variations in study design, stimulation parameters, or patient characteristics. The dissociation between improvements in continuous gait parameters and persistent FOG episodes emphasizes the need to differentiate between continuous motor control and episodic gait disturbances in DBS evaluations. Future studies should consider tailored stimulation strategies, including alternative targets or parameter modulation, to specifically address FOG, because evidence indicates that low-frequency stimulation (LFS, ~60 Hz) may improve freezing of gait (FOG) in Parkinson’s disease patients treated with bilateral STN-DBS (Xie et al., 2017; Blumenfeld et al., 2017), thereby enhancing the translational relevance of DBS for complex gait impairments in PD.

### Robustness of evidence and heterogeneity

Methodological rigor was a cornerstone of this investigation. Despite this statistical stability, we observed moderate-to-high heterogeneity. Our subgroup and meta-regression analyses identified disease duration as a significant moderator, with shorter disease duration associated with greater improvements in gait speed. This suggests a “window of opportunity” for DBS intervention, where earlier treatment—before the development of refractory axial symptoms or extensive non-dopaminergic degeneration—may yield superior functional mobility outcomes. Additionally, the apparent publication bias observed in FOG-Q outcomes (Egger’s test p < 0.0001) should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited number of studies per outcome, implying that smaller negative studies may remain unpublished and potentially inflate the perceived efficacy in the broader literature.

### Limitations

While this NMA offers high-level evidence, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, owing to missing data in some reports, we were unable to include some key parameters—preoperative motor severity and electrode placement etc.—in subgroup or regression analyses. Second, non-motor outcomes, such as cognition and mood (Poewe, 2008), were not systematically analyzed due to inconsistent or incomplete reporting in the included studies. These outcomes are clinically relevant and may be differentially affected by DBS modalities. Future research should more consistently assess and report non-motor outcomes to enable a comprehensive evaluation of DBS effects. Thirdly, because gait variability was not assessed in the majority of the included studies, this important outcome could not be analyzed in the present review; future systematic reviews should place greater emphasis on evaluating the effects of DBS on gait variability.

### Future directions

The field of neuromodulation for PD gait disorders stands at a critical juncture. The dissociation between motor score improvements and persistent freezing episodes necessitates a paradigm shift in target selection and stimulation paradigms. Future research should focus on the mechanistic effects of DBS at different targets on PD gait disturbances, clarifying the roles of specific targets in neural circuit modulation, motor function improvement, and gait parameter changes. Specifically, combined stimulation strategies warrant further investigation, though current evidence is preliminary and limited. Moreover, current studies display methodological inconsistencies in gait-related outcome assessment. The adoption of objective, wearable sensor-based gait analysis, rather than reliance solely on subjective scales like the FOG-Q, represents a vital step toward precision medicine in functional neurosurgery.

## Conclusion

Across multiple network meta-analyses, distinct DBS modalities demonstrated differential efficacy across motor and gait-related outcomes. For MDS-UPDRS Part III, Low-PPNa-DBS, PPN-DBS, and CuN-DBS yielded the most pronounced and statistically robust improvements, whereas several other STN-targeted interventions achieved moderate but meaningful reductions in motor symptoms. In contrast, analysis of FOG-Q scores revealed no statistically significant effects for any DBS intervention, with all confidence intervals crossing zero, indicating limited and inconclusive benefits for freezing of gait. Regarding gait speed, spDBS and STN-DBS showed clear and significant enhancements, while other modalities produced non-significant or borderline effects, suggesting variability in efficacy across interventions. Collectively, these findings underscore that while certain DBS approaches confer substantial motor and gait improvements, their impact on FOG remains uncertain, and heterogeneity in response highlights the need for individualized treatment considerations and further high-quality investigations.

# What is new in Freezing of Gait?

## Abstract
Freezing of Gait (FOG) research is entering a rapidly evolving phase. Published in early 2026, the International Consortium for FOG (ICFOG) released updated clinical and technical definitions of FOG and introduced a consensus-based standardized testing protocol, the Giladi protocol (GP-FOG), which is currently still under evaluation in an ongoing study. Complementing these advances, two new outcome measures, the clinician-reported outcome (ClinRO) and the patient-reported outcome (PRO), are highlighted in this review. Recent technological developments in both detection and potential prevention of FOG are moving the field toward individualized, biomarker-based, on-demand treatment strategies. In addition, emerging insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of FOG, particularly nondopaminergic contributions, are discussed. Together, these developments provide a contemporary framework for improving the assessment, understanding, and personalized management of FOG in Parkinson’s disease.

## Introduction

A substantial body of new work has emerged in the field of Freezing of Gait (FOG) in recent years, making an updated synthesis both timely and necessary. FOG is a common and highly disabling gait disturbance in Parkinson’s disease (PD), typically triggered during gait initiation, turning, or navigating narrow spaces, with around 80% of patients experiencing FOG across all disease stages [31]. In particular, FOG is strongly associated with reduced quality of life, an increased risk of falls and fractures, and greater loss of independence [59]. FOG is characterized by its paroxysmal and sudden onset, often described as a sensation of the feet being “glued” to the floor. Its episodic nature and clinical heterogeneity complicate experimental investigation, objective measurement, and reliable scoring in both clinical practice and research. These challenges have contributed to considerable heterogeneity in how FOG is defined, assessed, and reported across studies [25].

To address these limitations, the International Consortium for FOG (ICFOG) was established to bring together clinical and technical expertise and to develop a unified framework for FOG research. This review builds on these efforts by introducing the updated clinical and technical definitions of FOG, outlining a new testing protocol for accurately assessing FOG ‘on the fly’ and through video-based analysis, and presenting two upcoming rating scales: the clinician-reported (ClinRO) and patient-reported outcome (PRO). In addition, recent advances in technology-assisted assessment and treatment approaches are highlighted, reflecting the rapidly evolving landscape of FOG research and management. We conclude by reviewing current pathophysiological mechanisms, with a focus on nondopaminergic contributions.

## The clinical and technical definition of FOG

Why are two distinct definitions of FOG required, and what are the differences? The previously widely used definition proposed by Nutt et al. from 2011, describing FOG as a brief, episodic absence or marked reduction of forward progression of the feet despite the intention to walk [67], led to ambiguity in FOG classification, particularly with respect to episode duration and the presence of effective forward progression. The updated 2026 ICFOG clinical definition describes FOG as paroxysmal episodes wherein there is an inability to step effectively, despite attempting to do so [25]. A major advantage of this revised clinical definition is that FOG may occur during any gait-related stepping movement, including turning, step initiation, and walking in any direction, and can be easily applied in clinical settings. The attempt to step can be verified through self-report, observable body movements, or sensor-based measures, while the inability to step effectively is judged by the assessor relative to the individual’s own normal stepping pattern.

With regards to the technical definition of FOG, video scoring remains the gold-standard method for assessing FOG in clinical trials and research studies, typically quantified as the percentage of time spent with FOG in relation to the total task duration (%TF) [24]. Within the ICFOG framework, the onset of a FOG episode is defined as either the moment when any part of the foot lifts off the ground as part of the first ineffective step or the first attempt to initiate a step that does not result in any observable foot movement. A FOG episode ends with 1) no further attempt to take a step, 2) two consecutive effective steps that resemble typical stepping performance, or 3) a FOG-induced fall. Importantly, as FOG does not always involve a complete cessation of forward progression, the core of the episode, formerly referred to as motor blocks [22], may be additionally labeled and may occur once or repeatedly at any point during the FOG episode. The technical definition, therefore, distinguishes between FOG onset, FOG termination, the FOG core, and different FOG manifestations (Fig. 1). These manifestations include akinetic, kinetic-trembling, and kinetic-no-trembling FOG. Akinetic FOG is characterized by a complete cessation of lower-limb movement, whereas kinetic-trembling FOG is defined by rapid oscillatory movements. Kinetic-no-trembling FOG includes any other ineffective movements, such as paroxysmal shuffling or festination-freezing. Notably, continuous gait impairments, such as persistent shuffling or reduced step length, are not considered FOG under this definition. ## The Giladi protocol to objectively measure FOG in clinic and research settings

In addition to an updated FOG definition, a standardized measurement protocol is essential, since current patient-reported outcomes are not sufficiently reliable, and existing FOG testing protocols diverge across studies [25]. A variety of clinical observation measures or instrumented gait assessments have used a range of different tools to assess FOG [56, 73, 94]. However, the utility of these approaches is constrained by several limitations, including insufficient standardization and gold-standard validation, extensive test batteries, no representation of common triggers of freezing (e.g., turning, dual-tasking, navigating doorways), as well as the impression that scores (e.g. the Ziegler FOG score) do not truly reflect clinical severity [30, 53]. To resolve these issues, a newly developed consensus-based FOG-provoking protocol, the Giladi protocol (GP-FOG), using a clinician-reported outcome (ClinRO) to quantify FOG severity in real-time, is currently being validated in an international, multicenter study (FOG-COA, NCT06519279). The Giladi protocol is named in memoriam of our dear colleague Nir Giladi, who made substantial contributions to the ICFOG and the development of these new definitions, the protocol, and outcomes scores. The test itself comprises a series of eight walking trials of varying complexity, as well as common FOG triggers, such as turning on the spot [5, 26] or walking through a doorway [2, 57]. Notably, walking in fast, short steps (“shuffling”) as well as full, rapid turns have been identified as the most sensitive ways to provoke FOG [66]. The GP-FOG consists of eight walking trials, including walking, dual-task walking, carrying a ball, turning, dual-task turning, shuffling around a box, box agility, which includes performing small forward, backward, and lateral steps around a ground-marked square, and walking through a doorway [55]. Dual-tasking involves performing the movement task simultaneously with a serial-3 subtraction task, i.e., counting backward from a randomly selected three-digit number in threes. The test is user-friendly, can be completed within 20 minutes, and does not require any additional equipment. The participants are asked to accomplish each task as quickly and safely as possible, while avoiding any strategies or techniques to prevent FOG.

## New rating scales: ClinRO & PRO

Along with the new Giladi FOG-provoking protocol (GP-FOG), a new rating scale, the clinician-reported outcome (ClinRO) is currently being developed by the ICFOG and is being validated in the FOG-COA study above. Current FOG rating scales fall short in rating the complexity of FOG: the MDS-UPDRS part III item 3.11. [29] is often used to evaluate the presence of FOG. Therefore, the participant walks for at least 10 m, turns 180°, and returns to the examiner, which is not a very sensitive approach to assess FOG [45, 66]. Although widely used, the Ziegler FOG score, covering 360° turns, doorway walking, and a dual-task (counting backward) with or without carrying water, also fails to differentiate between episodes of varying frequency or duration [30, 94]. Thus, there is a pressing need for a reliable clinical assessment outcome that can be widely implemented in both clinical and research settings to evaluate FOG severity, incorporating both frequency and duration of freezing episodes.

The patient-reported outcome (PRO) is also currently being developed and validated to evaluate the impact of FOG on a patient’s quality of life and to capture ‘the voice of the patient’, which will be critical when seeking FDA approvals for future therapies. This validation is also part of the ongoing FOG-COA study, described above. Current patient-reported outcome questionnaires, including the Giladi Freezing of Gait Questionnaire [23], the new Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (NFOGQ) [65], and the MDS-UPDRS part II item 2.13. [29] have limited validity and insufficient sensitivity to detect small effect sizes, rendering them unsuitable for clinical trials [37, 74]. The novel PRO that is being evaluated will potentially capture information based on the last 4 weeks from patients and their partners, addressing aspects of emotion, confidence, and self-worth, which could then be correlated with objective measures of FOG.

## Advances in technology to measure FOG

While ClinRO and gold-standard video-based ratings remain task-specific, emerging technologies offer the potential for more sensitive and comprehensive FOG assessments across both supervised and unsupervised settings. Notably, objective accelerometry-based measures provide a reliable quantification of freezing severity and mitigate the variability inherent with subjective clinical ratings [62].

Inertial measurement units (IMUs) are among the most commonly used and easily deployable tools for quantifying movement [55]. However, considerable variability remains in sensor number and placement, with current recommendations favoring five sensors in supervised laboratory settings and a single lumbar sensor for unsupervised assessments [55]. Despite their potential, IMU-based FOG detection is limited by the absence of a robust, validated analytical framework [55]. Moreover, unsupervised, continuous at-home IMU monitoring alone is not sufficiently sensitive to capture all aspects of FOG, highlighting the need to combine patient-reported outcomes, in-home FOG-provoking tests, and continuous monitoring to comprehensively assess FOG severity [15]. The Gait Advisors Leading Outcomes for Parkinson’s (GALOP) committee, an advisory committee for the Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson’s Research (MJFF), recommended a minimum set of metadata to accompany standardized gait protocols, including pace, rhythm, asymmetry, variability, and turning measures [54].

The ultimate goal is to sensitively detect FOG in home-based environments, as PD patients’ gait performance in supervised laboratory settings often overestimates patients’ real-life gait abilities [36]. Besides, IMUs need to be permanently worn on the body, and the recording duration is limited by battery life [49]. One promising technology for continuous gait assessment is the potential use of a radar system where electromagnetic waves are transmitted, and the shift in reflected waves is measured, using the Doppler effect to measure the velocity of a participant. Radar provides multiple benefits, being independent of lighting, ensuring participant anonymization, and offering a cost-effective solution [87]. It was initially demonstrated to reliably capture gait characteristics, such as gait speed and step time, in a laboratory setting [87]. A three-node radar network featuring one torso node and two feet nodes has been shown to be the most adequate to extract spatiotemporal gait parameters in PD patients [49]. A recent study applied a low-power radar system in the homes of 50 participants, with and without PD, using a signal strength 1000 times lower than typical home Wi-Fi [46]. Combined with advanced signal processing and machine learning algorithms, this approach successfully assessed in-home gait. Strikingly, radar-measured gait speed correlated with PD severity, including MDS-UPDRS part III total scores, axial symptoms, and Hoehn and Yahr stage. Furthermore, the radar system captured disease progression by monitoring gait decline in a small sub-cohort of two participants over one year and motor fluctuations in response to levodopa intake, with gait speed increasing following medication administration [46]. These novel findings suggest the potential for automatic, wearable-free detection of freezing episodes in home environments.

## Advances in technology to prevent FOG

In FOG research, one important aim is to detect freezing episodes before they occur and ultimately prevent them. With emerging approaches, this objective is becoming increasingly attainable. In recent years, electrophysiological signals have been analyzed to identify a typical “freezing signature” with abnormal activity preceding and accompanying FOG episodes. These signals include cortical measures such as electroencephalography (EEG) and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), local field potentials (LFP), and kinematic signals, which may enable the development of multimodal systems for individualized monitoring and prevention of FOG [40].

Electrical activity of cortical neurons can be measured using EEG, which provides high temporal resolution, whereas fNIRS captures hemodynamic responses with better spatial resolution. During a Timed-Up-and-Go (TUG) task, EEG power in the alpha and beta bands increased in the central sensorimotor and occipital regions before the onset of freezing episodes, compared with normal walking, and the actual freezing episodes were associated with significantly increased theta and alpha band power within the central and occipital areas [10]. Furthermore, freezing episodes could be distinguished from voluntary stopping periods, which were associated with increased high-beta power in frontal, central, parietal, and occipital regions, as well as decreases in delta to low-beta power relative to FOG episodes. These findings were corroborated by fNIRS, which demonstrated that turning and doorway negotiation elicit cortical activity patterns resembling those observed during voluntary stopping, characterized by activation of the supplementary motor area (SMA) and prefrontal regions. In contrast, during freezing episodes, prefrontal cortical activation was reduced compared with voluntary stopping. Based on these observations, the authors hypothesized that freezing-provoking tasks may engage a stopping network with increased susceptibility to freezing [14]. However, due to the low signal-to-noise ratio of fNIRS and EEG, a large number of gait cycles must be recorded and analyzed using advanced algorithms to reliably distinguish gait-related from movement-related neural activity [55].

One of the major advances in Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) technology in recent years has been the introduction of local field potential (LFP) sensing using the SenSight™ electrodes (Medtronic). LFPs reflect the summed postsynaptic activity of neuronal populations in the vicinity of the stimulation target. Within this signal, beta-band oscillations in the subthalamic nucleus (STN) have been consistently associated with motor impairment, particularly bradykinesia, and are modulated by dopaminergic and DBS therapy, with increased beta power serving as a marker of greater motor symptom severity [17, 42, 43]. Current DBS therapy uses continuous, 24/7 stimulation, which may lead to stimulation-related side effects such as dysarthria, stimulation-induced dyskinesia, or neuropsychiatric adverse effects [16, 80, 92]. By leveraging patient-specific beta-band activity, the newly available approach of adaptive DBS (aDBS) enables automatic adjustment of stimulation parameters based on predefined thresholds. However, movement-related LFP fluctuations [63] as well as ECG-artifacts [76] still remain challenging, complicating subsequent analyses. The successful implementation of aDBS still requires substantial clinical experience and research.

Despite overall improvements in global motor outcomes, gait worsening after bilateral subthalamic nucleus (STN) stimulation has been reported in approximately 42% of patients [85]. Furthermore, while DBS generally provides sustained long-term improvement in bradykinesia and tremor, gait performance often declines within the first five years post-implantation [77]. DBS programming troubleshooting strategies include low-frequency stimulation, lateralized subthalamic stimulation, and STN-substantia nigra costimulation [60] [47] [70, 90]. However, these approaches yield mixed results and are often associated with worsening of overall motor scores. A recent approach is to program aDBS for STN-DBS patients experiencing FOG [3]. In fact, a small pilot study confirmed the safety, tolerability, and feasibility of beta-burst driven aDBS for FOG [91]. Theoretically, identifying the FOG spectral biomarker in real time may allow DBS to be dynamically adjusted to either prevent a freezing episode or alleviate freezing episodes [55].

An alternative but well-established clinical strategy to prevent and overcome FOG is through the phenomenon of cueing [72]. Visual and auditory cues help focus attention on walking and shift gait control from habitual to goal-directed motor patterns [28]. In general, visual cues tend to improve stride length, whereas auditory cues primarily enhance cadence [78]. Externally generated cues can even improve gait performance during dual-task conditions [48]. Various, often expensive, cueing devices have been shown to improve gait, such as laser pointers [93] and laser-light shoes with foot pressure and inertial sensors [11]. Nevertheless, repeated exposure can result in habituation, reducing their long-term utility [28]. Moreover, no single cue is universally optimal, as effectiveness depends on individual factors such as cognitive function, and it remains unclear which patients benefit most from specific types of cues [27, 28]. Accordingly, the multicenter UNITE-PD study is currently investigating patient characteristics, changes in cueing efficacy, and the neural correlates of cueing [1]. Additionally, externally generated cues may be less effective than internally generated cues, such as singing [32], highlighting the need for more advanced cueing technologies [68] that integrate electrophysiological and kinematic signals. In line with the concept of aDBS, providing each patient with individually tailored, on-demand stimulation, adaptive cueing could offer similar benefits by responding to a patient’s unique ‘freezing signature’ and potentially preventing FOG before it occurs. Early approaches have shown promising results. For example, a novel on-demand cueing system using instrumented insoles detects FOG episodes via increased variability in step time, and delivers visual cues on demand, resulting in reduced freezing and increased walking speed [68]. Another innovative device, Cue2Walk, employs a single sensor worn on the lateral side of the shank below the knee to provide auditory and/or vibratory cues. Cueing can be triggered manually via a button press or hands-free gesture, such as a heel tap, or automatically, based on real-time detection of FOG episodes using a tri-axial integrated accelerometer and a specialized algorithm [84]. Notably, a model combining IMU and plantar pressure features accurately identified the shift from preFOG to FOG, highlighting its potential application in real-time FOG detection and adaptive cueing [69]. Additionally, analyses using fNIRS and EEG indicated higher parietal alpha activity during cueing in patients at later Hoehn and Yahr stages, which could be used as an electrophysiological signal to identify cueing responders [86]. Together, these findings further underscore the need for an individualized, biomarker-based cueing approach.

## Updates on FOG pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of FOG is still not well understood, but likely relates to a loss of automated motor control when there is a processing overload across disseminated neural networks, resulting in gait disturbances [83]. The Neural Reserve or Cross-Talk Hypothesis proposed by Lewis and Barker considers FOG as the momentary breakdown of normal gait, when there is an inability to concurrently process motor, cognitive, and limbic information, which overloads the system [44, 64]. The proposed model further asserts that in overcoming a freezing episode, the patient is able to suspend their performance of additional cognitive and limbic processes. By focusing on a goal-directed behavior, there is a reduction in the degree of over-activation within the output nuclei of the basal ganglia, allowing this circuitry to be ‘‘reset’’, once again facilitating movement. Thus, FOG appears to reflect a lack of neural reserve across shared pathways dealing with incoming information and functional output. A recent review from Tosserams and colleagues highlights the core pathophysiological principles of FOG: (1) loss of gait automation due to basal ganglia neurodegeneration with high inhibitory basal ganglia output, (2) degeneration of compensatory cortical areas, and 3) impaired integration of cerebral compensatory networks [83].

While dopaminergic therapies can improve FOG in some individuals, they do not fully ameliorate FOG symptoms [50]. Levodopa-unresponsive FOG suggests the involvement of nondopaminergic pathways, as does the clinical heterogeneity observed in patients with FOG [19]. Current evidence suggests that noradrenergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic systems might be involved in the pathophysiology of FOG. Beyond dopaminergic contribution, the role of the neurotransmitter noradrenaline has been recently implicated in FOG. An increase in arousal is associated [18, 52] and biologically linked to states of brain network integration implicated in this phenomenon [81, 82]. Effective integration of distinct and segregated brain networks critically relies on the noradrenergic ascending arousal system, which appears to be impaired to varying degrees in patients with FOG [58, 75, 88]. Widespread noradrenergic denervation has been observed in patients with FOG, particularly involving the Locus coeruleus and the thalamic noradrenergic system, both of which are closely linked to limbic circuits implicated in FOG pathophysiology [58]. Atomoxetine is a promising noradrenergic drug, as it is safe and well-tolerated in PD [71, 89]. It is a selective noradrenaline transporter inhibitor (SNRI), which prevents the cellular reuptake of noradrenaline, and is widely used for the treatment of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [41]. Atomoxetine has been shown to improve attention, response inhibition, executive functions, and anxiety in PD [89], which are all clinical features that have been associated with FOG severity. However, to date, only inconsistent reports of improvements in FOG have been reported in small samples [39, 71]. The Antifreeze trial (NCT07316296), sponsored by the MJFF, is a multicenter, single-dose, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial with a cross-over design. The goal of this clinical trial is to investigate whether atomoxetine can reduce FOG and identify potential biomarkers for freezing responders.

Regarding cholinergic involvement, the striatum, the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), and the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) represent the principal cholinergic sources in the brain and are affected by α-synuclein-related neurodegeneration [6]. Levodopa-unresponsive PD patients experiencing FOG have been shown to exhibit bilateral reductions in cholinergic terminals, predominantly in extrastriatal regions, using vesicular acetylcholine transporter positron emission tomography (VAChT-PET)[12]. Cholinergic deficits have also been demonstrated in patients with PD and a history of falls, suggesting a shared underlying mechanism for falls and FOG within a framework of impaired cognitive integration [7]. Therefore, the PPN has emerged as a promising DBS target, with small pilot studies reporting reductions in FOG and falls in subsets of patients [21, 61]. A currently ongoing clinical trial (NCT04605263) is comparing bilateral STN-PPN DBS with conventional STN-DBS. However, interim results from an exploratory randomized, double-blind, crossover trial did not demonstrate gait improvement after two months of combined STN-PPN DBS in patients with levodopa-resistant gait and balance impairments [9]. Pharmacologically, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial (ReSPonD) reported promising results with improved step-time variability in patients treated with rivastigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor [33]. Nevertheless, the results of the CHIEF-PD trial (NCT04226248), a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study evaluating rivastigmine for fall prevention, are still pending.

Apart from noradrenergic and cholinergic deficits, depression, a symptom commonly associated with FOG [20], is linked to the serotonergic dysfunction in PD [4]. Notably, reduced cerebrospinal fluid serotonin levels have been observed in advanced PD patients with gait disorders [38]. Clinical data on serotonergic drug effects on gait function remain limited. However, previously, ritanserin, a highly selective 5-HT2 receptor antagonist, significantly improved gait in 7 out of 10 patients in one small clinical trial [34]. Similarly, a short-term randomized, placebo-controlled 4-week trial found an improved walking speed in patients treated with paroxetine, a serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), compared to placebo [13]. With regard to FOG, functional MRI studies have revealed reduced functional connectivity between the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) and cortical structures, such as the supplementary motor area (SMA), a key region implicated in gait control and FOG [51]. Furthermore, preliminary evidence suggests that both selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) may improve FOG [79].

In summary, nondopaminergic mechanisms underlying FOG may help delineate specific freezer subtypes and inform tailored noradrenergic, cholinergic, or serotonergic treatment strategies.

## Conclusion

Altogether, rapid progress in the field is enabling greater standardization in both clinical and research settings. The emergence of on-demand treatment strategies offers new hope for patients with Parkinson’s disease affected by FOG. Beyond identifying freezing episodes and developing potential interventions, the mechanisms underlying why and when nonfreezers transition to freezers remain poorly understood. Several risk factors have been identified and can be categorized into modifiable factors, such as more severe motor and cognitive impairment, and nonmodifiable factors, including older age and longer disease duration [35]. A recent machine-learning-based study further highlighted specific gait characteristics, including step length and stride width across both medication states, that are associated with freezing severity [8]. Understanding these predictors represents a pressing step toward elucidating the mechanisms behind freezing conversion and ultimately guiding targeted, individualized interventions.

## Introduction

Gait and balance impairment are among the most disabling manifestations of Parkinson’s disease and represent a major contributor to falls, loss of independence, institutionalization, and mortality. Although dopaminergic therapy can improve gait in earlier stages of the disease, after some time, the gait and balance issues worsen and become progressively resistant to medication, reflecting the increasing involvement of non-dopaminergic and multisystem pathology [1], [2], [3].

Deep brain stimulation, most commonly targeting the subthalamic nucleus, is a well-established and effective treatment for advanced Parkinson’s disease, with a strong therapeutic effect on tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and motor fluctuations. Its effects on gait and balance, however, are less consistent and appear to evolve over the postoperative course [4], [5]. While some patients experience early improvement in gait, particularly in the medication OFF state, a substantial proportion develop worsening of freezing of gait, postural instability, or falls during follow-up, even when appendicular motor symptoms remain well controlled [6], [7]. This apparent dissociation between appendicular and axial outcomes has been reported across multiple observational cohorts and longitudinal studies and represents a major unresolved challenge in the long-term management of patients treated with STN-DBS [8]. Traditionally, late-onset gait and balance deterioration after STN-DBS has been attributed to disease progression and the emergence of non-dopaminergic deficits affecting locomotor and postural control networks [9]. This, however, does not fully explain the variability and timing of postoperative gait worsening. There are a number of indications that suggest a potential role of stimulation in gait and balance worsening in at least a subset of patients.

The early postoperative worsening, which in some cases occurs weeks to months after surgery, is difficult to explain in terms of a neurodegenerative process, and this phenomenon has been described in both cohort studies and mechanistic case series [10], [11]. In addition, gait deterioration may preferentially occur in specific medication or stimulation states or improve following stimulation adjustment, further challenging a purely progression-based explanation [12].

Gait and balance outcomes after STN-DBS are also sensitive to stimulation parameters. Changes in stimulation frequency, amplitude, or contact configuration can markedly influence freezing of gait and other axial symptoms, with some patients experiencing improvement following systematic parameter modification [13]. These findings highlight the complexity of the neural network subserving gait control and indicate that the effects of stimulation on neural networks might influence disease progression and dopaminergic state.

The anatomical aspects have added another layer of complexity to the understanding of the mechanisms. Acute levodopa-resistant FOG has been described in the context of electrode implantation and stimulation activation, and it has been attributed to stimulation of fibers located near or dorsal to the STN, indicating network disruption rather than neurodegeneration as a possible causal factor [14].

Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that STN stimulation and levodopa can have partly independent or even opposite effects on gait and postural control, highlighting the role of stimulation-medication interactions [15].

Other targets provide useful context, even though they are not the focus of this review. Comparative meta-analytic data suggest that STN-DBS and GPi-DBS result in a similar long-term motor benefit, without clear evidence that one target confers a uniform long-term gait advantage [16]. The pedunculopontine nucleus has also been targeted for severe gait freezing and postural instability, but published responses remain variable, with variable methodology, and are limited by small cohorts and uncertain durability [17].

However, despite extensive investigations, most of the studies are still descriptive, though they differ in terms of design, testing conditions, and follow-up time, making the results difficult to reconcile. Perhaps more importantly, none of the current studies clearly address the distinction between stimulation-induced effects, stimulation-therapy interactions, and disease progression, nor do they organize information along lines commonly utilized in counterfactual decision-making. In clinical practice, attribution of gait worsening is inherently counterfactual, involving systematic comparison across stimulation, medication, and time conditions. Counterfactual thinking, in this context, is a question of how gait or balance might have performed under a different stimulation, medication, or time condition. This is simple. It is also clinically useful.

This review aims to address these issues by reassessing gait and balance worsening following STN-DBS within a counterfactual, attribution-based model. This review was undertaken because a similar postoperative complaint can have different implications for management, such as acute worsening that varies across stimulation conditions, suggesting a need to adjust stimulation, versus late worsening that is consistent across stimulation conditions, suggesting disease progression and a need to adjust rehabilitation, fall risk, and axial care strategies. By reviewing mechanistic, programming, and observational studies, we hope to shed light on which aspects of gait and balance deterioration are plausibly stimulation-related, which reflect interactions with dopaminergic therapy, and which are more consistent with disease progression.

## Methods

### Study design and scope

This review has been performed as a structured narrative synthesis of systematic literature searches, specifically on the decline in gait and balance following subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation in PD. The review has aimed to attribute the decline in gait and balance following STN-DBS in PD to the effects of stimulation, drug-stimulation interactions, or natural disease progression over time. In contrast to other reviews that have simply described the occurrence of gait/balance decline following STN-DBS in PD or summarized the efficacy of specific programming interventions, this review has been specifically designed to address clinically relevant counterfactual questions.

### Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception through December 2025. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords related to Parkinson’s disease, subthalamic nucleus, deep brain stimulation, gait, balance, postural instability, freezing of gait, axial symptoms, stimulation parameters, programming, and longitudinal or long-term outcomes. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full-texts of potentially eligible articles were reviewed. Reference lists of eligible articles were further screened for potentially suitable studies.

### Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were eligible if they were focused on Parkinson’s disease treated with STN-DBS and reported gait- or balance-related outcomes. In order to be involved, studies were required to provide at least one of the following: stimulation state during testing, medication state during testing, stimulation parameter manipulation, contact or target information relevant to interpretation, or follow-up duration sufficient to assess temporal change. Original studies, including observational cohorts, interventional programming studies, controlled experimental paradigms, and mechanistic case series, were included as primary sources of evidence. Animal studies, technical reports without clinical gait or balance outcomes, studies that did not separate axial outcomes from global motor scores, and duplicate reports without materially new gait analyses were excluded from the primary database. Conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail, non-English publications without accessible full text, and studies lacking interpretable postoperative gait or balance assessment conditions were also excluded. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and narrative reviews were added to contextualize findings and to compare methodological approaches, but were not used as sources of primary data.

### Data extraction

From each included study, data were extracted on study design, follow-up duration, gait and balance outcomes assessed, stimulation parameters, medication state during assessment, and temporal patterns of change. Particular attention was paid to whether outcomes were assessed under stimulation ON versus OFF conditions, medication ON versus OFF conditions, following stimulation parameter modification, or across different postoperative time points.

### Attribution framework and synthesis

Given heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted. Evidence was organized according to clinically relevant counterfactual contrasts routinely used in clinical decision-making, including stimulation ON versus OFF, medication ON versus OFF, stimulation parameter modification, and early versus late postoperative periods, to distinguish stimulation-related effects from stimulation-medication interactions and time-dependent changes consistent with disease progression. Counterfactual contrasts were defined as within-patient or within-cohort comparisons that approximate the clinical question: “What would gait or balance have been under an alternative stimulation, medication, or temporal condition?”.

### Methodological considerations

Formal risk-of-bias assessment tools were not applied due to the predominance of observational, mechanistic, and hypothesis-generating studies and the substantial heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and assessment conditions. Methodological limitations relevant to causal interpretation were considered qualitatively and integrated into the interpretation of findings.

### PRISMA compliance

The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [18]. Following database searching and reference screening, 29 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, comprising 18 original studies reporting primary gait or balance outcomes and 11 review articles used for contextual interpretation. In addition to these included articles, further background and methodological references, including reporting guidelines, were cited to support study design, interpretation, and contextual framing.

### Stimulation-related gait and balance worsening

Acute gait worsening temporally associated with stimulation activation, transient gait deterioration related to electrode implantation, and early postoperative deterioration emerging within weeks to months after surgery were reported in mechanistic case series and observational studies. Acute freezing of gait induced by STN stimulation and resolving with stimulation deactivation was described in early postoperative assessments [32]. Levodopa-resistant freezing of gait emerging immediately after electrode implantation was reported in isolated cases and small series independent of stimulation activation [33]. Acute axial worsening shortly after stimulation onset, with partial reversibility following stimulation adjustment, was observed in several reports [34].

Parameter-dependent modulation of gait outcomes was demonstrated in multiple interventional studies. Improvement in freezing of gait following systematic reduction of stimulation frequency from conventional high-frequency settings was reported in short-term crossover and interventional designs [35], [36], [37]. The literature supports a clinically relevant low-frequency range, usually 60–80 Hz in selected patients, rather than a single reproducible cutoff. The converse point is just as important: available studies do not identify a universal amplitude, pulse width, or contact threshold above which freezing of gait or imbalance reliably occurs. Contact location appears clinically relevant, but the signal is anatomical rather than binary; adverse axial effects have often been linked to current spread dorsal to the STN or into adjacent fibers, whereas improvement after contact relocation suggests that some cases are programming-sensitive. In practical terms, the available literature more often implicates dorsal or dorsally spreading stimulation than a specific lower contact as a trigger of gait worsening, likely because current spread above the intended STN motor territory may recruit pallidofugal, zona incerta, or other adjacent fiber pathways involved in locomotor control. However, the evidence does not support a uniform upper-versus-lower contact rule across patients, because contact effects depend on lead position, individual anatomy, and the volume of tissue activated. Evidence comparing monopolar with bipolar configurations remains limited and inconsistent, so a general rule cannot be justified. Additional programming strategies, including amplitude reduction, asymmetric stimulation, interleaving, and contact relocation, were associated with variable effects on gait and axial symptoms and were frequently accompanied by trade-offs in appendicular motor control [38], [39], [16].

### Stimulation-medication interactions

Several experimental and clinical studies reported differential gait and balance outcomes depending on combined stimulation and medication state. Independent and sometimes opposing effects of STN stimulation and levodopa on postural sway were demonstrated using quantitative posturography [40]. Differential freezing of gait responses across stimulation ON/OFF and medication ON/OFF conditions were reported, with effects strongly influenced by baseline dopaminergic responsiveness and postoperative medication reduction [41], [17]. Attenuation of levodopa-related improvement in gait and akinesia during STN stimulation was observed in controlled experimental paradigms [42]. Prospective studies further documented that freezing of gait responses varied across medication conditions, including improvement in the medication OFF state with stimulation and worsening when stimulation and medication were combined [43]. These interaction effects were observed despite sustained improvement in other motor domains.

### Time-dependent changes in gait and balance

Longitudinal cohort studies consistently demonstrated divergence between appendicular motor outcomes and axial features over time. Progressive worsening of gait and postural stability during extended follow-up was reported despite sustained improvement in tremor and rigidity [23], [24]. Long-term studies focusing on axial outcomes documented relative deterioration of gait and balance compared with appendicular motor symptoms several years after surgery [25], [44]. Persistent gait and balance impairment emerging with longer postoperative duration was reported across deep brain stimulation targets, with some alternative targets demonstrating short-term benefit but limited durability over time [45]. Meta-regression analyses and longitudinal syntheses demonstrated progressive worsening of balance and postural stability outcomes with increasing follow-up duration, while gait-related outcomes showed greater heterogeneity, even when global motor scores remained improved [46].

### Balance-specific outcomes

Quantitative posturography studies demonstrated that STN stimulation may differentially affect balance control, with improvement in some static measures of postural sway but worsening of dynamic or adaptive balance responses under certain conditions, despite improvement in other motor domains [29], [40]. Reviews focusing on balance physiology emphasized that postural instability reflects deficits in multisensory integration and anticipatory postural control, which were inconsistently responsive to STN stimulation across studies [28].

### Summary of findings across attribution domains

Across the included literature, three non-exclusive attribution patterns were observed, with parameter-dependent and contact-dependent phenomena grouped as stimulation-related/programming-sensitive effects. Early postoperative gait worsening that varied by stimulation state or stimulation parameters was reported in acute and short-term studies [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]. Differential gait or balance responses across medication conditions were documented in experimental and prospective investigations [40], [41], [17], [42], [43]. Progressive late-onset balance and postural stability decline, with more heterogeneous effects on gait, was most consistently reported in longitudinal cohorts with extended follow-up [23], [24], [25], [44], [45], [46]. Individual studies frequently contributed evidence to more than one attribution domain. ## Discussion

### Integration with prior reviews

Previous reviews have addressed gait and balance outcomes after STN-DBS, but most have approached the topic descriptively or interventionally rather than through explicit attribution. Systematic reviews have documented the frequency and timing of gait and balance worsening following STN-DBS, highlighting that axial symptoms often deteriorate despite sustained appendicular motor benefit [7]. Programming-focused reviews and meta-analyses have emphasized the potential of parameter modifications, particularly low-frequency stimulation, to alleviate freezing of gait while noting substantial heterogeneity and limited durability of benefit [13], [14], [35]. Consistent with prior narrative reviews, axial disability after DBS has been attributed predominantly to disease progression and non-dopaminergic pathology, although these reviews have not systematically disentangled stimulation effects from medication interactions or temporal evolution [5], [6].

The present review differs from these prior syntheses by explicitly organizing evidence around clinically relevant counterfactual contrasts. Rather than grouping studies by intervention category alone, findings were interpreted according to stimulation ON/OFF effects, stimulation-medication interactions, and time-dependent change. This framework allows apparently contradictory findings, such as improvement of freezing of gait in some contexts and worsening in others, to be reconciled within a unified attribution model.

### Stimulation-related gait and balance worsening

Acute gait worsening temporally associated with stimulation activation, transient gait deterioration related to electrode implantation, and early postoperative deterioration emerging within weeks to months after surgery have been reported in mechanistic case series and observational studies. Acute freezing of gait induced by STN stimulation and resolving with stimulation deactivation was described in early postoperative assessments [32]. Levodopa-resistant freezing of gait emerging immediately after electrode implantation was reported in isolated cases and small series, independent of stimulation activation [33]. Acute axial worsening shortly after stimulation onset, with partial reversibility following stimulation adjustment, was observed in several reports [34].

Parameter-dependent modulation of gait outcomes was demonstrated in multiple interventional studies. Improvement in freezing of gait following systematic reduction of stimulation frequency from conventional high-frequency settings was reported in short-term crossover and interventional designs [35], [36], [37]. The literature supports a clinically relevant low-frequency range, usually 60–80 Hz in selected patients, rather than a single reproducible cutoff. By contrast, available studies do not identify a universal amplitude, pulse width, or contact threshold above which freezing of gait or imbalance reliably occurs. Contact location appears clinically relevant, but the signal is anatomical rather than binary: adverse axial effects have more often been linked to dorsal or dorsally spreading stimulation above the intended STN motor territory, likely through recruitment of adjacent fiber pathways involved in locomotor control, whereas improvement after contact relocation suggests that some cases are programming-sensitive. However, the evidence does not support a uniform upper-versus-lower contact rule across patients, because contact effects depend on lead position, individual anatomy, and the volume of tissue activated. Evidence comparing monopolar and bipolar configurations remains limited and inconsistent, so no general rule can be justified. Even when deterioration is obvious after DBS, it should not automatically be solely interpreted as direct stimulation-induced harm, because medication reduction, altered network compensation, and time-dependent disease progression may contribute concurrently.

### Stimulation-medication interactions as a source of apparent worsening

Several studies indicate that gait and balance deterioration after STN-DBS may reflect complex interactions between stimulation and dopaminergic therapy rather than a unidirectional stimulation effect. Experimental studies have demonstrated that STN stimulation can attenuate the beneficial effects of levodopa on gait and akinesia, leading to paradoxical worsening in the medication ON state [42]. Prospective cohorts have further shown that freezing of gait responses differ depending on medication condition, with some patients improving in the OFF state but worsening when stimulation and medication are combined [17], [43]. These findings are supported by quantitative posturography studies showing partially independent and sometimes opposing effects of STN stimulation and levodopa on postural sway and balance control [40].

### Time-dependent progression and late axial decline

Long-term studies consistently demonstrate that gait and balance outcomes differ from appendicular motor responses over time. Multicentre cohorts with extended follow-up have shown progressive worsening of gait and postural instability despite sustained improvement in tremor and rigidity [23], [24]. Studies that explicitly focus on axial outcomes have identified time since surgery as a major determinant of late-stage gait and balance decline, often refractory to further stimulation or medication optimization [25], [45]. Regression and longitudinal syntheses reinforce this pattern, indicating that balance outcomes worsen with increasing follow-up duration, while gait outcomes show greater heterogeneity, even when overall motor scores remain improved [46]. These findings support the interpretation that late axial deterioration predominantly reflects disease progression and non-dopaminergic pathology rather than stimulation failure alone.

### Balance outcomes and non-locomotor mechanisms

Balance-specific outcomes have been studied less frequently than freezing of gait but offer important mechanistic insight. Experimental studies using posturography have demonstrated that STN-DBS can have divergent effects on balance, often improving static postural sway while impairing dynamic balance control and adaptive postural responses under certain conditions, despite improving other motor domains [40]. Reviews of postural control emphasize that balance impairment in Parkinson’s disease reflects deficits in multisensory integration, anticipatory postural adjustments, and brainstem network function, which may be less responsive to modulation by STN stimulation [28].

### Clinical and methodological implications

Systematic assessment of gait and balance across stimulation and medication states should be routine when postoperative worsening is reported. The first practical split is temporal and physiologic: early decline that changes with stimulation state, contact selection, or frequency is more likely to be stimulation-related; late decline that persists despite structured reprogramming is more likely to reflect disease progression. In programming-sensitive cases, clinicians should test alternative contacts, reduce current spread when possible, and consider lower-frequency stimulation in selected patients, while documenting trade-offs in tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. No single contact level, pulse width, voltage, amplitude, or monopolar-bipolar configuration can be recommended as a universal gait-safe setting. Responses are patient-specific and should be determined empirically through structured testing. When deterioration proves stimulation-insensitive, management should shift toward medication review, rehabilitation, fall-risk reduction, assistive strategies, and counseling about axial progression rather than repeated empiric programming alone.

Future studies would benefit from standardized gait and balance outcomes, explicit reporting of stimulation and medication conditions, and longitudinal designs capable of capturing time-dependent change. Randomized trials of programming strategies should incorporate causal contrasts rather than relying solely on pre-post comparisons.

## Limitations

The included literature is heterogeneous, with most studies being observational, exploratory, or small interventional investigations rather than randomized controlled trials. Because counterfactual contrasts were reconstructed from non-randomized studies, causal attribution remains probabilistic and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Balance outcomes remain underrepresented relative to freezing of gait, limiting definitive conclusions in this domain.

## Conclusions

Gait and balance deterioration after DBS in Parkinson’s disease reflects a heterogeneous set of mechanisms, rather than a single uniform process. Evidence indicates that postoperative worsening may arise from stimulation-related effects, interactions between stimulation and dopaminergic therapy, or time-dependent disease progression. These mechanisms frequently coexist and may dominate at different postoperative stages. By organizing the literature around attribution-relevant counterfactual contrasts, this review provides a clinically grounded framework for interpreting postoperative gait and balance outcomes, guiding stimulation and medication adjustments, and informing long-term management after STN-DBS. Early, stimulation-sensitive deterioration should prompt systematic programming and medication-state testing, whereas late, stimulation-insensitive decline is more consistent with disease progression and warrants non-stimulation-based interventions.

# An initial machine learning model applied to local field potential data from the subthalamic nucleus to detect freezing of gait in Parkinson’s disease

## Abstract
Freezing of gait (FOG) is an unpredictable and debilitating symptom of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Current approaches to treating FOG are lacking; adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) holds promise in treating FOG. However, a necessary precursor to using aDBS is understanding changes in the subthalamic nucleus (STN) prior to and during FOG. The aim of this project was to develop a machine learning model to detect FOG episodes using local field potential (LFP) data from the STN in individuals with PD. The LFP data were collected from five individuals with PD using the Medtronic Percept DBS platform while in the off-therapy state (off-DBS and off-medication) as they walked through virtual reality environments designed to induce FOG by manipulating levels of physical, anxiety, and cognitive load. The LFP time-series data were z-score normalized within each participant and canonical frequency band powers, rolling means, maxima, minima, and standard deviations were calculated. In addition, alpha-beta burst dynamics were calculated and included for analysis. The model was trained and subsequently tuned with a transfer learning component before testing its capacity to detect FOG episodes. The deep learning model achieved an average weighted F1 score of 0.67, a macro F1 score of 0.62 across eight trials from five participants and successfully detected 24/29 (80%) of FOG episodes. The predictive importance of the twelve LFP channels varied across participants and FOG triggers, underscoring the need for an individualized, adaptable approach to modelling FOG using only LFP data. Initial results indicate that FOG detection using LFP data gathered while walking is feasible. Future aDBS applications should contemplate a patient- and environmental-specific approach to optimize FOG treatment potential. clinicaltrials.gov, Identifier (NCT05103384). ## Introduction

Gait impairment is a hallmark symptom of Parkinson’s disease (PD). As the disease progresses, freezing of gait (FOG) becomes more prevalent. Frequently described as “having one’s feet glued to the floor”, FOG is associated with falls (1) and results in decreased quality of life (2). Unfortunately, FOG is difficult to treat. Medication aimed to prevent FOG becomes less effective as PD advances, and a subset of patients exhibiting FOG are unresponsive to dopaminergic medication (3). Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has limited and mixed impact on treating FOG (4–6) with several reports noting a worsening of FOG following DBS (7–9). Contributing to the gap in treating FOG is a lack of understanding the precise role of the basal ganglia in FOG. Advancements in DBS technology (10) provide an opportunity to directly monitor and understand patterns of neural activity within the subthalamic nucleus (STN) which is necessary to realize the potential to use adaptive DBS (aDBS) to effectively treat FOG.

The safety and potential value of aDBS in treating PD tremor, bradykinesia and rigidity are well documented (11–13). A personalized aDBS approach aims to deliver stimulation through a closed loop system that modulates DBS stimulation in response to patient-specific pathological activity detected through local field potential (LFP) recordings (11) in contrast to conventional DBS systems that deliver continuous stimulation at a fixed electrical amplitude. Although both forms of stimulation are presumed to improve PD motor symptoms through the suppression of oscillations in the beta band (13–30 Hz), aDBS has been shown to selectively target prolonged beta bursts (14) which has been correlated with greater PD disease severity, bradykinesia and rigidity (15, 16). In contrast to conventional DBS which broadly suppresses beta band activity, aDBS preserves short-duration beta bursts (14), now considered integral to normal motor function (17) and associated with improvements in overall disease state (15). These findings are supported by a recent systematic review and meta-analysis that concluded aDBS improved overall PD motor symptoms, specifically rigidity, bradykinesia, dyskinesia, and speech function comparably to conventional DBS, while using significantly less electrical stimulation (18). There was insufficient data to draw conclusions regarding aDBS and its effectiveness in treating gait dysfunction (18).

Preliminary reports suggest that the basal ganglia play a dynamic, modular role throughout the gait cycle (19) and during FOG (20–22). At rest, differences between beta activity in people with PD with and without FOG is mixed with some reporting an increase in beta activity in freezers compared to non-freezers (21, 23) and others reporting no differences in beta power or entropy between those with and without FOG (24). During movement, LFP signals appear to differ in freezers and non-freezers. During both cycling and walking, non-freezers displayed a power reduction throughout the beta band; freezers experienced a similar power reduction in the overall beta band; however, a slight power increase in a narrow band around 18 Hz was present with movement initiation (21). Singh reported a reduction of beta oscillations in non-freezers during walking compared to standing or sitting. By contrast, freezers had an increase of low beta oscillations during walking (20). During a stepping in place task, Syrkin–Nikoula reported lower beta power, but greater beta entropy in the STN in freezers compared to non-freezers (24). Others report enhanced STN beta and theta band synchronization in people with PD during FOG (25) and greater low beta and theta power LFP power during periods of vulnerable gait, or where the FOG index is high and FOG is likely to occur (26), suggesting that there may be a neural signal associated with FOG episodes. The preliminary results characterizing the neural signature underlying FOG provide rationale for monitoring and recording LFP data across a range of environmental stimuli to enhance the potential treatment of FOG using aDBS. The aim of this project was to determine if STN LFP data gathered during walking through a series of virtual environments designed to trigger FOG could be used to inform a machine learning model that could aid in the detection of FOG. Identifying an effective machine learning model approach using only STN LFP data has the potential to provide the cornerstone for the development of an effective aDBS approach to treating FOG.

## Materials and methods

Individuals with PD who previously (>3 months) underwent bilateral STN DBS surgery (Percept™ DBS, Medtronic, Inc.) were eligible for participation. Additional inclusion criteria were: the ability to ambulate with or without an assistive device for at least 10 min and a score of ≥1 on the Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (27). Exclusion criteria were documented clinical diagnosis of dementia, neurological disease other than PD including multiple sclerosis, stroke, and epilepsy, musculoskeletal issues that would prevent prolonged standing or ambulation, untreated mood disorder, active substance abuse, and uncorrected hearing or vision impairment. Participants completed the informed consent process approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board prior to data collection.

Data were gathered from five individuals with PD in the OFF-therapy condition defined as withholding antiparkinson medication >12 h and DBS off for ~1 h. Participants completed the Cleveland Clinic-Virtual Home Environment (CC-VHE) while LFP data were streamed and recorded from the electrodes in the STN. ### Cleveland clinic – virtual home environment (CC-VHE) paradigm

The CC-VHE platform combines an omni-directional treadmill with immersive virtual reality scenarios to create real-world scenarios known to provoke FOG. Each participant completed three CC-VHE modules, previously detailed (28). The modules were developed to operationalize theoretical models of FOG: (1) Physical (29), (2) Anxiety (30), and (3) Cognitive (31). Briefly, the Physical module changes the external characteristics of the environment including different flooring patterns, narrow doorways and hallways, and multiple turns. The Anxiety module aims to evoke a stress response by requiring the participant to walk on a narrow plank suspended high above the ground. Finally, the Cognitive module requires dual-tasking as the participant ambulates around a large kitchen island while responding to a picture interference task (i.e., identify whether the picture of the animal is congruent or incongruent with the text). The Physical and Anxiety modules required walking ~30 meters while the Cognitive module had a trial time of 2 min of continuous walking. The participants completed one trial of the Physical, Anxiety, and Cognitive modules.

### Local field potential recording

Bilateral STN LFP data were continuously recorded at 250 Hz using Indefinite Streaming Mode. Data from three channel pairs, LFP0–2, LFP0–3, LFP1–3 for each STN, were streamed wirelessly and stored on the programmer tablet (Model CT900D, Medtronic). The data were passed through built-in filters prior to streaming including a low pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 100 Hz, and a high pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 1 Hz (32). The filtered LFP magnitude (μV) in the time domain were downloaded as a JSON file for offline analysis in MATLAB version R2022b (MathWorks, Inc., Natick) and Python version 3.11.3.

### Data synchronization and freezing of gait detection

Our previously described methods of data synchronization ensured LFP and gait data were synchronized to within 50 ms (28). The methodology leveraged an electroencephalogram (EEG) system to synchronize the CC-VHE (i.e., FOG events) and LFP data. While EEG data were collected as part of a larger project, these data are not presented in this study as the focus of this manuscript is detecting FOG using only the LFP data. Briefly, the STN LFP and EEG data were synchronized using a commercially available TENS device (BioStim Plus; Biomedical Life Systems), which injected an electrical artifact into both data streams prior to the start of the module. A physical therapist (Board-Certified Clinical Specialist in Neurologic Physical Therapy with 15 + year of clinical and research experience with PD) operated the control computer to (1) initiate and terminate each trial and (2) to mark the start and stop times of each FOG episode in real-time (termed clinician-identified FOG events). The EEG system (LiveAmp 64; Brain Vision) recorded the two aforementioned events as well via a hardwired connection with the control computer. The FOG start and end timepoints recorded in the EEG files were confirmed post-hoc via video review by the same physical therapist. Specifically, the onset of FOG was defined as akinesia or trembling resulting in non-functional steps (33); the offset of FOG was defined as two functional steps. In addition, the timeseries data from shank IMUs were visually inspected to verify clinically identified FOG episodes. The timepoints of the FOG episodes were identified in the LFP data following temporal synchronization of the EEG and LFP data streams and utilized for subsequent analysis.

### Data feature engineering

Prior to modeling, features were derived and combined with individual participant LFP data. These data were then segmented to create input windows for model training and evaluation. All derived features were computed at each timepoint with preceding data, prior to segmentation into overlapping 3 second windows for input to the model.

#### Additional channels and Z-score normalization

Raw LFP data from the 6 channels from the DBS leads were linearly combined to calculate 6 additional channels, 3 per side: LFP0–1 = LFP0–3 − LFP1–3; LFP1–2 = LFP1–3 + LFP0–2 − LFP0–3; LFP2–3 = LFP0–3 − LFP0–2. The additional channels were included in the analysis as they provided greater spatial resolution and enhanced sensitivity to localized neural changes during FOG events compared with exported channels, which spanned a larger anatomical area. Z-score normalization was performed on all 12 channels of time-series LFP data for each participant across their three trials. Accurate lead placement in the sensorimotor region of the STN was inferred using improvements in MDS-UPDRS III scores; reported improvement of ~30%–50% have been reported following DBS (34, 35). The mean standard deviation (SD) MDS-UPDRS III score for participants in this study in the OFF-DBS/OFF-Med state was 53.2 (18.6) compared to 33.2 (17.8) points in the ON-DBS/ON-Med state, representing an average improvement of 38%.

#### Canonical frequency band powers

Power spectral densities were calculated using the Welch method (36) for the delta (1.0–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–100 Hz) bands using 500, 250, 125, 62, and 31 samples, respectively. All band-power estimates were derived from a sliding window consisting of data preceding each timepoint, with the window advancing one timepoint per step.

#### Custom frequency band powers

Power spectral densities (PSD) were calculated from at least 20 seconds of data collected while the participant was seated at rest prior to initiating CC-VHE modules and used to calculate participant-specific frequency bands. Specifically, PSDs were calculated using the Welch method with a 1 second Hanning window and 0.5 second overlap (36). A generalized power law (1/falpha) function was applied to each channel’s PSD to simulate the physiological baseline activity in the brain. Peak power values, maximum value above the 1/f baseline in the alpha-beta band (8–30 Hz), were selected from the PSDs. The frequency corresponding to the largest peak power across all 6 channels was identified per participant per hemisphere. Custom frequency bands of 6, 10, and 20 Hz bandwidth centered at the frequency of the peak power per hemisphere were then determined and used to calculate power with the same methods described above used for canonical band power calculation for all channels per trial.

#### Rolling features calculation

Rolling means, minima, maxima, and standard deviations were computed per timepoint on all features described in sections 2.4.1–2.4.3 using 0.125, 0.5, and 2 second sliding windows to highlight trends in LFP data leading up to and during changes in FOG state.

#### Alpha-beta burst and duration

Participant-specific, resting-state alpha-beta burst thresholds were determined using methods described by Neuville et al. (16) which provides a broader range of burst durations than approaches based on high power thresholds (17). Notably, burst activity was not restricted to the beta band; alpha-beta frequencies (8–30 Hz) were included, consistent with emerging evidence linking both alpha and beta burst activity to FOG (37). To calculate burst durations, the resting-state LFP was bandpass filtered to produce five datasets per trial; each corresponding to a consecutive 6 Hz bandwidth spanning 45–63 Hz. The filtered LFP data were squared. Local minima were then extracted via interpolation, and the median value was computed per dataset. For each participant and channel, the average of the five median values was multiplied by 4 and used as a baseline threshold to calculate alpha-beta burst durations in the LFP data acquired during the modules. Specifically, for each module, the peak power in the alpha-beta band (8–30 Hz) and corresponding peak frequency in the PSD was identified for each participant, hemisphere, and channel. The data were then bandpass filtered using a 6 Hz bandwidth centered on the peak frequency and then squared. The amplitude envelope was estimated using the local maxima. Bursts were identified when the envelope of the signal exceeded the resting-state baseline threshold. Average burst duration was calculated on a per module, per participant, per hemisphere, per channel basis.

The features described in sections 2.4.1–2.4.5 totaled 1,020 features, split evenly between the 12 LFP channels. For pairs of highly correlated features (R > 0.95), one feature of each pair was dropped at random, with 970 features remaining for modelling. Data were then processed into model input windows for training and evaluation of FOG state.

### Data processing

Data were processed into model input windows to support a deep learning model that predicts a timepoint’s binary state of FOG or non-FOG based on a short history of data immediately preceding it. Training the model involved passing in segmented windows of LFP data labeled with the binary outcome variable of FOG state of the final timepoint. The model also employs transfer learning (38), which is additional training, or tuning, of an existing model using labeled data closely related to the set to be predicted. Tuning assists an initial (or global) model that has already learned general features to refine its predictions in a specific (or local) setting. It is often used when there is a limited amount of closely related labeled data available for the target set.

Each timepoint was labeled FOG or non-FOG according to the confirmed FOG start and stop times. The time series was segmented into 3 second sliding windows, with 0.5 second steps as shown in Figure 2. Downstream predictions were made by classifying the final timepoint of each 3 second window as FOG or non-FOG to evaluate performance. Predictions were made independently of subsequent data, reflective of a real-world environment where future data is unknown.

[fig fig2] Clinician-identified FOG event, (red bar above the spectrogram) and corresponding input window labels (below the spectrogram). LFP data from a single participant are displayed in the spectrogram from the DBS channel with the greatest alpha-beta peak power. Data were processed into overlapping 3 second windows (orange and gray bars below spectrogram), each labeled according to the clinician-identified freeze state (red bar above spectrogram) at its final time point.

Data from each trial were truncated to include no more than 10 seconds of non-freeze prior to the first freeze and no more than 10 seconds of non-freeze following the final freeze for each trial to mitigate class imbalance. Class weights were also applied during model training to further address class imbalance.

LFP data were modeled and evaluated using leave one trial out cross validation (LOTOCV). For each LOTOCV iteration, one trial was held out from the data used to train an initial model. The held-out trial was partitioned into mutually exclusive tuning and testing sets; the first 40 seconds served as the tuning set while the remaining portion became the testing set used for performance evaluation. Held-out trials that did not result in at least one freeze in both their tuning and testing partitions after all processing and truncation were completed were excluded from modelling. The training set then consisted of (1) all remaining trials from the same participant as the held-out trial plus (2) all other participants’ trials from the same module as the held-out trial. The exclusion criteria ensured meaningful contributions from the trials included in the training and tuning sets, and interpretable results in the performance metrics from the held-out trials.

Tuning data were resampled with added noise to increase data set size prior to model tuning. Specifically, a Gaussian jitter with a standard deviation equal to 1% of the SD of each numeric feature was applied. Two augmented copies of the dataset were generated and concatenated with the original tuning data, ensuring sufficient variability for tuning without distorting the underlying time-series dynamics.

The test sets for the LOTOCV ranged from 33.5 to 77 seconds in length (Table 2), reflecting differences in overall trial duration and the amount of truncation necessary. In each LOTOCV iteration, these segments were evaluated for model performance using F1 score (an accepted measure of class-imbalanced model accuracy; range 0–1; higher values represent greater accuracy) to mitigate the impact of any remaining class imbalance on performance metrics.

### Model design

A deep learning model was designed to predict the final timepoint as FOG or non-FOG using the preceding 3 second window of time series LFP data and derived variables as input. Python packages Keras (version 3.6.0) and TensorFlow (version 2.16.2) were used. The model leverages a combination of several types of machine learning layers. The model architecture is designed using layers capable of capturing temporal dependencies and handling high-dimensional data, making it well-suited to learn meaningful patterns of FOG and non-FOG periods in neural data. A convolutional stack was chosen to learn local features across multiple time scales (39, 40). Positional encoding blocks assist the model to maintain sequence order and preserve temporal dependencies within the data. The cross-attention block was used because of its ability to focus on the portions of input sequences most relevant to prediction (41) and the ability to interpret temporal flow and understand global patterns in the data (42). Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) layers model time-series data (43) by allowing the flow of information in both forward and backward directions. Transformer blocks enhance the model’s long-range temporal reasoning (44). Shortcut connections of the convolutional stack connect local and broader global signals together. Finally, a temperature-scaled output enhanced the confidence of model predictions. Transfer learning was implemented to assist the model in learning the heterogenous neural signatures of FOG across unique individuals and unique FOG triggers. This technique improved performance by splitting model training into two parts: the first, where it learns general, high level trends during training on data from the entire cohort, and the second, where data from the beginning of the held-out trial to be predicted is input to fine-tune the model weights and optimize prediction capability for the specific test set.

Collectively, this modelling architecture implemented with transfer learning enabled the model to capture low- and high-level features required to successfully predict FOG using only LFP data across several individuals and FOG triggers.

### Model training

Data were trained for 100 epochs with a learning rate of 1×10−3 using an Adam optimizer and a binary focal cross entropy loss function with learning rate reduction on plateau and early stopping callbacks monitored by training loss and validation loss, respectively. Transfer learning was applied by freezing the first two layers of the model, reducing the learning rate to 5×10−6, and training the model for 100 additional epochs with data only from the held-out trial to fine-tune the model weights to the individual and FOG trigger in each iteration’s testing set. The same optimizer, loss function, and callbacks were used with the reduced learning rate during transfer learning.

### Feature importance

Deep learning models process large numbers of individual inputs in a complex, non-linear manner, making calculation and interpretation of individual feature importances challenging. In this data, one 3 second input window contains 970 input features at 750 timepoints, for a total of 727,500 inputs per window, which pass through several layers of additional feature extraction, pattern recognition, and regularization, before the model outputs a prediction of the probability that the window ends in a FOG event. A feature importance approach was selected that leverages estimates of Shapley values (45) to quantify the marginal contribution of each input to the model’s prediction, then sums the values from related inputs into feature groups that are more easily interpretable.

Shapley values were estimated using the SHAP package (46) which approximates the values using a gradient-based approach and a set of background reference samples (46, 47), which were pulled from the training set. For a given input window and output prediction, the sum of the 727,500 individual contributions, or local SHAP values, combined with the model’s expected baseline output, equals the final prediction for that window. SHAP values were grouped across all inputs from the same channel and plotted against raw feature values to provide a high-level view of how the model predictions relate to input values.

## Results

Five participants completed three modules each for a total of 15 completed trials. Of those 15 completed trials, 8 contained sufficient FOG to satisfy the requirements of at least one FOG each in the tuning and testing set and were therefore held out for one iteration of LOTOCV as the test set.

In total, the testing sets contained data from 30 FOG episodes (169 seconds) across all trials (Table 2). The model successfully detected 24 (80%) of the 30 freezes across the cohort with a specificity of 55%. A 0.5 prediction probability threshold was used to classify predictions as FOG or non-FOG. Successful detection of a FOG at the event level was defined as at least 25% of the predictions made per true FOG event at or above the chosen prediction probability threshold of 0.5. A 25% detection threshold was selected using ROC analysis by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1), subject to a minimum specificity constraint of ≥ 0.5 to ensure adequate classification of non-FOG events (48). This constraint was imposed to reflect the unequal costs of misclassification, with the minimum specificity determined by a clinician. The number of freezes containing at least one positive prediction within the duration of a FOG event was 26 (87%) of the 30 total freezes.

## Discussion

Results from this project support the feasibility of using LFP data to detect FOG events while walking through VR environments that replicate those in the real-world known to trigger FOG. Several findings from this study inform future LFP FOG detection machine learning algorithms. First, the environmental stimuli or FOG trigger appears to be an important factor in model accuracy. Unique environmental triggers (i.e., physical, anxiety, and cognitive) produced differing feature importance magnitude and directionality of channel feature values, suggesting a unique pattern of neural data between environmental triggers. Second, FOG-related changes in neural activity appear to vary across individuals. To address the importance of the environmental stimuli and individual signal, we developed individual models using custom metrics and transfer learning. Custom frequency bands were defined to capture each participant’s peak power rather than relying on uniform canonical bands, as diverse neural signatures limit the comparability of standard power metrics. The patient-specific bands enable the model to directly compare peak power across individuals. Leveraging comprehensive LFP frequency bands, not just beta, is consistent with previous findings related to the development of a neural network model to predict weight shifting during a rhythmic stepping in place activity in PD (49). This data set provides an initial signal that FOG events produce a highly individualized, stimuli-specific neural signature.

Transfer learning was used to personalize predictions and increase performance beyond the base models trained on data from multiple participants and FOG triggers. The LFP data from five participants was used to create individual base models. Although each base model captured mild signal, they lacked the precision necessary for accurate, individual predictions. The base models were tuned on a single trial’s dataset to create personalized, trigger-specific LFP FOG detection. Marked improvement in predictive performance across all participants and all FOG triggers was present after fine-tuning model weights during transfer learning (0.57 vs. 0.67 weighted F1 score), underscoring the diversity of neural signatures across participants and triggers and highlighting the need for an individualized modelling approach in the absence of lower extremity kinematic and accelerometer data. The transfer learning approach is a feasible model for clinical care, as a general model trained on a large dataset of FOG events could be used as a base model for all patients, with transfer learning to the individual occurring at the same time as the initial DBS programming session.

Currently, the gold-standard of identifying FOG is through clinician assessment of gait behavior; this approach may be prone to error. Individual FOG patterns can present as akinetic, trembling (knees or lower shank), and festinating (small, non-functional steps) (33, 50), which makes visual FOG detection challenging and subjective. Despite recent work to standardize the definition and features of FOG (33), variability remains due to the nature and temporal occurrence of FOG presentations which can compromise intra- and inter-rater consistency. In the present study, model performance was evaluated using FOG episodes in which alignment between clinician-identified and model-identified FOG were present, resulting in conservative model performance estimates. For example, in Figure 3, the model-identified FOG preceding both freezes are scored as false positives, despite representing clinically advantageous early detection. Similarly, false positives following the first freeze and false negatives following the second freeze in Figure 3 weaken performance metrics regardless of their minimal practical impact in a real-world environment. Greater standardization through the consensus definition and utilizing biomechanical data to objectively identifying FOG will improve accuracy and reliability of subsequent FOG detection models.

This project included extensive walking and freezing data in multiple realistic virtual environments from a relatively small number of participants, resulting in multiple personalized initial and tuned models. The protocol validated that the CC-VHE platform is an effective tool for collecting FOG data in a clinical setting, reliably inducing FOG from multiple triggers. The success in data collection and personalized modeling supports the pursuit of a broader data collection effort. With additional subjects, a single base model could be trained on a comprehensive dataset across many individuals and FOG triggers, eliminating the need for individualized base models. Further exploration of neural signatures associated with FOG triggers within a specific module (i.e., turning vs. narrow hallway in the Physical module) will be possible with a larger dataset. Refinement of the machine learning model, including adding aperiodic components (49), additional high-quality data, and the implementation of a single generalized base model will likely improve and refine performance.

Current on-demand FOG detection models predominately utilize external sensors integrated with other technologies to deliver an intervention, such as auditory, visual, or sensory cues (51). On-demand cuing technologies may be effective in reducing FOG (51); however, there appears to be no carryover when the device is removed (52) and implementation barriers such as external device wear compliance, comfort, appearance, technology failure, and charging requirements limit adoption. Adaptive DBS can alter stimulation in response to a detected electrical stimuli, thus the potential for disrupting a pathological FOG signal and preventing FOG. Our preliminary data demonstrate that FOG detection is feasible and that consideration of individual LFP data and the FOG trigger is paramount. While preliminary, the modelling approach provides rationale for moving forward with continued investigation into aDBS to detect and potentially treat FOG.

# Deciphering Freezing of Gait: What Neuropathology Reveals About an Episodic Phenomenon

## Abstract
Freezing of gait (FoG) is an episodic, debilitating motor phenomenon defined as paroxysmal episodes wherein there is an inability to step effectively, despite attempting to do so. Although FoG is most commonly associated with Parkinson's disease, it manifests across a spectrum of progressive neurodegenerative and potentially transient non‐neurodegenerative conditions that affect overlapping locomotor circuits. The episodic nature of FoG implies transient disruptions of a distributed locomotor network rather than fixed structural lesions, rendering its neuropathological basis particularly challenging to define. This review synthesizes current knowledge of the structural, neurochemical and proteinopathic substrates that precondition and precipitate FoG. The principal disease categories implicated in FoG are reviewed, including Lewy body diseases, progressive supranuclear palsy, multiple system atrophy, corticobasal degeneration and argyrophilic grain disease, as well as non‐neurodegenerative conditions such as cerebrovascular disease and metabolic encephalopathy. Mixed and overlapping pathologies are common. Across these disorders, different disease stages and phases, and their frequent combinations, interact with selective vulnerability of distinct neuronal and glial populations, giving rise to a complex pathophysiological landscape. It is proposed that this convergence results in heightened network susceptibility, whereby cumulative damage to cortical, subcortical and brainstem locomotor nodes progressively lowers the threshold for episodic circuit failure. Understanding these multilevel neuropathological determinants is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at stabilizing the locomotor network and reducing FoG burden.

## Introduction

Freezing of gait (FoG) is one of the most disabling and enigmatic manifestations of a neurological phenomenon. The 2025 ICFOG definition describes FOG as “paroxysmal episodes wherein there is an inability to step effectively, despite attempting to do so” (Gilat et al. 2026). While its prevalence in advanced Parkinson's disease (PD) has long been recognized, FoG is increasingly recognized in other conditions, arising whenever specific nodes of the locomotor network are sufficiently compromised. Unlike fixed motor deficits, FoG fluctuates in relation to environmental triggers, attentional demands, emotional stress and pharmacological state (Nutt et al. 2011). This implies that FoG does not reflect irreversible structural destruction alone but rather represents a dynamic collapse of network synchrony in a brain rendered susceptible by underlying structural damage. The concept of ‘locomotor network vulnerability’ provides the conceptual framework for understanding why FoG emerges episodically rather than persistently (Weiss et al. 2020).

From a neuropathological perspective, this framework presents a fundamental challenge. Neuropathology excels at identifying fixed structural lesions and protein aggregates, yet FoG, by definition, reflects transient dysfunction rather than permanent ablation. Establishing causal neuropathological correlates therefore requires distinguishing lesions that precondition the network, creating vulnerability, from those that are merely coincidental findings. This distinction is rarely straightforward, and the field has not yet resolved whether specific regional pathologies are causally linked to FoG, or whether the network as a whole must reach a threshold of cumulative damage before episodic freezing can occur. Indeed, the heterogeneous risk profile of FoG underscores the multidimensional nature of pathophysiology and indicates that no single neurotransmitter system or anatomical structure accounts for its emergence.

This review examines the neuropathological basis of FoG from two complementary angles: first, by surveying the diseases—both neurodegenerative and non‐neurodegenerative—that damage brain regions relevant to FoG; second, by exploring how the combination of diseases leads to the degeneration of the locomotor network and its multiple neurotransmitter systems creates the conditions for episodic gait failure. The review concludes by integrating these structural, proteinopathic and neurochemical mechanisms into a unified model through the concepts of mixed pathology and network susceptibility.

## Neuroanatomy and Neurotransmitter Systems Underlying FoG

Effective gait requires the dynamic integration of cortical executive control, subcortical motor programming and automatic brainstem and spinal rhythm generation (Nutt et al. 2011). The locomotor network responsible for this integration is anatomically distributed and functionally hierarchical. Cortical signals descend through corticostriatal and corticoreticular pathways to engage the basal ganglia loop, the midbrain locomotor region (MLR), comprising the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN), cuneiform and subcuneiform nucleus, and the pontomedullary reticular formation (PMRF), which ultimately drives spinal central pattern generators (CPGs) responsible for rhythmic locomotor output (Nutt et al. 2011). Based on functional neuroimaging studies, walking engages a distributed network of cortical regions involved in motor planning, execution, sensory integration and visuospatial processing. In addition, cortical areas such as the supplementary motor area and lateral premotor cortex, and further brain regions including medial primary sensorimotor cortex, cingulate cortex, parahippocampal cortex, superior parietal lobe and occipital cortex are also considered (Nutt et al. 2011).

Neuroimaging studies reveal that FoG arises from distributed network dysfunction rather than a single lesion site (Fasano et al. 2015). Atrophy and hypometabolism affecting frontal regions impair motor planning and executive control, while parietal and posterior cortical regions show changes linked to deficits in visuospatial processing and navigation. Subcortically, the caudate nucleus, putamen and thalamus demonstrate denervation and volume loss, disrupting movement initiation and sensorimotor relay. At the brainstem level, atrophy and reduced connectivity compromise gait initiation and locomotor control, and cerebellar under‐activation further impairs rhythm and balance (Fasano et al. 2015). Indeed, based on lesion network mapping, lesions that cause FoG converge on a shared functional network connected to the cerebellar locomotor region (Fasano et al. 2017). Collectively, FoG reflects a failure of connectivity across cortical executive‐attention networks and subcortical locomotor circuits, with this widespread network disruption underlying its characteristic episodic presentation. Furthermore, comorbid depression and anxiety as risk factors in FoG (Weiss et al. 2020) underscore the importance of limbic system involvement, particularly the amygdala, in linking neuropathological changes to this episodic motor phenomenon. Finally, experimental models show that lesions of the ventral periaqueductal grey (PAG) attenuate freezing behaviour, whereas lesions of the dorsolateral PAG exacerbate freezing (De Oca et al. 1998).

Regarding neurotransmitters, the basal ganglia, receiving dopaminergic input from the substantia nigra, modulate cortical motor programs through direct and indirect pathway signalling. Dopaminergic depletion, the hallmark of PD, disrupts this modulation and is the principal target of current pharmacological therapy. However, the incomplete and often paradoxical responsiveness of FoG to levodopa indicates that the dopaminergic system alone cannot account for its pathophysiology (Nutt et al. 2011). Cholinergic neurons of the PPN, particularly the pars compacta, play a key role in gait initiation and postural control, while the pars dissipata also contains glutamatergic, GABAergic and monoaminergic neurons. Glutamate provides the main excitatory drive from the cortex and brainstem locomotor regions to spinal CPGs. In contrast, GABAergic output from the globus pallidus interna tonically inhibits the mesencephalic locomotor region, with disinhibition promoting locomotion. Noradrenaline and serotonin further modulate descending brainstem–spinal pathways, supporting gait rhythm, tone and adaptability. FoG thus reflects a systems‐level failure of this multi‐transmitter architecture rather than an isolated dopaminergic deficit.

Another neurological phenomenon is festination. Festination is distinct from FoG but shows partial overlap: Type I festination (often termed ‘festinating‐freezing’) is closely related to FoG and, by consensus, is considered a manifestation of FoG, whereas Type II festination represents a separate, balance‐related gait disturbance and should be classified as a festination‐related impairment rather than FoG (Gilat et al. 2026). Although neuropathological evidence on festination is limited, neuroimaging studies suggest that cognitive motor control, postural regulation, memory and emotional factors appear to be closely linked to festination in PD (Lee et al. 2025). The absence of a clear association with striatal dopaminergic activity points to the contribution of extranigral mechanisms beyond primary dopaminergic dysfunction (Lee et al. 2025).

### Non‐Neurodegenerative Causes

A clinically important category of FoG‐associated pathology comprises non‐neurodegenerative conditions that structurally or functionally compromise locomotor circuitry. These conditions are of particular relevance because they may produce FoG‐like disturbances in the absence of neurodegeneration and because some are at least partially reversible, providing natural experiments for understanding the anatomical requirements for FoG generation.

Cerebrovascular disease is among the documented non‐neurodegenerative causes of FoG. Ischemic strokes involving bilateral PPN (Kuo et al. 2008) can produce gait freezing likely by directly interrupting the MLR. Three patients with isolated dorsal midbrain infarcts presented with prominent gait hesitation and irregular, ataxic gait without parkinsonism or peripheral cerebellar dysfunction; all had associated oculomotor abnormalities, including third nerve palsies and/or vertical gaze deficits (Hathout and Bhidayasiri 2005). A haemorrhage at the pontomesencephalic junction involving the right pedunculopontine area rendered an 83‐year‐old woman unable to stand or initiate stepping movements (Masdeu et al. 1994). Lacunar infarcts in the basal ganglia, thalamus or periventricular white matter can similarly disrupt cortico‐subcortical locomotor loops without producing the classical parkinsonism of PD. Similarly, cortical venous thrombosis has been reported to lead to reversible parkinsonism, altered consciousness and progressive gait slowing (Jenkins et al. 2001). Right hemorrhagic stroke of the supplementary motor area was also associated with injury with gait apraxia including FoG (Yada and Kawasaki 2022). Furthermore, chronic subdural hematoma (Noda et al. 2017) represents additional vascular mechanisms capable of producing episodic or persistent gait dysfunction by compromising cortical motor and supplementary motor areas or their efferent pathways.

Idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) constitutes another cause, in which gait disturbance arises from distortion of periventricular frontal pathways subserving locomotor control (Giladi et al. 1997). The reversibility of gait impairment with cerebrospinal fluid diversion in NPH underscores the functional nature of the underlying network disruption in these cases. Metabolic encephalopathies, including haemodialysis (Nakajima et al. 2013) and liver disease‐associated (Sousa et al. 2019) metabolic syndrome, represent a further category, which can produce transient gait abnormalities without fixed structural lesions. While the former was thought to be associated with vasogenic edema on bilateral basal ganglia associated with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, in the latter, the role of ammonia was discussed. Additional non‐neurodegenerative causes documented in neuroimaging and clinical series include neoplastic lesions (metastatic disease and lymphoma), carbon monoxide toxicity (Lee et al. 2010) that preferably damages the globus pallidus, and post‐surgical states affecting basal ganglia or brainstem structures (Nutt et al. 2011).

Although the clinical course and symptom constellations differ in these conditions from FoG associated with neurodegenerative diseases, some similarity in gait disturbances supports the notion that these brain regions are critical to the pathogenesis of FoG. These non‐neurodegenerative aetiologies demonstrate that FoG can arise from focal or diffuse disruption of specific network nodes without underlying neurodegeneration or neuronal loss, directly validating the network vulnerability model. They also carry important diagnostic implications: FoG of vascular, metabolic or structural origin may respond to entirely different interventions than those effective in neurodegenerative conditions such as PD, making etiological distinction a clinically meaningful step in management.

### Neurodegenerative Diseases: Fundamentals and Relevance for FoG

Neurodegenerative diseases are clinically and pathologically diverse conditions characterized by progressive neuronal dysfunction and loss, commonly beginning with synaptic disruption before overt cell death becomes apparent (Forrest and Kovacs 2025). The modern conceptual framework of ‘proteinopathy’ recognizes that each major neurodegenerative disease is defined by the characteristic accumulation of specific misfolded proteins in filamentous aggregates within neurons, astrocytes or oligodendroglia. These proteinopathic deposits exhibit distinct biochemical properties, structural strains, stereotypical anatomical staging patterns and associations with specific clinical phenotypes (Forrest and Kovacs 2025).

The pathogenesis of these disorders involves misfolding of specific proteins that can propagate through connected brain regions in staged patterns, acquire toxic properties, and form aggregates that sequester other proteins, impair proteostasis, disrupt RNA processing, associate with loss‐of‐function of proteins, and activate neuroinflammatory cascades (Forrest and Kovacs 2025). Classification of neurodegenerative diseases reflects their presumed aetiologies (idiopathic, genetic or acquired), their anatomical patterns of neuronal loss and protein deposition, and the biochemical and structural properties of their predominant misfolded proteins (Forrest and Kovacs 2025). Understanding these levels of classification is essential when interpreting how specific pathologies affect FoG‐relevant circuitry.

Neurodegenerative disorders associated with FoG are predominantly from the group of diseases classified as α‐synucleinopathies and tauopathies. Within the α‐synucleinopathies, PD and multiple system atrophy (MSA) are of primary relevance, whereas the tauopathies most commonly linked to FoG include progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and corticobasal degeneration (CBD). Among these conditions, PD and PSP are most frequently associated with FoG, reflecting their prominent involvement in basal ganglia–brainstem locomotor networks. FoG has been reported in less common neurodegenerative diseases, including the tauopathy argyrophilic grain disease (AGD) and certain lysosomal storage disorders, such as neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis.

### The Concept of Mixed Pathologies

Neurodegenerative diseases have traditionally been classified by their predominant misfolded protein; however, neuropathological and biomarker evidence increasingly challenges this single‐protein paradigm (Forrest and Kovacs 2023). Many individuals simultaneously harbour multiple misfolded proteins—including amyloid‐β, tau, α‐synuclein and TDP‐43—forming an individualized ‘protein code’ that shapes prognosis and therapeutic response in ways that single‐target models cannot capture (Kovacs et al. 2010). These co‐pathologies are not merely incidental: they interact, influencing each other's aggregation kinetics and sequestering soluble, physiologically active protein forms, thereby altering their availability and detectability in cerebrospinal fluid and plasma. Where deposits overlap anatomically, such interactions may modify the clinical phenotype beyond what any single proteinopathy would produce in isolation. Cerebrovascular disease and systemic disorders add a further layer of complexity when they co‐occur with proteinopathies (Forrest and Kovacs 2023). Capturing this biological reality requires integrating biomarkers of protein aggregation with markers of neuroinflammation, neuronal injury, genetic risk and vascular pathology—stratifying patients by their combined pathological burden rather than their dominant clinical diagnosis (Kovacs et al. 2010). This approach holds the potential to explain past trial failures, identify distinct progression trajectories and guide the development of personalized, combination‐aware therapies.

## Major Neurodegenerative Diseases Affecting FoG‐Relevant Brain Regions

### PD and Lewy Body Diseases (LBDs)

PD is the paradigmatic and most extensively studied neurodegenerative disease associated with FoG. It is characterized by the progressive aggregation of α‐synuclein into Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites, spreading in a stereotypical caudo‐rostral pattern (Braak et al. 2003). PD belongs to the broader group of LBD—which also includes PD dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies (Fu and Halliday 2025)—all of which share a common α‐synuclein filament fold structure, as revealed by cryogenic electron microscopy (Yang et al. 2022). Braak staging captures the sequential anatomical progression of this pathology: early stages are confined to the olfactory bulb, dorsal vagal nucleus and lower brainstem; intermediate stages involve the substantia nigra pars compacta, locus coeruleus (LC) and raphe nuclei; and later stages reach limbic and neocortical regions integral to gait control and executive function (Braak et al. 2003).

Recent studies challenge the notion of a uniform pattern of LBD pathology to include body‐first and brain‐first patterns and extend its cytopathological features beyond neurons (Andersen et al. 2025). Notably, many antibodies used in early neuropathological studies failed to detect α‐synuclein pathology in glial cells. Subsequent methodological advances have revealed prominent astroglial α‐synuclein pathology, particularly in the striatum from Braak stage 3 onward, as well as in the amygdala and cortical regions at later stages (Kovacs et al. 2014; Altay et al. 2022). In addition, bulky oligodendroglial inclusions are observed in Braak Stage 4 and advanced disease, most prominently within the pallido‐thalamic tract and also in the substantia nigra (Rohan et al. 2016). Together, these findings underscore a significant role for glial involvement in PD pathogenesis and highlight glial dysfunction as a potentially relevant contributor to the mechanisms underlying FoG. These aspects have recently been integrated into a staging system that accounts for protein pathology across different affected cell types (Otero‐Jimenez et al. 2025).

The clinical hallmarks of PD, bradykinesia, rigidity and tremor, principally reflect dopaminergic degeneration of the substantia nigra. FoG is more associated with the non‐tremor dominant forms of PD (Weiss et al. 2020). The tremor‐dominant form of PD differs, for example, from the akinetic rigid form most notably in the regional pattern of substantia nigra degeneration, the latter showing greater overall nigral cell loss concentrated ventrolaterally, while the former shows less total loss but more medial nigral and retrorubral A8 damage (Boonstra et al. 2022). At a broader level, akinetic rigid form carries heavier cortical Lewy body and amyloid burden with more diverse co‐pathologies (Boonstra et al. 2022). Molecularly, widespread neuronal mitochondrial complex I deficiency strongly predicts non‐tremor dominant phenotypes, suggesting the two subtypes reflect fundamentally different neurodegenerative processes rather than different disease stages (Flones et al. 2024).

FoG in PD correlates more strongly with extranigral pathology (Weiss et al. 2020). This multi‐system involvement explains the frequent incompleteness of levodopa responses in FoG and supports combination therapeutic approaches targeting non‐dopaminergic transmitter systems. Importantly, FoG appears later in the disease course in PD, associated with higher Hoehn and Yahr stage and longer duration of levodopa treatment (Giladi et al. 1992). Risk factors for the future development of FoG in PD span motor, non‐motor, imaging and genetic domains. Non‐modifiable factors include older age at disease onset, longer disease duration, lower dopamine transporter uptake in the caudate and putamen, presence of the APOE ε4 allele, and reduced CSF Aβ42 levels (Herman et al. 2023). Among modifiable factors, greater motor severity—particularly the postural instability and gait difficulty subtype—alongside depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment and higher levodopa equivalent daily doses are the strongest predictors (Herman et al. 2023). Non‐motor features, including autonomic dysfunction, sleep disturbance and hyposmia, further contribute to risk, while neuroimaging evidence of white matter hyperintensities increases FoG risk more than threefold (Herman et al. 2023). Collectively, this multidimensional risk profile reinforces the view that FoG emerges from the convergence of multiple pathological processes rather than any single causal factor. It must be noted that LBD can be rarely the morphological substrate of pure akinesia with gait freezing (PAGF) (Williams et al. 2007), which is mostly associated with PSP pathology. Interestingly, the case reported in that study (Williams et al. 2007) had also occasional neuronal and glial tau inclusions in the pontine base, STN, striatum, thalamus and substantia nigra, which would be currently suggestive of a concomitant early‐stage PSP pathology (Kovacs et al. 2020).

### MSA

MSA is an α‐synucleinopathy that is pathologically distinguished from LBDs by the predominant accumulation of α‐synuclein in oligodendroglial cytoplasmic inclusions—termed Papp‐Lantos bodies (Gilman et al. 2008). However, neuronal cytoplasmic and nuclear inclusions constitute an important feature of the disease (Wiseman et al. 2025). MSA presents in two principal clinical subtypes: MSA‐P (parkinsonian variant), in which striatonigral degeneration dominates, and MSA‐C (cerebellar variant), in which olivopontocerebellar atrophy is the principal substrate. A lesser‐known variant, atypical MSA (also termed frontotemporal lobar degeneration‐synuclein), presents initially with frontotemporal dementia‐like symptoms before evolving into classical MSA clinical features (Aoki et al. 2015). Neuropathologically, it is distinguished by prominent neuronal α‐synuclein pathology in the limbic system superimposed on the classical oligodendroglial inclusions of subcortical regions (Rohan et al. 2015). Notably, while the α‐synuclein filament fold in typical MSA differs from that seen in LBDs (Schweighauser et al. 2020), atypical MSA harbours a hybrid Lewy–MSA fold specifically within the limbic system, suggesting that variability of cellular protein pathologies, filament folds, can be associated with distinct clinical phenotypes (Enomoto et al. 2025).

In MSA‐P, degeneration of putamen, caudate, globus pallidus and substantia nigra disrupts the corticostriatal and nigrostriatal circuits central to locomotor programming. α‐Synuclein pathology is thought to begin simultaneously across the striatum, lentiform nucleus, substantia nigra, brainstem white matter tracts, cerebellar subcortical white matter, and motor, midfrontal and sensory cortices (Phase 1), subsequently spreading to the spinal cord and thalamus (Phase 2), then to the hippocampus and amygdala (Phase 3), and finally reaching the visual cortex (Phase 4) (Brettschneider et al. 2018).

In MSA‐C, cerebellar and brainstem involvement—including pontine nuclei, inferior olivary complex and cerebellar Purkinje cells—disrupts the cerebellar contribution to gait. A‐synuclein pathology is thought to progress through four sequential phases: beginning in the cerebellum and cerebellar‐brainstem connections (Phase 1), spreading to pyramidal and extrapyramidal white matter (Phase 2), then involving neocortical and basal ganglia grey matter (Phase 3) and finally reaching the amygdala and hippocampus (Phase 4) (Brettschneider et al. 2017).

FoG is considered an additional feature in MSA (Poewe et al. 2022). In a clinicopathological study of 15 patients with pathologically confirmed MSA, the median age at onset was 56 years and median survival was about 6.5 years. FoG was present in 40% of patients at the first clinical visit and increased to 54% by the final visit, indicating progression with disease duration (Muller et al. 2002). Restricted neuropathological examination did not identify a substrate for FoG. In other pathology‐based studies, FoG was not reported as a leading symptom (Wenning et al. 1997).

### PSP

PSP is a main form of tauopathies characterized by the accumulation of misfolded tau composed of 4‐repeat (4R) isoforms of tau. The Movement Disorders Society criteria (Hoglinger et al. 2017) distinguish eight clinical phenotypes, Richardson syndrome being the most frequent. Further subcortical phenotypes such as PSP‐parkinsonism (PSP‐P) and PSP with progressive gait freezing (PSP‐PGF) follow a slower course with longer survival, whereas cortical phenotypes—including PSP‐frontal (PSP‐F), PSP‐speech/language variant (PSP‐SL), and PSP‐corticobasal syndrome (PSP‐CBS)—present with more rapid decline.

Misfolded tau accumulates in characteristic morphological forms—subcortical neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), tufted astrocytes and oligodendroglial coiled bodies—following a hierarchical staging pattern that preferentially and early affects subcortical and brainstem nuclei integral to locomotion (Kovacs et al. 2020). Early‐stage PSP pathology is prominent in the globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and striatum, with subsequent extension to frontal cortex and dentate nucleus of the cerebellum, and eventual involvement of occipital regions in the most advanced cases (Kovacs et al. 2020). Studies have shown intra‐disease variability in PSP, supported by recent findings of differences in high–molecular weight tau assemblies and seeding activity across cases (Martinez‐Valbuena et al. 2025). PSP clinical variants further demonstrate heterogeneity in cortical vulnerability and in the cellular composition of tau pathology, involving neurons, astrocytes or oligodendrocytes (Kovacs et al. 2020).

Neuronal tau pathology in the midbrain, including the substantia nigra, superior colliculus and PAG, and in the subthalamic nucleus is particularly relevant to FoG in PSP. These structures constitute critical nodes of the locomotor network: the subthalamic nucleus modulates basal ganglia output via the indirect pathway, while midbrain structures govern postural reflexes and vertical gaze control. PSP also demonstrates a profound multi‐transmitter profile likely contributing to the early onset, severity and levodopa‐unresponsiveness of FoG and postural instability in PSP.

While FoG is frequently present as a symptom in PSP, the concept of primary progressive freezing gait (PPFG), introduced in 1993 (Achiron et al. 1993), along with the term gait ignition failure (Atchison et al. 1993), and reported in earlier studies as isolated or pure akinesia (summarized by Compta et al. 2007), requires special attention. Finally, the 2017 Movement Disorders Society criteria recognized progressive gait freezing (PSP‐PGF) as a distinct clinical phenotype (Hoglinger et al. 2017). Based on the criteria, probable PSP‐PGF requires progressive gait freezing within 3 years plus either vertical supranuclear gaze palsy or slow vertical saccades, while possible PSP‐PGF requires progressive gait freezing within 3 years alone, without the ocular motor features. While there are a few neuropathological studies on PSP‐PGF, systematic mapping of different cytopathologies as compared with all other clinical phenotypes are lacking. One study describing six cases of PAGF reported a milder degree of PSP pathology and noted several atypical pathological features (Williams et al. 2007). In particular, the distribution of pathology differed from that seen in classic PSP, with relative sparing of the pontine base. These aligns with the unpublished experience of the author of the present article, while ADNC as co‐pathology might be present in some cases. Apart from PAGF, an earlier study on a cohort of 24 patients with PSP, FoG was present in 25% at initial evaluation and increased to 53% by the final visit, becoming more common with disease progression (Muller et al. 2002).

Similar patterns of restricted pathology have been reported in cases with pallido–nigro‐luysian atrophy (PNLA); however, not all such cases present with primary FoG, and many exhibit other clinical features of PSP (Ahmed et al. 2008). More recently, a small cohort of patients with PSP with a markedly protracted disease course has been described, characterized by long intervals between major disease milestones, although FoG was not the dominant clinical feature (Couto et al. 2022). Neuropathologically, these cases showed only very mild alterations along the pallido–nigro‐luysian axis, with mild to moderate neuronal loss, suggesting a relatively benign molecular behaviour of misfolded tau. These cases differ from previously reported PSP phenotypes with long disease duration (Lukic et al. 2022), where cytopathological differences have been observed, but not in the restricted pattern described in protracted‐course PSP (Couto et al. 2022). Together, these observations suggest that early tau molecular behaviour and selective vulnerability of specific microcircuits may strongly influence both clinical presentation and disease progression. Introduction of a 4R tau seeding amplification assay from cervical skin will facilitate in vivo diagnosis of PSP (Martinez‐Valbuena et al. 2024), which, together with biomarker possibilities to exclude synuclein (Martinez‐Valbuena et al. 2026) and ADNC type co‐pathologies, will facilitate better understanding of disease progression and pathogenesis in clinical cohorts.

### CBD

CBD is also a major 4R tauopathy, distinguished neuropathologically from PSP by its characteristic astrocytic plaques, tau‐positive threads and coiled bodies within white matter, alongside cortical neuronal loss with ballooned neurons and tau inclusions in the frontoparietal cortex. CBD is therefore a neuropathological diagnosis. CBS refers to a characteristic clinical presentation that may be associated with CBD pathology (Saranza et al. 2019). The clinical phenotype is determined by the regional distribution of neuronal loss and tau lesions. Consequently, CBD pathology can give rise to a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, and conversely, CBS may result from diverse underlying neuropathologies (Saranza et al. 2019). CBD shares with PSP an early predilection for basal ganglia and brainstem nuclei, disrupting motor circuits, but its greater cortical involvement—particularly of the motor and premotor cortex, supplementary motor area and parietal regions—contributes to the additional clinical features of apraxia, alien limb phenomenon and cortical sensory loss (Saranza et al. 2019).

Studies of incidental CBD cases suggest that cortical astrocytic tau pathology precedes neuronal tau accumulation, representing an early pathogenic event (Ling et al. 2016). This is supported by symptomatic CBD cases in which astrocytic tau deposition occurs in regions lacking neuronal tau pathology (Kovacs et al. 2018). CBD shows a staged progression of astrocytic tau pathology, spreading from frontal–parietal to temporal–occipital cortex, then to subcortical regions and brainstem, accompanied by intracellular maturation from fine granular deposits in distal astrocytic processes to more advanced tau accumulations (Kovacs et al. 2017). A staging system that integrates neuronal and glial tau pathology and clinical phenotypic variability has not yet been developed. The neuropathological tau load in CBD correlates with disease severity and progression. Emerging evidence points to tau strain heterogeneity (Sanders et al. 2014) as a potential determinant of regional and cytopathological variability, exemplified by the predominance of neuronal tau pathology in the malignant form of CBD (Ling et al. 2020). While gait disturbance and postural instability are recognized features of CBD, the specific neuropathological determinants of FoG in this condition remain less thoroughly characterized than in PSP or PD, partly because CBD is rarer and its clinical and pathological heterogeneity is considerable. In a cohort of 13 patients with CBD, FoG was uncommon, affecting 8% at first presentation and 25% by the final visit, remaining less frequent than in other atypical parkinsonian disorders. This low prevalence may partly reflect the diagnostic difficulty in distinguishing FoG from apraxia, and no consistent pathological correlate was identified (Muller et al. 2002).

### Differential Neurotransmitter Degeneration in Parkinsonian Disorders: Insights From Postmortem Studies Relevant to FoG

While developments in neuroimaging clearly advanced the spectrum of neurotransmitter alterations (Kanel et al. 2022), it is worth returning to archival literature on postmortem studies that collectively paint a picture of overlapping yet disease‐specific neurodegeneration across the neurotransmitter systems in parkinsonian disorders.

Regarding the serotonergic raphe system, a clear dissociation between oral and caudal raphe nuclei was demonstrated (Kovacs et al. 2003): tryptophan hydroxylase‐positive (TpOH+) neurons were significantly reduced in the nucleus centralis superior (NCS) in PD, pointing to oral raphe vulnerability, while the nucleus raphe obscurus‐pallidus (NROP) showed a paradoxical increase in TpOH+ neuron proportion in PSP and MSA, suggesting either compensatory upregulation or preferential loss of non‐serotonergic neurons in the caudal raphe (Kovacs et al. 2003). Preservation of the oral raphe was confirmed by others focusing on the dorsal raphe nucleus (Eser et al. 2018). The increased proportion of TpOH+ neurons in the NROP in PSP and MSA most likely reflects preferential loss of non‐serotonergic raphe neurons rather than a true increase in serotonergic cells, shifting the balance toward serotonergic dominance in this nucleus (Kovacs et al. 2003). Since the NROP projects descending pathways preferentially to axial motoneurons and participates in locomotor rhythm production and supraspinal reflex modulation, this imbalance might eventually contribute to the axial rigidity and postural instability that define both conditions. The disproportionate serotonergic drive onto axial motor pools, normally counterbalanced by non‐serotonergic raphe neurons, may produce dysregulated tonic motor facilitation, potentially underpinning freezing episodes and gait dysfunction. Notably, PSP and MSA share a common pattern: both exhibits disproportionately heavier protein pathology in non‐raphe brainstem regions, independent of whether the aggregating protein is tau or α‐synuclein (Kovacs et al. 2003). It remains unclear, however, whether protein pathology selectively involves the non‐serotonergic cells in the NROP. Similarly to this investigation, MSA stood out strikingly in another study, which found sparing of rostral raphe serotonergic neurons (dorsal and median raphe) despite severe cholinergic depletion (Benarroch et al. 2002), contrasting with PD, where rostral raphe neuron loss has been documented.

The cholinergic PPN emerges as the most consistently vulnerable structure across diseases, but with differing severity. It was demonstrated that PPN neurons were reduced to approximately 43% of control values in PD and to 21% in PSP (Hirsch et al. 1987), establishing PSP as having the more severe and consistent cholinergic brainstem deficit. These findings were corroborated in another study reporting over 50% neuronal loss in PD and approximately 60% in PSP, with NFTs affecting 40%–64% of remaining PPN neurons in PSP versus bodies in 6%–39% in PD (Jellinger 1988). Another study indicated glutamatergic neuronal loss also in the PPN both in PSP and CBD with higher proportion of neurons with tau pathology (Eser et al. 2018). Notably, ad showed comparatively mild PPN loss (~34%), with tangles in fewer cells, suggesting relative cholinergic brainstem sparing. In MSA severe PPN depletion (to ~25% of controls) alongside equally severe loss in the laterodorsal tegmental nucleus (LDT), a pattern broadly similar to PD but previously undocumented in MSA was reported (Benarroch et al. 2002). CBD, examined by showed a distinct profile (Kasashima and Oda 2003): the LDT was largely preserved, while only mild PPN neuronal reduction was found, in sharp contrast to PSP, which exhibited severe LDT cholinergic loss (~41% reduction in ChAT‐positive neurons) alongside high NFT burden—making LDT vulnerability a potential neuropathological discriminator between PSP and CBD. Across the basal forebrain, the nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) was more severely affected in a single case studied CBD than in PSP (Kasashima and Oda 2003). Additional striatal cholinergic deficits were also highlighted (Warren et al. 2005). Importantly, despite all this cholinergic depletion, consistent failure of cholinesterase inhibitors in clinical trials for this disease emphasize further aspects to be explored (Warren et al. 2005).

The LC, the brain's principal source of noradrenaline, is differentially affected across FoG‐relevant neurodegenerative syndromes. In PD, the LC undergoes noradrenergic neuron loss and Lewy body accumulation (Braak et al. 2003), peaking further in PD with dementia (Fructuoso et al. 2025). In MSA, LC degeneration is also severe and is accompanied by glial cytoplasmic inclusions (Benarroch et al. 2002; Campese et al. 2021); uniquely in MSA, noradrenergic degeneration extends beyond the LC to include near‐complete loss (~90%) of the pontine A5 noradrenergic group, which projects to sympathetic preganglionic neurons (Benarroch et al. 2002). In PSP, LC neurodegeneration is driven by tau accumulation, with an average 49% reduction of pigmented noradrenergic neurons relative to controls and 44% of surviving neurons bearing tau‐positive inclusions; importantly, the degree of LC cell loss correlates with clinical severity of PSP even after adjusting for disease duration, implicating noradrenergic deficits in the heterogeneous clinical expression of the syndrome (Kaalund et al. 2020). CBD shares with PSP a pattern of prominent LC tau burden and neurodegeneration (Ohm et al. 2020). These findings contrast with another study that reported increased tau pathology but relatively preserved neuronal numbers in PSP and CBD compared with ad (Eser et al. 2018).

In summary, PSP is characterized by severe, widespread cholinergic neuronal loss spanning the PPN (including glutamatergic cells as well), LDT, NBM, striatum, and brainstem eye movement nuclei, with relative serotonergic predominance over non‐serotonergic neurons (including GABAergic, glutamatergic, or neuropeptidergic populations) in the caudal raphe projecting toward the spinal cord; PD shows moderate and variable PPN/LDT loss, oral raphe serotonergic depletion; MSA uniquely combines severe mesopontine cholinergic depletion with striking serotonergic raphe preservation; and CBD is distinguished from PSP by greater forebrain (NBM) cholinergic loss with relative LDT sparing, though the limited case numbers in CBD studies temper firm conclusions. Importantly, the LC is a convergent site of pathology across these disorders, yet the underlying protein, the severity and spatial extent of noradrenergic loss, and the clinical correlates differ.

A major limitation of postmortem neurotransmitter studies is the lack of clinically homogeneous phenotypes across cohorts, the small number of cases examined in some studies, the use of heterogeneous methodologies and the failure to assess all key nuclei within the same studies, which hampers interpretation of neurotransmitter alterations within individual cases. Innovative approaches to investigate the whole extent of these nuclei as shown for the LC are needed (Gilvesy et al. 2022).

### Regional and Cellular Distribution of Protein Pathology

Systematic mapping of the stage‐dependent regional vulnerability and cell‐type‐specific burden of protein pathology across PD, MSA‐P, MSA‐C, PSP and CBD highlights fundamental differences in both the sequential staging and cellular tropism of pathology across parkinsonian syndromes. PD follows a caudal‐to‐rostral neuronal progression, MSA is distinguished by early and severe oligodendrocytic involvement across multiple regions, PSP by early and widespread neuronal and astrocytic burden with prominent subcortical predilection, and CBD by a uniquely pan‐cellular, high‐burden pattern that does not conform to a linear staging model—reflecting the biological distinctiveness underlying their overlapping clinical presentations.

Regarding FoG‐relevant brain regions a complex pattern emerges when comparing these disorders (Figure 2). Across neurodegenerative diseases, brain regions are affected at different times and involve different cell types. In PD, the frontal cortex is involved relatively late and mainly shows neuronal pathology, whereas in MSA, especially in MSA‐P, this region is affected earlier in contrast to MSA‐C. PSP engages the frontal cortex at an intermediate stage, with a strong predominance of astrocytic pathology alongside neuronal and oligodendrocytic involvement, a pattern similar to CBD, where all three cell types are heavily affected although no specific staging is defined. The striatum is an early and severely affected region in MSA‐P due to marked oligodendrocytic pathology, while in PD it is involved in the fourth out of six stages and affects both neurons and astrocytes. PSP affects the striatum early, again with prominent astrocytic pathology, whereas CBD shows consistently high pathology across neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. The globus pallidus is particularly vulnerable in PSP and CBD, showing severe neuronal pathology and mostly in PSP, oligodendroglial involvement; in contrast, PD affects this region late and moderately, and MSA‐P affects it very early but largely through oligodendrocytes. The subthalamic nucleus is a key distinguishing site for PSP, where it is affected very early and severely in neurons, while it is spared in MSA and less affected in PD. In the midbrain tegmentum, PD shows variable neuronal involvement reflecting disease heterogeneity, whereas MSA‐P and PSP show earlier involvement, but PSP shows clearly more prominent mixed neuronal‐glial pathology compared with MSA‐P where oligodendrocytes can be affected; in MSA‐C, this region is involved in a later phase. In particular, the PPN and the PAG are predominantly affected in PSP and PD; early in PSP, and in PD from the transition between Braak Stages 3 and 4 (Braak et al. 2003). The ponto‐medullary reticular formation is the earliest affected region in PD, with severe neuronal pathology, while MSA‐P, PSP and CBD show intermediate involvement. The cerebellum is especially vulnerable in MSA, particularly MSA‐C, with early oligodendrocytic pathology, whereas PD shows only mild and late involvement. Involvement of the dentate nucleus (neurons) and cerebellar white matter (oligodendrocytes) in PSP is accumulating from intermediate disease stages to prominent levels. Finally, the spinal cord is affected at early‐intermediate stages across disorders, with neuronal pathology predominating in PD, mixed glial involvement in MSA and PSP, and high pathology in CBD.

### Less Common Neurodegenerative Diseases Associated With FoG

In genetic PD, GBA is the strongest and best‐evidenced risk gene for FOG. In a longitudinal study, GBA carriers developed FOG significantly earlier than idiopathic PD (Yang et al. 2023). Two mechanisms appear operative: an Aβ‐dependent pathway, in which reduced CSF Aβ42 in GBA carriers predicts FOG and mediates gait impairment via cognitive decline, and an Aβ‐independent pathway likely driven by α‐synuclein accumulation (Yang et al. 2023). By contrast, parkin‐associated PD shows FOG as a late complication, emerging after decades of disease (Doherty et al. 2013; Barbosa et al. 2025). Clinicopathological data indicate restricted ventral nigral degeneration with relative sparing of cognitive and brainstem nuclei, implying that symptomatology in parkin is driven mainly by ventral nigral degeneration rather than by α‐synuclein spread or amyloid pathology (Doherty et al. 2013; Barbosa et al. 2025).

AGD is a 4R‐tauopathy characterized by early atrophy of the ambient gyrus and abundant argyrophilic, AT8‐positive tau grains predominantly affecting medial temporal lobe structures (Tolnay and Clavaguera 2004) and a distinct filament fold (Shi et al. 2021). Core neuropathological features include tau‐positive grains, pretangles, oligodendroglial coiled bodies and granular/fuzzy astrocytes—most prominently in the amygdala—with relatively few senile plaques and low Braak NFT stages (I–III) (Tolnay and Clavaguera 2004). AGD pathology shows a stereotyped, stage‐dependent progression from the ambient gyrus and CA1 to limbic, paralimbic and eventually neocortical regions (Saito et al. 2004), and is commonly observed as a comorbid pathology in the elderly, where it may lower the threshold for dementia.

AGD usually presents with amnestic syndrome and neuropsychiatric symptoms, but a recent case report described an 80‐year‐old man in whom AGD extension into the midbrain and substantia nigra produced a clinical picture indistinguishable from PSP, including gait freezing, postural instability and bradykinesia over an 11‐year course (Sengoku et al. 2025). Autopsy with Western blotting and immunoelectron microscopy confirmed AGD‐specific tau pathology distinct from PSP. Authors concluded that clinicians should consider AGD in elderly patients with parkinsonism and cognitive decline, particularly when features such as early memory disturbance, slow progression, right‐dominant limbic atrophy on imaging and normal CSF tau levels are present.

Neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis type 6 (CLN6) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder causing progressive neurodegeneration through intraneuronal ceroid lipopigment accumulation, typically presenting between 18 months and 8 years with seizures, vision loss, psychomotor regression, myoclonus and ataxia, leading to rapid decline and early death. A recent study documented an 8‐year‐old boy who developed FoG at age 4 as an isolated early symptom, preceding all other motor signs and occurring without classic parkinsonian features such as bradykinesia, tremor or rigidity (Damasio et al. 2014). MRI findings of white matter changes and atrophy in the frontal lobe, internal capsules and pons support the authors' hypothesis that storage material disruption of frontal‐pontine gait circuits underlies the FoG (Damasio et al. 2014).

### Co‐Pathologies Affecting Brain Regions Relevant for FoG

Regarding mixed pathologies relevant to FoG, several conditions warrant consideration (Figure 3), including Alzheimer's disease neuropathologic change (adNC), limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy neuropathologic change (LATE‐NC), AGD, ageing‐related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG), primary‐age‐related tauopathy (PART), cerebrovascular disease and metabolic encephalopathy. LBD should also be acknowledged as a common co‐pathology; regarding FoG, it is important to note that LBD has been reported in up to 20% of cases with PSP and CBD, and in approximately 5% of cases with MSA.

adNC is defined by extracellular accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) fibrils and intraneuronal deposition of abnormally phosphorylated tau as NFTs (Forrest and Kovacs 2025). Both pathologies show stereotyped spatial progression. Aβ deposition advances from neocortical regions (phase 1) to the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex (2), basal ganglia and diencephalon (3), brainstem (4) and cerebellum (5). Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) frequently accompanies parenchymal Aβ deposits and is classified as capillary (type 1) or non‐capillary (type 2), each with progressive stages. Traditional CERad criteria focus on argyrophilic neuritic plaques, representing only a subset of Aβ morphologies. Importantly, isolated Aβ deposition without tau pathology is insufficient for an ad diagnosis, although it is now viewed as preclinical ad when detected by biomarkers or post‐mortem in cognitively unimpaired individuals. Tau pathology follows the Braak sequence, beginning in the transentorhinal cortex (stage I), entorhinal cortex (II), inferior and middle temporal gyri (III–IV) and later neocortical regions, including occipital cortex (V–VI). Evidence of early tau involvement in the brainstem has led to proposed pre‐cortical staging, supporting the concept that AD pathology begins decades before symptoms. Based on NFT distribution, clinicopathological subtypes (e.g., typical, limbic‐predominant, hippocampal‐sparing) have been described. Neuropathological diagnosis currently follows NIA‐AA guidelines using the ABC system, integrating Aβ phase (A), tau Braak stage (B) and neuritic plaque density (C) to classify AD neuropathologic change as none, low, intermediate or high (Montine et al. 2012).

Recent clinicopathological evidence shows that ADNC significantly worsens FoG in PD (Parmera et al. 2025). A higher global AD pathology burden—especially amyloid‐β deposition—is associated with earlier onset and greater severity of FoG, whereas tau pathology shows no independent effect (Parmera et al. 2025). The findings suggest a synergistic interaction between amyloid‐β and α‐synuclein, possibly through cholinergic pathway degeneration. Further frequent forms of co‐pathologies as listed below have not yet been evaluated systematically to have an effect on FoG.

PART is a common age‐associated tauopathy observed in individuals over 60 years of age (Crary et al. 2014). It is defined by NFT pathology limited to Braak stages ≤IV, with prominent hippocampal involvement, few or no amyloid‐β plaques, and therefore does not meet criteria for adNC. It occurs in neurologically normal individuals as well as in those with cognitive impairment and encompasses NFT‐predominant dementia or tangle‐only associated cognitive impairment.

LATE‐NC is a recently defined, common TDP‐43 proteinopathy strongly associated with advanced age (typically > 80 years) and cognitive impairment, occurring without a clinical motor neuron disease or frontotemporal dementia phenotype (Nelson et al. 2019). It is characterized by TDP‐43–immunoreactive neuronal cytoplasmic inclusions and/or dystrophic neurites, predominantly affecting the medial temporal lobe. Although limbic TDP‐43 pathology has long been recognized in aging and various neurodegenerative disorders, LATE‐NC is now considered distinguishable from FTLD‐TDP and may occur in isolation, though it frequently coexists with Alzheimer's disease (Nelson et al. 2019). In the neuropathologic practice, a three‐tiered staging is used, emphasizing early involvement of the amygdala, followed by the hippocampus and frontal lobe (Nelson et al. 2023). Hippocampal sclerosis commonly accompanies LATE‐NC and is reported in up to 20% of individuals over 85 years. It is defined by disproportionate neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampal CA1 region and subiculum and is strongly associated with TDP‐43 deposition.

As discussed above, AGD is a limbic tauopathy that often presents as co‐pathology. AGD stages II or III were present in 18.5% of LBD cases, but no differences were found in the frequency of AGD among the LBD subgroups (Colom‐Cadena et al. 2017), it can be present in approximately 60% of PSP and practically all CBD cases.

ARTAG is a tau astrocytic pathology commonly observed in individuals over 60 years of age. Consensus criteria define five ARTAG types—grey matter, white matter, perivascular, subpial and subependymal—characterized by thorn‐shaped and/or granular fuzzy astrocytes (Kovacs et al. 2016). ARTAG can occur in isolation but more often coexists with neurodegenerative diseases. Predilection sites include the medial temporal lobe (especially the amygdala) and basal forebrain, with disease‐specific distribution patterns (Kovacs et al. 2017). Different ARTAG types associate with distinct clinicopathological features and aging measures, though their clinical significance remains incompletely understood (Kovacs 2020). Importantly, ARTAG is thought to develop in response to barrier dysfunction and to reflect the cumulative accumulation of eventually transient pathological events (Kovacs 2020).

Among non‐neurodegenerative co‐pathologies, small vessel disease is common and typically manifests as fibrohyalinosis of small vessels in the basal ganglia and cerebral lobar white matter. Even in the absence of a definitive infarct, enlargement of perivascular spaces can increase reactive astrogliosis in the white matter and basal ganglia. In addition, fibrotic changes in small vessels may cause chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, affecting neural circuits relevant for gait. These effects can be transiently exacerbated by fluctuations in blood pressure or metabolic disturbances, such as changes in blood glucose levels.

In addition to vascular disease, metabolic encephalopathy—even at low or subclinical levels related to liver or kidney disease—is common and potentially reversible. These conditions arise from direct neurotoxic effects (e.g., ammonia, urea), impaired cerebral energy metabolism, systemic inflammation and disrupted excitatory–inhibitory neurotransmission and are associated with the development of so‐called metabolic astroglia (Alzheimer type II astrocytes), underscoring the central role of astrocytes in pathogenesis (Gelpi et al. 2020).

Regarding the latter two as co‐pathologies, an interesting observation from a longitudinal aging study described individuals initially classified as having clinically possible AD who later showed cognitive improvement, giving rise to the impression of reversible cognitive decline (Kovacs et al. 2013). In these cases, vascular lesions or metabolic encephalopathy were proposed as contributing factors, occurring in the context of a low‐grade neurodegenerative disorder. This observation may be relevant for understanding how metabolic states or transient alterations in cerebral perfusion can precipitate or exacerbate neurological symptoms.

## Toward an Integrated Neuropathological Framework to Understand FoG

FoG likely arises from impaired gait pattern generation, disrupted central drive, reduced movement automaticity, abnormal posture‐gait coupling, perceptual dysfunction and deficits in frontal executive control (Nutt et al. 2011). Although the anatomical structures involved have been generally defined, the mechanisms underlying transient neuronal dysfunction at the cellular level remain poorly understood. Understanding neurodegeneration requires moving beyond isolated protein pathology to a systems‐level framework that accounts for disease stage, co‐pathologies (vascular, neuroinflammatory, metabolic) and proteinopathy subtypes (Figure 4). This reframes diagnosis from a categorical label into a dynamic, individualized pathological map that better reflects heterogeneity, informs prognosis and enables targeted intervention. FoG is best understood not as the product of a single lesion but as an emergent failure of a locomotor network operating near its functional threshold—a ‘network vulnerability’ model (Weiss et al. 2020) in which cumulative structural and neurochemical compromise erodes normal compensatory capacity. As this reserve is depleted, physiological fluctuations in attention, emotion, neurotransmitter tone or environmental complexity can precipitate episodic gait collapse (Nutt et al. 2011; Weiss et al. 2020). This implies that observed neuropathological abnormalities may represent a heterogeneous mix of direct causes, permissive preconditions, incidental comorbidities or secondary consequences—a complexity that must inform how imaging and post‐mortem findings are interpreted. Indeed, the broad, heterogeneous clinical risk profile associated with FoG in PD (Herman et al. 2023) supports the notion here that low‐level co‐pathologies precondition individuals to states that aggravate or precipitate freezing episodes.

Although there are relatively few observations on how co‐pathologies influence the development of FoG, several considerations can be drawn from known anatomical–pathological patterns. First, some co‐pathologies involve brain regions critical for gait control and may therefore predispose the system to transient failures that result in FoG. Importantly, ADNC involves brainstem nuclei with neurotransmitter relevance to FoG (Eser et al. 2018; Gilvesy et al. 2022). Second, the consistent involvement of the limbic system—particularly the frequent and severe involvement of the amygdala—suggests an indirect effect on gait. This aligns with clinical observations that emotional factors, including anxiety and depression, can contribute to the development or exacerbation of FoG. Indeed, the limbic system, particularly the amygdala, appears to play a critical role in FoG by driving heightened striato‐limbic connectivity that overwhelms top–down attentional control (Gilat et al. 2018). This dysfunctional fronto‐striato‐limbic interaction, likely rooted in anxiety and fear of falling, may precipitate gait freezing when the additional cognitive demands of walking further challenge an already overburdened system (Gilat et al. 2018). Beyond this, the amygdala is implicated in vestibular processing relevant to FoG (Bohnen et al. 2022), reflecting the extensive integration of vestibular and limbic networks that enable vestibular stimulation to modulate emotional behaviour via higher central and autonomic nervous system pathways (Rajagopalan et al. 2017). For the sake of completeness, it should be noted that the PAG, which is considered as an extralimbic anxiety (‘fear conditioning’) area (De Oca et al. 1998), is affected early in PSP and in intermediate stages of PD. Furthermore, beyond the amygdala, the co‐pathological involvement of the hippocampus is particularly significant given its central role in multisensory processing (Raccah et al. 2026) and spatial navigation (Strange et al. 2014), both of which are critical to the development of FoG (Fearon et al. 2015; How et al. 2022). Third, as observed in the primary disorders associated with FoG (i.e., PD, PSP, CBD), astroglial responses play a central role in certain co‐pathologies such as cerebral response to systemic metabolic conditions. Given their functions in buffering neurotoxicity and regulating ion transport, astrocytes are particularly relevant when considering mechanisms of transient neural dysfunction.

## Conclusion: Contribution of Neuropathology

In conclusion, neuropathological investigation advances understanding of FoG by identifying lesions in gait‐control regions, uncovering low‐burden combined pathologies concentrated in the limbic system, alongside primary neurodegenerative disease that compound neuronal dysfunction in gait‐relevant brain regions, which collectively predispose to freezing, and by implicating astroglia as an underrecognized contributor to episodic neurological dysfunction. Postmortem studies have been particularly instrumental in delineating further the neurotransmitter spectrum of nuclei implicated in FoG. Nevertheless, research on the neuropathological basis of FoG remains limited, largely due to its intermittent nature and the lack of well‐characterized prospective cases (Weiss et al. 2020). The inherently cross‐sectional nature of postmortem investigation further prevents dynamic tracking of how neurochemical and structural changes evolve in relation to FoG onset and progression. Progress will therefore require multimodal, in vivo approaches interrogating distributed neural networks across neurotransmitter systems (Weiss et al. 2020) into a more comprehensive mechanistic framework. Moving forward, neuropathological inquiry must also expand beyond protein aggregation characterization to encompass astroglial reactions bridging the gap between static lesion confirmation and a dynamic understanding of FoG. Finally, rigorous studies examining the contribution of co‐pathologies lay the groundwork for multimodal biomarker approaches in clinical practice (Martinez‐Valbuena et al. 2026), with the aim of improving prediction of FoG across disease populations. Together with further research into shifts in neurotransmitter balance, these efforts may ultimately enable more effective therapeutic strategies.
