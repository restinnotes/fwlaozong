# Local Field Potentials in Movement Disorders: A Narrative Review

## Abstract
Local field potentials (LFPs) represent the summed electrical activity of neuronal populations and provide a critical window into synaptic and oscillatory dynamics across brain networks. In recent decades, advances in deep brain stimulation (DBS) and chronic sensing technology have established LFPs as essential biomarkers for understanding movement disorders and optimizing neuromodulation therapies. This review synthesizes evidence on LFPs in movement disorders, focusing on methodological aspects, functional characterization, disease-specific findings, and clinical applications. In Parkinson’s disease (PD), exaggerated beta-band activity in the subthalamic nucleus and globus pallidus internus (GPi) correlates with bradykinesia and rigidity, while dyskinesias are linked to broadband gamma activity and disrupted cross-frequency coupling. Essential tremor (ET) shows tremor-frequency synchronization in the thalamus and related networks. In dystonia, theta and alpha oscillations in the GPi correlate with symptom severity and guide DBS targeting. Tics in Tourette syndrome involve thalamic low-frequency oscillations, while chorea in Huntington’s disease is associated with elevated beta-gamma power. Clinically, LFPs are increasingly used for intraoperative targeting, postoperative programming, outpatient monitoring, and as biomarkers for adaptive DBS (aDBS). LFPs provide fundamental insights into disease-specific neural oscillations across PD, ET, dystonia, tics, and chorea. Pathological patterns serve as reliable biomarkers of symptom states. By leveraging these signals, DBS can be more accurately targeted, programming can be streamlined, and long-term outpatient monitoring can be enhanced. aDBS based on LFP biomarkers is already in clinical use for PD, offering superior motor control and fewer side effects than conventional stimulation. Keywords: Local field potential, Deep brain stimulation, Movement disorders, Programming, Adaptive deep brain stimulation

## Introduction

The cumulative body of knowledge on local field potentials (LFPs) marks an essential development in the understanding of various aspects of brain function and a crucial window into the coordinated behavior of neurons. LFPs define the electrical signals generated by the activity of neuronal clusters, typically identified using electrodes placed in brain tissue, reflecting synaptic activity and membrane oscillations [1]. Notably, similar recording patterns can be associated with different functions depending on the brain network involved, varying according to disease and individual variables, such as motor and behavioral states.

The earliest studies involving human LFPs can be traced back to Hans Berger’s introduction of the electroencephalogram in the 1920s. Although electroencephalogram records large-scale brain activity from the scalp, historically, it showed evidence of rhythmic electrical brain patterns, hinting at the underlying localized activity later studied through LFPs [2]. In the mid-20th century, groundbreaking studies by Herbert Jasper, Wilder Penfield, and others led to insights into cortical oscillations using direct cortical recordings in patients with epilepsy [2]. The first descriptions of LFPs as mesoscopic signals reflecting synchronized population activity emerged soon after, with seminal work by Jasper and Andrews [3] and by Hubel [4] demonstrating that low-frequency potentials recorded from the cortex captured population-level synaptic activity distinct from single-unit discharges.

Between the 1960s and 1970s, in vivo recordings were refined by the use of tungsten microelectrodes, offering high mechanical resistance, sharp tips, and good electrical conductivity, which allow chronic implantation in animals [5]. In recent decades, the resurgence of interest in neural oscillations, especially in the beta and gamma frequency ranges, has elevated LFPs as critical tools in understanding motor control, cognition, and disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and epilepsy. Techniques like deep brain stimulation (DBS) have incorporated LFP recordings to optimize therapy, exemplifying the translational relevance of LFPs in modern neurology [1].

Building upon these technological advancements, pivotal studies have demonstrated how LFP recordings from implanted DBS electrodes can directly link neural oscillatory activity with clinical states in movement disorders. Landmark studies by Brown [6] and Priori et al. [7] were among the first to systematically demonstrate that LFPs recorded from the subthalamic nucleus (STN) in PD patients reveal distinct oscillatory patterns that correlate with motor symptom severity and are modulated by both voluntary movement and dopaminergic therapies. Brown [6] highlighted two principal modes of synchronized activity within the subthalamo-pallidal-thalamo-cortical circuitry, one in the beta range (<30 Hz), which is pathologically increased in PD and reduced by dopaminergic therapy, and the other in the gamma range (>60 Hz), which is associated with normal movement and restored by treatment [6]. Priori and colleagues provided compelling evidence that the amplitude of beta-band oscillations in the STN is specifically suppressed following administration of dopaminergic agents, whereas other frequency bands show distinct, rhythm-specific responses to pharmacological modulation, supporting the concept of multiple autonomous oscillatory systems within the human basal ganglia, each linked to different aspects of motor function and dysfunction [7]. Collectively, these pioneering studies established LFPs recorded from DBS electrodes as robust biomarkers linking basal ganglia network oscillations to the pathophysiology and treatment response in PD, thereby catalyzing subsequent advances in adaptive DBS (aDBS) and biomarker-driven neuromodulation strategies.

## Methods

We conducted a narrative review of the literature on movement disorders and LFPs until October 2025. Searches were conducted in the PubMed and Embase databases. Combinations of LFP with each of the following conditions were included in the search terms: PD (or Parkinson), tremor, dystonia, Tourette syndrome (TS) (or Tourette), tics, and chorea. Title and abstract were used to filter the first search results. When abstracts indicated possible relevance, full texts were examined. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and clinical guidelines were included for their comprehensive and evaluative synthesis of the field. Original research articles were defined as investigations reporting new data in human subjects distinct from experimental or translational studies conducted primarily in animal models or in vitro. Experimental and translational studies were included only if their mechanistic findings were explicitly linked to the pathophysiology, biomarkers, or clinical features of movement disorders in humans. We integrated systematic reviews and meta-analyses by extracting their major thematic syntheses, using them to contextualize and compare individual study findings. Original research articles were used to illustrate specific new discoveries, while experimental and translational studies contributed mechanistic insights where directly relevant. Clinical guidelines were used to highlight consensus or standards of care when indicated.

The evidence that was extracted was compiled narratively and arranged into the following thematic sections: (i) techniques for recording and processing LFPs; (ii) event-related dynamics and functional characterization; (iii) disease-specific findings across movement disorders; and (iv) clinical and translational applications such as aDBS. Studies were included if they provided original or review data on human or translational models relevant to movement disorders and employed LFP recordings or analyses from subcortical or cortical structures. Exclusion criteria comprised articles not involving LFPs, studies limited to non-motor disorders, animal-only studies without human relevance, and publications not available in English. Although this was a narrative rather than systematic review, we aimed for transparent and reproducible screening. The initial search retrieved 914 records across databases. After title and abstract screening, 175 full-text articles were reviewed in detail, and 72 publications were ultimately retained for synthesis, including key original studies, meta-analyses, and recent reviews. Reference lists of these papers were further examined to identify additional relevant sources. Frequency-specific oscillatory signatures, their associations with clinical features, and potential use as biomarkers for neuromodulation were given particular emphasis.

## Results

### Recording Methods

In humans, LFPs are recorded using electrodes designed to capture low-frequency population activity, typically in the range of 0.5–250 Hz. These signals can be obtained during invasive procedures such as epilepsy monitoring (using subdural strips, grids, or depth electrodes) or during DBS surgery [8] (Table 1).

[table-wrap T1] Applications of LFPs in DBS and clinical practice

During DBS implantation, microelectrode recording (MER) is used for high-resolution mapping of neuronal activity to localize the target nucleus [i.e., STN or globus pallidus internus (GPi)]. MER electrodes, which are high-impedance tungsten or platinum-iridium microelectrodes with fine tips (3–25 µm), are designed to record single- and multiunit activity, not LFPs, due to their geometry and impedance characteristics [9]. LFPs can, however, be captured either from the reference ring of the MER electrode, where impedance is lower, or, more commonly, from the macroelectrode contacts of the permanent DBS lead once it is implanted. From the microelectrode tip, only multiunit burst activity can be detected, which may correlate with LFP dynamics but does not represent LFPs per se [8–10].

LFPs may be recorded under several conditions following DBS electrode implantation. Acute recordings are typically obtained from externalized leads in the immediate postoperative period, before the implantable pulse generator (IPG) is placed, using external cables that connect the intracranial electrodes to a digital signal processor; this setup enables recording during movement tasks and under various medication and stimulation states [7, 11]. Intraoperative recordings can also be conducted before the IPG is connected, utilizing external amplifiers and processors [8, 9]. With recent advancements, chronic recordings are now possible using modern bidirectional DBS systems capable of sensing and storing LFPs, allowing data acquisition both in clinic (streaming mode) and at home (chronic mode) for long-term monitoring [12]. These systems further enable outpatient analysis of brain LFP activity using the clinician’s programmer tablet.

In the context of recordings done acutely after DBS lead implantation, local peri-lead edema formation and progression alter the surrounding tissue matrix and LFP signal characteristics. A few studies demonstrate that edema volume can significantly correlate with low beta-band power and may contribute to transient fluctuations in impedance and LFP stability [13, 14]. These effects must be considered when interpreting immediate postoperative LFP recordings as they may confound assessments of disease-specific oscillatory activity and biomarker validity. Accordingly, studies should document recording timing, use imaging to quantify edema, and incorporate methodological caveats regarding the acute phase after electrode placement in both research and clinical settings (Table 2) [14].

### Functional Characterization of LFPs

LFPs are analyzed using a spectral analysis framework, primarily reflecting synchronized synaptic inputs, subthreshold membrane oscillations, and slow ionic currents, rather than the spiking of individual neurons. These are complex signals composed of multiple frequency bands – delta (0–3 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), gamma (31–200 Hz), and high frequency (>200 Hz), each associated with distinct neural processes [10, 15]. These frequency bands are defined arbitrarily and may exhibit overlapping boundaries, contributing to instances in which findings are not uniformly documented in the literature. Higher frequency oscillations extending up to 350–400 Hz have been reported, encompassing ripple-like activity relevant in particular neurological conditions, such as epilepsy where they have been identified as biomarkers of epileptogenic tissue in the mesial temporal lobe and neocortex [16].

The amplitude and power of LFPs reflect the degree of neuronal synchronization near the electrode; these frequency bands are not inherently pathological as their functional relevance depends on spatial and contextual factors. For instance, normal LFP activity in the GPi features low-amplitude, transient beta oscillations and increased gamma activity during movement, reflecting healthy motor processing. In contrast, in PD, GPi LFPs show abnormally elevated and sustained beta activity, particularly in the low-beta range correlating with motor symptoms like rigidity and bradykinesia [1, 10, 15]. These distinct oscillatory patterns help differentiate normal from pathological states and guide DBS strategies. As such, functional characterization of LFPs involves analyzing their spectral content, coherence, and phase relationships with behavior or external stimuli. Importantly, oscillatory signatures are not static but dynamically modulated by behavioral, motor, and cognitive states. The spectral profile of LFPs can vary substantially depending on whether recordings are obtained during rest, voluntary movement, or under different medication and vigilance conditions [17, 18]. For example, beta desynchronization typically accompanies movement execution, while re-emergent beta synchrony is observed during rest or postural maintenance [18, 19]. Likewise, attention, task engagement, and sleep-wake transitions modulate both oscillatory power and coupling patterns across basal ganglia-cortical circuits. These state-dependent dynamics emphasize that the validity and interpretation of LFP biomarkers are contingent on recording conditions and that variability across studies may often reflect differences in behavioral or cognitive context rather than inconsistent physiology [17–19].

In addition to the oscillatory components typically analyzed in LFP studies, the aperiodic or non-oscillatory component of neural power spectra, often described by a 1/f-like decay, encodes crucial information about the underlying synaptic and circuit dynamics. The slope and offset of this aperiodic component reflect the excitation-inhibition (E/I) balance and the scale of neuronal population activity, providing complementary physiological insights beyond oscillatory rhythms [20]. Methodologically, spectral parameterization techniques now allow the decomposition of LFP power spectra into periodic (oscillatory) and aperiodic (1/f) components, enabling more accurate quantification of both rhythmic and broadband activity. In PD, the aperiodic exponent has been shown to vary with dopaminergic state, decreasing after medication and during effective DBS, consistent with restoration of physiological E/I balance [20, 21]. These findings suggest that aperiodic metrics may serve as additional biomarkers of disease state and treatment response, enhancing the interpretive precision of LFP analyses. Integrating aperiodic measures with traditional oscillatory features may therefore provide a more complete representation of neural network dynamics in movement disorders.

Beyond spectral power analysis, contemporary approaches increasingly emphasize temporal and coupling-based metrics to capture the dynamic organization of oscillatory activity. Amplitude-amplitude and phase-phase coupling analyses assess synchrony across frequency bands or spatially distinct regions, providing insight into functional connectivity within and between basal ganglia-cortical networks [22]. Phase-amplitude coupling, in particular, quantifies how the phase of a low-frequency oscillation (e.g., beta) modulates the amplitude of a higher-frequency rhythm (e.g., gamma), reflecting cross-scale coordination relevant to motor control and disease states. Abnormal beta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling in PD and dystonia has been linked to pathological synchronization and impaired movement execution [23]. In the time domain, transient oscillatory bursts, especially beta bursts, are now recognized as physiologically meaningful events rather than sustained rhythms. Their rate, duration, and timing correlate with motor performance and dopaminergic modulation [24]. These complementary analytical dimensions expand traditional spectral power frameworks, allowing a richer characterization of network dynamics in movement disorders.

While frequency-specific power changes are valuable for distinguishing pathological from physiological brain states, several methodological factors must be considered when interpreting these findings. Band-power metrics are highly dependent on referencing schemes, filtering choices, and behavioral or medication states, all of which can influence both spectral amplitude and peak frequency estimation [17, 25]. Furthermore, frequency bands represent heuristic divisions of a continuous spectrum rather than fixed physiological boundaries, and their optimal limits may vary across individuals, brain regions, and disease states [25]. Recent work by Bronte-Stewart and colleagues has demonstrated that spectral peaks can shift toward lower or higher frequency ranges depending on dopaminergic state and cortical involvement, with exaggerated alpha rhythms emerging in some patients with PD [26]. Such inter- and intra-individual variability underscores the importance of adaptive or data-driven approaches to spectral analysis rather than relying solely on canonical frequency-band definitions. Acknowledging these dependencies is crucial to ensure that band-power features are interpreted as context-sensitive markers rather than static, universally applicable biomarkers.

Finally, although the present review focuses on motor aspects of movement disorders, it is important to note that LFP recordings have also been extensively applied to study non-motor processes such as emotional regulation, reward processing, moral decision-making, and impulsivity. Recordings from the STN, nucleus accumbens, and anterior cingulate cortex have revealed frequency-specific modulations linked to affective valence, risk-taking, and cognitive control, indicating that these circuits contribute to both motor and limbic functions [19, 21]. For example, STN low-frequency oscillations (4–12 Hz) have been shown to encode conflict and emotional salience, while beta and gamma activity in limbic territories reflect decision uncertainty and reward expectancy [27]. These findings highlight that LFPs provide a valuable window into the broader functional integration of motor, cognitive, and affective networks, even though non-motor domains fall beyond the central scope of this review.

### Event-Related Synchronization and Desynchronization

Changes in power in response to specific events – such as movement, sensory input, or cognitive tasks – are referred to as event-related desynchronization (ERD) and event-related synchronization (ERS) [17]. These patterns, observed in LFP recordings, reflect dynamic shifts in neural activity. ERD typically indicates a transient decrease in power within a given frequency band and is interpreted as a sign of increased neuronal activation and reduced synchrony, allowing for more flexible information processing. It commonly occurs in the alpha and beta bands during motor preparation or execution [1, 17]. In contrast, ERS reflects a transient power increase whose functional significance varies with frequency and behavioral context. For example, beta/alpha rebounds are often linked to inhibitory or resetting processes, whereas gamma-band ERS is typically associated with local activation and information processing [18, 19]. The interpretation of ERD/ERS therefore depends strongly on the specific frequency band, anatomical locus, and task conditions. Together, these phenomena provide important insights into neural dynamics and are widely used in both basic neuroscience and clinical applications, including brain-computer interfaces and aDBS (Tables 2 and 3) [17–19].

### LFPs in PD

Abnormal neural oscillation synchronization in the basal ganglia, particularly within the GPi and STN, is an important trait of PD pathophysiology. One of the most robust and recurring observed features in the assessment of LFPs in PD patients is augmented beta frequency power, as mentioned previously in this review [28]. Importantly, beta oscillations can be further subdivided into two canonical spectral bands: low-beta (13–20 Hz) and high-beta (21–35 Hz). The relevance of this discrimination relies on the fact that their functions differ: low-beta activity is dominant in the STN, considered pathological, closely linked to disease severity, and sensitive to dopaminergic or DBS therapies [29]. This activity band is tightly correlated with certain motor symptoms, such as bradykinesia and rigidity. Conversely, high beta is thought to support physiological, prokinetic functions, probably playing a role in motor control and recovery during effective treatment [28, 29]. Tremor is less consistently correlated with beta power, tending to occur with oscillations synchronized at or near the tremor frequency. These phenomena correlate better with theta and may involve distinct circuits, such as the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit [30].

Postural instability and gait disorder (PIGD), which is typically more resistant to levodopa therapy, is more heterogeneous in terms of LFP recordings. In this scenario, beta oscillations are less directly correlated to PIGD symptoms and decreased beta power and lower frequency oscillations in the theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) bands have been reported in the STN and GPi [31]. Recordings of PIGD cases also show disrupted cross-frequency coupling (CFC) – defined as the structured, non-random interaction between different brain frequency bands – among beta and either higher (gamma) and lower frequency bands [32]. Furthermore, physiologic decrease in beta activity during movement (desynchronization) is blunted in PIGD patients, indicating reduced neural flexibility that might impair postural responses and gait initiation [31]. As a final point, freezing of gait (FOG) has also been correlated with brief beta bursts just before or during episodes. However, given the complexity of FOG, this finding is not consistent across patients [33]. Of importance, other frequency bands may be equally or more relevant, especially in those cases with a marked cognitive component.

Conversely, levodopa and effective DBS alter LFPs by reducing beta power, restoring more normal patterns of neural activity, and potentially increasing gamma and, to a lower extent, theta and alpha oscillations. As such, this pattern is considered prokinetic, closely linked to clinical motor improvement and the occurrence of peak-dose dyskinesias. On the flipside, re-emergence of beta oscillations, either upon levodopa wearing off or by switching DBS off, correlates with more severe signs of parkinsonism [28, 29].

As already pointed out, dopaminergic medication suppresses beta activity and improves motor symptoms. However, when this suppression becomes excessive, it can be associated with the emergence of dyskinesias, suggesting that while some beta reduction is therapeutic, undue suppression can lead to hyperkinetic side effects [28–30]. In contrast, STN and GPi gamma band activity, particularly in the low to mid-gamma range (60–90 Hz), often increases during dyskinesias. Some patients also exhibit broader increases in power across the gamma range, sometimes referred to as broadband gamma [28]. This increase is believed to reflect the abnormal, involuntary movements characteristic of dyskinesias and may involve both subcortical and cortical motor circuits. Additionally, theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–12 Hz) activity may also increase in some patients, although the significance of these changes is less clearly defined [15]. These oscillations may contribute to the pathological network synchrony that underlies dyskinetic states. Finally, during dyskinesias, CFC between low-frequency phases (theta or alpha) and gamma can be disrupted or disorganized, reflecting impaired information processing and abnormal motor control [23]. Together, these changes (suppressed beta activity, increased gamma power, and disrupted CFC) represent a candidate neurophysiological fingerprint of dyskinetic states in PD. While increased broadband gamma power and altered CFC during dyskinesias are consistent findings across multiple studies, it is important to acknowledge that such signals can be influenced by movement-related artifacts [28]. Dyskinesias often involve vigorous, repetitive muscle contractions capable of introducing high-frequency components that may overlap with true neural gamma activity. To mitigate this confound, most studies apply rigorous preprocessing, including high-pass filtering, notch and harmonic artifact removal, and the use of electromyographic or accelerometry signals for artifact detection and exclusion [18, 23, 28]. Furthermore, simultaneous cortical and subcortical recordings, as well as recordings during both medication- and stimulation-induced dyskinesias, have provided converging evidence that at least part of the broadband gamma enhancement reflects genuine neural activity rather than muscular contamination [18, 23]. Nonetheless, the potential contribution of peripheral artifacts cannot be completely ruled out and should be explicitly considered when interpreting gamma- and coupling-based biomarkers. This methodological consideration is particularly relevant for aDBS paradigms that rely on high-frequency features for real-time control, underscoring the need for artifact-aware signal processing and multimodal validation [23, 34].

Finally, while gamma-band activity during dyskinesias is a consistent finding in several studies, its pathophysiological role remains under discussion. Some authors have reported a predominance of broadband gamma activity during levodopa-induced dyskinesia and during aDBS paradigms that use gamma-based control signals [35], whereas others have questioned whether this represents a truly pathological oscillation or a physiological correlate of movement and cortical excitation [9, 17, 18]. In parallel, theta and alpha oscillations have been more consistently observed in both motor and non-motor states, including emotional and cognitive processing [36, 37]. Moreover, high-frequency oscillations (HFOs) (250–330 Hz) recorded in the STN have been linked to the dopaminergic ON state, supporting their potential as markers of prokinetic network engagement [38].

As mentioned previously, an extensive body of literature has explored non-motor correlates of LFPs in PD, emphasizing that basal ganglia oscillatory activity extends beyond motor control, reflecting the integration of motor, limbic, and associative circuits [19, 21, 27].

LFP recordings of HFOs in distinct anatomical areas of the STN have also been associated with different clinical PD phenotypes. HFOs include part of the gamma spectrum (>60 Hz), high gamma (90–200 Hz), and faster ripple-like activity (200–450 Hz). While slow HFOs tend to occur in the dorsal STN and are associated with the tremor-dominant subtype of PD, fast HFOs above 90 Hz are often recorded in the ventral STN and are linked to PIGD. These distinctive anatomical and clinical features are more variable and less pronounced for the GPi recordings [15, 30–32]. The applications of LFP monitoring in PD and its integration into closed-loop or aDBS systems will be further discussed in a separate section of this review (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in ET

Essential tremor (ET) is increasingly recognized as a network disorder involving abnormal oscillatory activity within the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have demonstrated tremor-related activity and heightened coherence across the cerebellum, thalamus, and motor cortex [39]. This pathological synchronization correlates with tremor severity, underscoring the circuit’s central role in ET pathophysiology [39].

LFPs recorded from the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus consistently show prominent oscillatory activity at tremor frequency (4–12 Hz), with amplitude increases during tremor episodes. These oscillations are coherent with electromyographic and accelerometric signals from affected limbs, both at tremor frequency and at its harmonics, and are spatially confined to specific VIM subregions [40]. Furthermore, MER from motor thalamic nuclei (VIM and ventral oral posterior) reveals strong coherence with pathological tremor frequencies (4–7 Hz), whereas physiological tremor-related oscillations (8–12 Hz) are distributed across motor and somatosensory thalamic regions. CFC analyses indicate a hierarchical organization where low-frequency theta rhythms modulate beta and gamma band activity [40].

Patients with ET also exhibit excessive synchronization in the theta and beta frequency bands. During voluntary motor tasks, beta-band activity (13–30 Hz) typically decreases while gamma oscillations (55–80 Hz) increase, a dynamic that may serve as a physiological biomarker for aDBS. Notably, thalamic beta oscillations – especially those localized along the dentato-rubro-thalamic tract – have been associated with sustained tremor suppression, highlighting their potential as a therapeutic target [41]. Emerging evidence also suggests that beta oscillations in the VIM are modulated by task complexity. Specifically, during incongruent visuomotor center-out tasks, VIM beta-band power is significantly reduced compared to congruent tasks, while neuronal firing rates increase during periods of low beta power, particularly as targets are approached [42]. In contrast, patients with PD show no comparable modulation of beta power in the STN under similar conditions [17]. These findings imply that beta suppression may facilitate thalamocortical information transfer via enhanced neuronal firing, underscoring the task-dependent dynamics of thalamic oscillations in ET [40–42].

Complementing these observations, both voluntary movements and postural tremor can be reliably detected using thalamic LFPs recorded from the same electrodes used for therapeutic stimulation. Detection reached a mean sensitivity of 0.8 with a false-positive rate of 0.2. Low-beta (13–22 Hz) and -theta (4–7 Hz) oscillations were the primary features for identifying movement and tremor, respectively [43]. Importantly, movement detection often preceded the actual onset of movement. Classifiers trained on specific, cue-based tasks were able to successfully generalize to self-initiated movements that resemble everyday activities. However, distinct models appear necessary to optimize detection of voluntary movement versus postural tremor [41, 43].

Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of thalamic oscillatory dynamics in the pathophysiology of ET and support the use of LFP features as reliable biomarkers to detect tremor and guide neuromodulation. Incorporating these biomarkers into fully implantable, closed-loop DBS systems holds significant promise for advancing personalized, adaptive stimulation strategies aimed at improving clinical outcomes in ET (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Dystonia

In dystonia, LFPs recorded from the GPi – the primary DBS target – consistently show increased power in the theta range (4–12 Hz). This low-frequency activity has been correlated with EMG signal and symptom severity, reinforcing its pathological relevance [15, 30]. Directional coupling analyses reveal a bidirectional relationship between the GPi and affected muscles, with a dominant influence exerted by the GPi. Notably, this low-frequency activity is responsive to peripheral inputs, as demonstrated by its modulation during “geste antagoniste” in cervical dystonia [15].

This low-frequency oscillatory pattern appears to be distinct from those observed in other movement disorders, such as PD and Huntington’s disease (HD), further supporting its specificity to dystonia. Moreover, recordings from the GPi reveal greater low-frequency power than in the external pallidum (globus pallidus externus [GPe]), emphasizing its spatial specificity [10, 44]. The stability of this signal over time, confirmed through chronic recordings conducted months after DBS implantation, further supports its reliability as a biomarker for both disease monitoring and therapeutic targeting [44]. The therapeutic effects of DBS in dystonia may involve the suppression of abnormally synchronized low-frequency oscillations between the GPi and cortex, particularly in patients with phasic dystonia [45, 46]. Of note, the temporal dynamics of these changes differ substantially from those observed in PD, where modulation of beta activity by DBS or dopaminergic medication typically occurs within seconds, reflecting rapid circuit-level adjustments. In contrast, in dystonia, the reduction in pathological low-frequency power and the normalization of cortical coherence often develop gradually, over hours to days or even weeks, paralleling the delayed clinical response characteristic of this condition [45–47]. This slower timescale likely reflects plasticity-driven reorganization rather than acute suppression of pathological synchronization. Targeting regions within the GPi that exhibit maximal low-frequency power has been associated with better clinical outcomes, suggesting a functional relevance of this biomarker in guiding electrode placement [48]. Furthermore, recordings from patients with long-term DBS show that low-frequency activity continues to correlate with dystonia severity, reinforcing its enduring relevance for adaptive neuromodulation. Subtype-specific differences have also been reported, reflecting distinct underlying network dynamics. Phasic dystonia is associated with alpha-band oscillations (8–13 Hz) and functional coupling between GPi, GPe, and motor cortex, whereas tonic dystonia shows delta oscillations (2–4 Hz) and predominant coupling between the GPi and GPe [45, 48]. These patterns suggest phenotype-dependent variations in circuit dysfunction. Additionally, cervical dystonia has been shown to exhibit interhemispheric asymmetries in GPi activity across frequency bands, further highlighting the heterogeneity of oscillatory signatures across dystonia phenotypes [49].

Beyond low-frequency oscillations, a broader frequency spectrum has implications for both pathological and physiological motor control. For instance, walking has been shown to increase theta-alpha activity and reduce beta power, indicating that some oscillatory components may reflect normal motor function. Gamma-band activity is modulated during both movement and cognitive engagement and may contribute to motor learning and adaptation [44, 45]. These findings caution against indiscriminate suppression of neural oscillations during DBS as targeting pathological activity too broadly could interfere with essential physiological processes, as appears to be the case in dystonia patients with deterioration of gait after GPi DBS [47].

Finally, studies of cortico-subcortical coherence have revealed three main oscillatory networks: theta (pallido-temporal), alpha (pallido-cerebellar), and beta (cortico-pallidal) [44, 49]. Among these, only the alpha pallido-cerebellar network shows an inverse correlation with dystonia severity, emphasizing the importance of target modulation. Supporting this, both GPi and STN DBS were shown to reduce pathological alpha oscillations and interhemispheric motor cortex coherence, suggesting that network desynchronization is a core therapeutic mechanism [46]. Taken together, these findings support the pathophysiological relevance of low-frequency oscillations in dystonia and its potential as a biomarker for aDBS techniques (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Tics

Tics in the context of TS present as a childhood-onset disorder characterized by motor and phonic tics, often accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities. While many respond to pharmacological and behavioral therapies, a subset remains refractory. During tic episodes, the LFPs recorded in the thalamus, particularly in the centromedian (CM) nucleus, consistently show low-frequency oscillations in the theta-alpha/low-beta range (5–15 Hz), encompassing the theta (2–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and lower beta (13–20 Hz) bands [50]. These oscillations have been identified as biomarkers of TS, with their presence strongly correlating with tic severity and treatment responsiveness, particularly in cases with co-morbid obsessive-compulsive behaviors [50, 51]. This activity is also considered a general signature of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loop implicated in hyperkinesias, such as levodopa-induced dyskinesias and dystonia [15]. Additionally, recent evidence suggests that delta-theta (2–10 Hz) bands power within the CM nucleus are correlated with tic onset but not with voluntary movement, while reductions in cortical beta activity may precede premonitory urges [52].

In chronically implanted cases of TS, dynamic spectral changes in LFP patterns have been observed: while initial recordings at rest, conducted 3–5 days post-implantation, typically show prominent theta-alpha activity (5–15 Hz), chronic monitoring at rest, often performed with externalized leads during battery replacement, reveals a gradual shift toward both lower (theta, 5–7 Hz) and higher (beta, 13–35 Hz) frequency bands. The observed increase in the beta power may reflect the long-term DBS effects. Notably, in the context of obsessive-compulsive behavior as a comorbidity, greater LFP spectral power in the 5–15 Hz range shortly after implantation is associated with a better symptom control [53].

These findings place the CM nucleus as a primary DBS target for TS and highlight potential strategies for aDBS strategies. In this context, additional motor cortex (M1) strips have been used to detect tic-related activity. Newer devices utilize a single CM nucleus of the thalamus lead to both sense tics and trigger bilateral stimulation, demonstrating the feasibility of using low-frequency power increases (1–10 Hz) as a stimulation trigger [54]. However, several challenges remain. These include slow ramp times (gradual increase or decrease in stimulation intensity), which may hinder real-time tic suppression due to the rapid nature of frequency changes. Additionally, significant inter-subject variability requires the development of personalized algorithms to ensure effective stimulation and clinical outcomes (Tables 2 and 3).

### LFPs in Chorea

The use of DBS in HD and other hyperkinetic syndromes has underscored the utility of LFPs in optimizing therapeutic strategies for chorea. In these conditions, recordings of the GPi reveal distinct oscillatory patterns that aid in target location and serve as potential biomarkers for aDBS. In HD, LFPs commonly reveal elevated high-beta to low-gamma activity (26–43 Hz), correlating with chorea severity [55]. In contrast, dystonia is marked by increased low-frequency power (6–14 Hz), a spectral signature that may aid in distinguishing hyperkinetic disorders with overlapping clinical features [15]. As such, the ratio of low-frequency to high-beta/-gamma power provides a useful spectral power for differentiating these hyperkinetic movements and tailoring stimulation settings accordingly [33]. Furthermore, nonlinear analysis using Lempel-Ziv complexity reveals greater regularity in dystonic oscillations and increased irregularity in HD at higher frequencies, offering further refinement for aDBS strategies [54, 55].

Stimulation at the lateral border of the GPi and GPe has been linked to significant chorea reduction, highlighting this region as a potential DBS target [56]. Intraoperative LFP recordings in juvenile HD reveal alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) oscillations, similar to patterns seen in akinetic-rigid syndromes [15, 56]. Furthermore, resting-state LFPs correlate with clinical metrics, such as the Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS), supporting their potential use in ambulatory monitoring [55, 56]. The emergence of closed-loop DBS systems enables real-time adaptation based on LFP signatures – particularly high-beta and low-gamma activity, which track chorea severity in HD. However, long-term signal stability, particularly in the context of progressive brain atrophy, remains a critical limitation for implementing aDBS in this clinical practice context [56].

### Clinical Applications of LFP Recordings

#### Intraoperative Target Localization and Postsurgical Programming

Different from MERs that are capable of capturing high-frequency action potentials from individual neurons, LFPs offer wider spatial and temporal profiles of neural dynamics, allowing real-time physiological characterization of target areas during implantation [1, 10, 15, 17]. In this setting, LFPs are registered when the electrode reaches the planned target, and spectral analyses are performed to assess frequency-specific activity that supports accurate localization [10]. As previously described, in PD, beta-band oscillations (13–30 Hz) at rest can be detected across multiple nodes of the basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical network but are typically more meaningful in the dorsolateral STN and the GPi, the primary surgical targets [31, 33]. In this context, intraoperative LFP recordings from DBS leads allow identification of an electrophysiological signature, providing a functional marker that complements imaging and anatomical landmarks, particularly in asleep DBS procedures in which intraoperative patient feedback is unavailable (Table 1) [17, 28].

Similarly, in other movement disorders such as ET and dystonia, LFP recordings can be used to verify the engagement of a target within the motor network. In the case of dystonia, increased power in the theta-alpha range (4–12 Hz) can be recorded from the motor subregion of the GPi, and in ET detection of movement- or posture-related oscillations at tremor frequency may help target location in the VIM [39, 40, 45, 49].

Beyond intraoperative targeting, postoperative LFP recordings of DBS-implanted patients may identify potential “hotspots” along the electrode axis for optimal stimulation guiding electrode contact selection and initial programming [57]. In this context, contacts exhibiting the highest amplitude beta oscillations should correlate with greater therapeutic efficacy, providing an objective physiological marker for stimulation settings. This approach has been studied comparing various outcome measures with standard monopolar review, revealing similar efficiency in terms of motor improvement and total electrical energy delivered but much shorter programming time and inter-rater variability (Table 1) [57–59].

#### LFPs as Biomarkers of Disease State: Outpatient Monitoring

LFPs represent a paradigm-shifting tool for monitoring symptom fluctuations and disease progression in complex movement disorders such as PD. While traditional clinical assessments – motor exams or patient diaries – provide only an interpretive snapshot of the patient’s condition and often fail to capture the dynamic, day-to-day fluctuations in symptom severity, LFPs provide objective and continuous physiologically grounded data that directly reflect the real-time activity of neural circuits involved in motor control [34]. As such, recordings over longer timeframes allow the detection of not only the presence of abnormal activity (i.e., elevated beta power) but also of how a parameter may change in response to medication, DBS therapy, or daily activities.

The development of newer generation implantable DBS devices with chronic sensing capabilities marks a significant advancement in this realm as they can record LFPs continuously from hours to weeks without requiring hospitalization or special equipment [34, 60]. These data could transform personalized neurology by enabling fine-tuning of DBS or the medication regimen based on real-time physiological feedback rather than on clinical guesswork. Integrating LFP monitoring into routine care may allow earlier symptom detection, more targeted treatment, and improved long-term outcomes [60].

The process of event recording for outpatient monitoring of DBS patients using LFPs is based on capturing neural activity in relation to specific clinical events (i.e., medication intake, motor tasks, symptom fluctuations, etc.) [61]. Recordings can be continuous, scheduled, or triggered, and events are marked manually or automatically using synced external devices, mobile apps, or clinician input. A critical element is time-locking neural data to these events, allowing the analysis of how LFP patterns change surrounding the specific trigger. For example, a drop in beta activity following medication or the occurrence of brief beta bursts just before or during episodes of freezing can help link brain signals to symptoms. The data are then stored in the implant or transferred to external devices for analysis (Table 1; Fig. 1) [60, 61].

#### aDBS and Personalized Therapy

The identification of reliable physiological biomarkers – particularly LFPs – has catalyzed a new era in neuromodulation, enabling the development of closed-loop systems such as aDBS. In contrast to conventional DBS, which delivers continuous stimulation using fixed parameters regardless of clinical states, aDBS employs real-time neurophysiological feedback to dynamically adjust stimulation in response to fluctuating symptom severity. This personalized, demand-driven approach optimizes therapeutic precision, improves symptom control, and significantly reduces stimulation-induced side effects and energy consumption [61, 62]. aDBS systems typically utilize LFPs recorded from the STN or GPi to detect pathological beta-band oscillations. When elevated beta power is recorded, stimulation is increased to suppress these oscillations. Conversely, when beta activity subsides due to treatment response, stimulation is reduced. This closed-loop mechanism allows for a more physiologically attuned modulation of neural activity compared to continuous DBS, which delivers fixed-parameter stimulation irrespective of symptom variability [61–63].

To be able to provide this technique, the system must integrate sensing, processing, and stimulation functions. Hardware requires an IPG model with built-in sensing capability and electrodes capable of delivering stimulation and record LFPs [34]. The recorded signals are then processed in real time by onboard or external software that runs signal analysis algorithms. Minimum and maximum thresholds must be predefined to allow the software to adjust dynamically the stimulation parameters (more often amplitude but also frequency or pulse width) [34, 60–63].

The control strategies underlying aDBS encompass a range of algorithmic approaches that differ in complexity, adaptability, and computational demands. Early implementations employed single-threshold controllers, in which stimulation is activated when a pathological oscillatory feature exceeds a predefined level and is deactivated when it drops below that threshold [34, 60, 61]. While effective in proof-of-concept studies, these designs can produce frequent switching and stimulation instability when signal fluctuations hover near the threshold. To mitigate this, more recent systems have introduced dual-threshold or hysteresis-based architectures, providing a buffer zone that minimizes rapid toggling and enhances temporal stability [61, 62]. Importantly, prior to the availability of fully implantable aDBS systems, several early proof-of-concept studies employed a fundamentally different control strategy based on a linear proportional algorithm [63–65]. In these paradigms, stimulation amplitude was continuously scaled in proportion to the magnitude of the pathological biomarker (most commonly beta-band power), rather than being governed by discrete on/off thresholds. This approach was tested over substantially longer time windows, ranging from approximately 2 h to 24 h, and demonstrated stable symptom control with reduced stimulation delivery compared to conventional DBS [64]. Linear proportional control differs conceptually from dual-threshold or hysteresis-based architectures in that it provides a continuous, graded modulation of stimulation rather than binary or state-dependent switching. Notably, this algorithm has since been incorporated into commercially available fully implantable aDBS devices, underscoring its translational relevance and ongoing clinical applicability [63–65].

Beyond amplitude-triggered control, more sophisticated models have emerged, including reinforcement learning and model-predictive algorithms that continuously optimize stimulation parameters (e.g., amplitude, frequency, pulse width) based on probabilistic estimates of symptom state and network response [62, 66]. These adaptive schemes aim to personalize therapy to the patient’s evolving neural and behavioral context, paving the way for context-aware and multimodal control strategies that integrate cortical, peripheral, or wearable sensor inputs. Despite these advances, several technical and translational challenges remain. A major obstacle is the latency between biomarker detection and stimulation adjustment. Even brief delays can compromise efficacy for transient phenomena such as tremor bursts, dyskinesia onset, or tic expression. Artifact handling remains a central limitation: stimulation artifacts, movement-related noise, and electromagnetic interference often contaminate the recorded LFP, requiring complex real-time filtering and blanking strategies that may inadvertently remove physiologically relevant data [34, 60–62]. Cross-channel interference, arising from concurrent stimulation and sensing through neighboring contacts, can distort signal fidelity, particularly in multipolar and directional electrodes.

Additional technical constraints include limited bandwidth and on-board computational power of implantable devices, which restrict the implementation of high-resolution spectral analyses and multivariate models. Long-term signal stability is another unresolved issue; impedance drift, encapsulation tissue growth, and progressive neurodegeneration can alter signal amplitude and spectral composition, challenging longitudinal calibration of adaptive algorithms [34, 60–62, 66]. Moreover, the generalizability of biomarkers across behavioral states and disease stages remains uncertain as in the case of beta power, for instance, which may not consistently predict motor impairment during complex or cognitive tasks [28, 31, 34]. Finally, data privacy, cybersecurity, and regulatory validation of closed-loop algorithms represent emerging frontiers that will require rigorous oversight as adaptive systems transition from research to routine clinical practice [34, 67]. Ongoing work aims to address these challenges through advances in amplifier design, adaptive noise suppression, multi-site sensing (including cortico-subcortical coupling metrics), and machine learning (ML) frameworks that combine physiological and behavioral data streams to deliver more reliable, individualized neuromodulation [67].

Historically, the pivotal study by Little et al. [61] published in 2013 is widely credited as the first in-human proof of concept for aDBS in PD, showing that, compared to conventional DBS, aDBS provided greater motor improvement, reduced stimulation time, and lowered energy consumption. Since then, several other groups have significantly advanced the field, particularly through the development of fully implantable bidirectional systems capable of chronic sensing and adaptive control. The first study demonstrating stable long-term recordings of beta-band activity in freely moving patients was published in 2021 using the Summit RC + S platform, establishing the feasibility of continuous biomarker-based modulation in real-world conditions [65]. Earlier work from one of the groups involved in this study had already shown the utility of motor cortex signals for aDBS control, demonstrating that cortical gamma activity and phase-amplitude coupling can serve as reliable, movement-related control signals for real-time modulation [35]. Together, these studies illustrate a coherent trajectory from chronic biomarker streaming to multimodal network-level sensing, providing the physiological basis for increasingly personalized closed-loop neuromodulation. Likewise, recent work has highlighted the importance of movement-state specificity and physiological context for next-generation aDBS strategies. Busch and colleagues demonstrated that the performance of single-threshold adaptive stimulation critically depends on parameter choice, the patient’s motor state, and the intrinsic controllability of STN beta activity, underscoring the need for context-aware control policies [66]. Complementing these findings, Farokhniaee et al. [32] provided direct evidence that gait elicits distinct beta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling patterns within the STN of PD patients, reinforcing the potential for gait-informed or task-specific adaptive stimulation frameworks [32]. Also, foundation-laying work has established biomarker-driven control frameworks and charted a path for the translation of fully implantable aDBS systems, including detailed reviews of feedback control architectures and clinical considerations [68, 69]. Building upon these developments, a large retrospective analysis of 62 PD patients treated with bilateral STN DBS found that both conventional DBS and aDBS improved motor symptoms and quality of life; however, aDBS provided additional long-term advantages, including a reduced levodopa equivalent daily dose and greater improvements in MDS-UPDRS II, IV, and PDQ-39 scores [70].
