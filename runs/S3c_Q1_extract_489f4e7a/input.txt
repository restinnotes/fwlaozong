# Differential Effects of Levodopa and Stimulation on Post‐Surgery Freezing of Gait in Subthalamic Nucleus Deep Brain Stimulation Parkinson's Disease Patients: A Clinical and Kinematic Analysis

## Abstract
The long‐term efficacy of high‐frequency subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) on freezing of gait (FOG) remains unclear. We aimed to study the mechanism and optimal therapeutic approach to long‐term post‐surgery FOG. The aim was to assess the mechanism and optimal therapeutic approach to long‐term post‐surgery FOG. Seventeen Parkinson's disease (PD) STN‐DBS patients with a FOG score (item 3.11) ≥2 in the MedON/StimON condition were evaluated under 5 experimental conditions, including a low‐frequency (60 Hz) condition maintaining the same total energy delivered. In each condition, gait and FOG episodes (#FOG) were assessed using clinical (eg, a 3 × 14‐m Stand‐Walk‐Sit Test) and kinematic data from inertial measurement units (IMU). Compared to MedOFF/StimOFF, #FOG significantly decreased in the MedON/StimON 130‐Hz condition. Either stimulation or levodopa (LD) (MedOFF/StimON or MedON/StimOFF) also significantly reduced #FOG and Stand‐Walk‐Sit Test (SWS) time compared to MedOFF/StimOFF. No significant difference in #FOG episodes or SWS time was found between 130 and 60 Hz, though 60‐Hz stimulation had lower axial scores. Individual responses to LD or stimulation varied: (1) 3 patients improved #FOG with LD but worsened with stimulation, whereas 5 exhibited the reverse; (2) in 9 patients, low‐frequency stimulation outperformed high‐frequency stimulation in reducing #FOG. Gait variability was the strongest kinematic dimension associated with FOG, with severity accurately identified via a neural network trained on sensor data. Post‐DBS FOG in the best‐functional state is largely therapy resistant, partially improved by stimulation and medication. Individual variability in responses to LD and stimulation underscores the need to determine FOG circuit mechanisms, as treatment remains an unmet clinical need.

## Patients and Methods

We conducted a cross‐sectional and unblinded study to assess the effect of LD and stimulation on post‐surgery FOG. We recruited patients presenting with FOG in the ON condition defined as a score ≥2 on item 3.11 of the Movement Disorder Society unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS III) in the MedON/StimON condition. Clinical evaluations were conducted under 5 different conditions by a movement disorder expert (R.B.): (1) medication OFF/stimulation ON, (2) medication OFF/stimulation OFF, (3) medication ON/stimulation OFF, (4) medication ON/stimulation ON at 130‐Hz high‐frequency stimulation (HFS), and (5) medication ON/stimulation ON at 60‐Hz LFS. On the LFS, voltage was adjusted to maintain equivalent levels of energy. Number of FOG episodes (#FOG episodes) and gait time (Stand‐Walk‐Sit Test [SWS] time) were assessed during an SWS test where patients wore a set of 7 sensors positioned in the pelvis, right and left thighs, legs, and feet. Motor evaluations using the MDS‐UPDRS III,
29
 axial subscore (items 3.9–3.12),
29
 and nonaxial subscore were secondary endpoints. The primary outcome of the study was the difference in the number of FOG episodes between MedOFF/StimOFF and MedON/StimON 130 Hz. Secondary outcomes were (1) the change in the number of FOG episodes between the MedOFF/StimOFF and the MedON/StimOFF and the MedOFF/StimON conditions, (2) the differences in the number of FOG episodes between the 130‐ and the 60‐Hz conditions, and (3) the association between the automatic detection of FOG and FOG assessed using clinical gait metrics. Exploratory analysis was performed to dissect kinematic dimensions (Table S1) related to FOG. The statistical analysis was conducted in alignment with the prespecified hypothesis defined in our primary and secondary outcomes. A detailed description of methods and statistical analysis can be found in Supplementary Methods section.

## Results

Seventeen PD patients were included in the study: 71% male, disease onset at 51.9 ± 5.9 years, and age at bilateral STN‐DBS 62.2 ± 5.9 years. Patients were evaluated 7.7 ± 4.6 years after surgery. A full clinical description is presented in Table 1. FOG emerged 24.1 ± 2.11 months after surgery. Before surgery, none of the patients exhibited severe gait or FOG in their best ON state (0.6 ± 0.6 of item 3.10 and 0.4 ± 0.4 of item 3.11, during pre‐DBS levodopa challenge test [LCT]). Using the same dose of LD, MDS‐UPDRS III improvement during the LCT was significantly lower compared with pre‐DBS assessment (20.1 ± 13.4% vs. 56.1 ± 14.0%, P = 0.001). A similar result was found for the axial subscore of MDS‐UPDRS III (19 ± 37% vs. 78 ± 21%, P < 0.001). Total MDS‐UPDRS III scores, in both the OFF and ON states, were significantly worse compared with pre‐surgery evaluation (Fig. 1A–C; Table 1). ### FOG Assessment in MedOFF/StimOFF versus MedON/StimON

A high heterogeneity of responses on #FOG episodes was observed with either stimulation (change from −287% to 100%) or medication (from −600% to 94.12%). However, an average improvement of 75.5 ± 16.7% was obtained with at least 1 therapeutic intervention (Med‐only or Stim‐only) (Fig. 2E). The magnitude of this improvement was not related to the number of FOG episodes in the MedOFF/StimOFF condition, excluding a floor or ceiling effect (r = −0.11, P = 0.67; Fig. 2F).

Medication or stimulation also significantly improved nonaxial MDS‐UPDRS subscores, whereas reduction in axial subscores achieved only statistical significance on the MedOFF/StimON condition (Fig. 2G,H). Abnormal involuntary movements significantly increased with LD but not with stimulation (Fig. 2I). Detailed description of clinical metrics is provided in Table S3.

An increase in the #FOG episodes with stimulation was observed in 3 (17.7%) patients whose FOG improved with LD. In 5 patients (29.4%) #FOG episodes worsened with LD but improved with stimulation (Fig. 2I,J). In these 5 patients presenting with an LD‐worsened FOG, the #FOG episodes were significantly improved in the MedOFF/StimON compared to the MedON/StimOFF condition (1.8 ± 2.4 vs. 15.8 ± 6.8, P = 0.003) and baseline, supporting that it was possible to recover FOG (Fig. S2A). The addition of LD to this effective stimulation led to a trend in increase of #FOG episodes (Fig. S3A), supporting the possibly deleterious effect of LD in FOG in this subgroup. In contrast, an improvement in nonaxial MDS‐UPDRS III was observed with both stimulation and medication (Fig. S2A). This supports a pathophysiological dissociation between FOG and other motor symptoms.

On the Stim‐worsened subgroup, stimulation induced an increase i the #FOG episodes when compared to medication (MedOFF/SimOFF: 13.61 ± 6.73; MedON/StimOFF: 4.55 ± 2.86; MedOFF/StimON: 26.00 ± 11.53, P = 0.0278; Fig. S2B). In this group adding LD to stimulation completely prevented the lack of FOG response (Fig. S3A). The group presenting an FOG response with both strategies also presented an improvement across all motor domains (Fig. S2C).

Different response patterns were not related to the number of FOG episodes or severity of motor symptoms in the baseline (OFF/OFF) state (Fig. S2D).

### The Role of HFS and LFS in FOG

Globally, the #FOG episodes (HFS: 6.32 ± 7.91; LFS: 5.08 ± 8.26, P = 0.410) and SWS time (HFS: 76.52 ± 54.03; LFS: 71.84 ± 54.23, P = 0.836) were not significantly different between the HFS and LFS conditions (Fig. 3A,B). On the contrary, the effects on the MDS‐UPDRS nonaxial and axial subscores were distinct. HFS significantly improved the nonaxial scores (HFS: 31.18 ± 7.27; LFS: 35.12 ± 8.75, P = 0.004; Fig. 3C), whereas axial scores were significantly better with LFS (HFS: 7.2 ± 3.7; LFS: 5.59 ± 2.57, P = 0.0322; Fig. 3D) without changing dyskinesias (Fig. 3E).

Variability in patient response to different stimulation frequencies was observed (Fig. 3F): in 58.8% of the patients, LFS was superior to HFS in reducing the #FOG episodes (Fig. 3G), with a decrease in axial MDS‐UPDRS subscore (Fig. 3I) and a trend for an increase in nonaxial MDS‐UPDRS score (Fig. 3J). By contrast, 25.5% of patients had a better FOG response to HFS condition compared to LFS, although without statistical significance (Fig. 3F–J).

### Identification of FOG and Kinematic Features Related with FOG

Using a convolutional neural network in an independent group of 20 PD patients,
30
, 
31
 a model for automatic quantification of the percentage of time of FOG during straight gait (%FOG) was developed. This detection was not based on specific gait variables, but on the analysis of the inertial sensor signal (Fig. 4A–D; Supplementary Methods), and had a good specificity and sensitivity for freezing detection compared to clinical assessment (Fig. 4E).

The %FOG significantly decreased from MedOFF/StimOFF to MedON/StimON condition (61.51% ± 9.99 vs. 24.43% ± 24.43, P = 0.0128) (Fig. 4F, left). The #FOG episodes and the SWS time significantly correlated with the %FOG detected by inertial measurement units (IMU) across conditions (Fig. 4F, right; Table S4). There was a significant correlation between the %FOG and individual kinematic gait metrics across conditions (Fig. 4G; Table S5), with metrics related to gait variability having the strongest correlations with FOG. As power was limited to perform a comprehensible analysis, dimension reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) with the set of 30 prespecified kinematic variables. The first principal component (PC1) was able to explain 75% of the gait variability (Fig. S4B), and it was enriched with dimensions of gait variability and asymmetry (Fig. 4H; Fig. S4D). PC1 was correlated with multiple FOG metrics across conditions (Fig. 4I; Table S6). Another finding suggesting that these dimensions capture relevant FOG‐related metrics emerges from a comparison across frequency conditions. In the LFS‐better group, values of PC1 were lower with 60 Hz (low freezing) compared with 130 Hz (high freezing), whereas in the HFS‐better group, the reverse was observed (Fig. S4C,F).

### Using Three‐Dimensional Kinematics to Describe Gait Alterations

In the current study we found that LFS, when compared to HFS, was related to a global reduction in axial symptoms as assessed using the MDS‐UPDRS (Fig. 3D) with no difference in FOG (Fig. 3A). An exploratory analysis found that gait of patients on LFS tended to be faster with a significantly higher step cadence (P = 0.032) but no significant differences on step length (P = 0.640) or width (P = 0.330) (Fig. S5, left). LFS was also characterized by a lower stance time (P = 0.0219) and double support time variability (P = 0.022) translating into gait cycles with lower variability (Fig. S5, right). A description of gait metrics across study conditions is provided in Table S7.

## Discussion

The management of severe FOG in the best‐functional state after DBS remains a challenge in long‐term follow‐up. In this study, we found that FOG in the best‐functional state after surgery is mostly a therapy‐resistant FOG, and stimulation and medication are still able to partially improve the severe gait alterations observed in the treatment‐OFF condition.

### FOG Responses to DBS or LD Are Heterogeneous

In this study, either LD or stimulation improved MedOFF/StimOFF FOG, and they had a synergistic effect when combined. This suggests that patients presenting with FOG in MedON/StimON state 7 years after DBS still benefit from the effects of stimulation and LD, albeit with a lower magnitude of response than before surgery.

We found a lower magnitude of motor response to LD in the post‐op LCT compared to the pre‐op LCT. This can be explained by disease progression and loss of dopamine sensitivity,
8
, 
32
 as well as by postsurgical desensitization of the postsynaptic dopaminergic receptors.
33
 The conjunction of decreased LD responsiveness, particularly regarding axial symptoms, as well the increased severity of motor scores in the treatment‐OFF states, supports (as previously proposed
7
, 
8
, 
9
, 
10
, 
32
) that disease progression, with involvement of nondopaminergic pathways, is likely the major cause for therapy‐resistant FOG.
7
, 
8
, 
10
, 
32
 Additionally, these findings do not suggest, at least at a group level, a deleterious effect of stimulation. This observation aligns with prior observations that although FOG may persist in the MedON/StimON condition, its severity and frequency decrease in comparison to the OFF‐treatment state.
10
, 
15
, 
34
, 
35
, 
36

STN‐DBS and LD have been shown to affect motor outcomes differently,
37
, 
38
, 
39
, 
40
 and more recently, they were found to modulate brain motor circuits in distinct ways.
41
 Here, we expand these observations by demonstrating distinct individual responses and, importantly, causally dissociating improvements in axial and nonaxial symptoms (whether with LD or stimulation frequency). We describe a subgroup of patients whose FOG did not improve with medication but with stimulation. In fact, the number of FOG episodes nearly doubled when they were on medication, even though their bradykinesia and rigidity improved. This indicates that LD may have little effect on axial symptoms, whereas STN‐DBS is more effective.

Currently, patients with LD‐resistant FOG and gait impairment are excluded from surgical programs
42
, 
43
 even if some isolated findings have suggested that axial non‐LD responsive signs may improve with STN‐DBS.
44
 Even if the present study is not designed to answer this question directly, it adds further information regarding the potential distinct effects of LD and stimulation specifically on axial signs.

Stimulation‐induced FOG and gait akinesia have been previously described,
18
, 
45
, 
46
 and we identified it in 3 patients. Although the mechanisms remain unclear, current diffusion to neighboring structures of the STN such as the pallidothalamic and pallido‐pedunculopontine fibers has been proposed as the cause of stimulation‐induced gait impairment.
18
, 
45
, 
46
, 
47
 In line with this, recent studies have highlighted the role of electrode location (including the use of directional stimulation)
20
, 
48
 and stimulation laterality
49
 in FOG outcomes. This suggests that both a “sweet” and a “sour” spot for stimulation regarding FOG outcomes may exist.

The exact pathophysiologic mechanisms involved in FOG are not well understood, with multiple theoretical models
5
 and clinical triggers identified.
4
 By systematically exploring the response of the same patient in the same self‐paced scenario across multiple therapeutic manipulations, we are limited to extract conclusions regarding freezing in other contexts, but we benefit from constraining contextual variability. This allows us to argue that it is very likely that therapeutic responses of FOG can be obtained by manipulating different nodes of circuit dysfunction (with stimulation or medication).
50
, 
51

### LFS Can Lead to Improvement in Axial Symptoms

LFS has been appointed as a possible strategy to manage refractory FOG in STN‐DBS patients.
15
, 
22
, 
23
, 
52
, 
53
 These studies have different designs and outcome measures, but from most of them, the clinical variability in FOG response is clear, even if they focus on younger and less‐severe patients than our sample. In our study, we found no difference in the number of FOG episodes between HFS and LFS, even though LFS was associated with a significant reduction in axial MDS‐UPDRS III scores, as previously shown.
22
, 
54
 Although variability, unblinding, and a small sample size limited comparisons, 9 patients experienced fewer FOG episodes with LFS compared to HFS at equivalent delivered energy. The effect of LFS on axial symptoms was also captured by the kinematic analysis. Here, variables reflecting mostly the overall gait bradykinesia (eg, speed or step length) were not specifically modulated by LFS (Fig. S5), whereas variables known to be associated with axial signs,
55
, 
56
, 
57
, 
58
 such as gait cycle variability metrics, were improved by 60‐Hz stimulation, in accordance with a previous observation using 80‐Hz stimulation.
59
 This suggests a mechanistic dissociation between axial and appendicular symptoms probably emerging from distinct brain circuits. Despite the lack of statistically significant difference between HFS and LFS, our observations support the need for a personalized, systematic evaluation of patients under both medication and stimulation (Fig. 5) to determine the mechanisms of FOG and guide treatment strategies. This heterogeneity might be a source of bias in clinical trials assessing stimulation parameter strategies and should be considered when calculating sample size.

### Automatic FOG Detection Correlates with Clinical FOG Metrics

FOG detection and quantification have traditionally been carried out using clinical scales, often relying on retrospective self‐assessment.
26
, 
60
, 
61
 Even when evaluated by trained clinicians, a significant interrater variability concerning both the number and duration of FOG episodes exists.
62
 To find more robust, precise, and objective metrics, wearable devices, such as IMUs, powered by machine‐learning algorithms
63
, 
64
 have been increasingly used for FOG quantification and detection.
26
 Our FOG detection model was applicable across different clinical conditions and presented good correlation with clinic FOG metrics. In addition, the automatic detection of the percentage of FOG in gait significantly decreased from Med OFF/Stim OFF condition to Med ON/Stim ON condition, suggesting that these tools are sensitive to treatment modulation. Further replications with large samples and naturalistic conditions are necessary.

A discussion about the true episodic nature of FOG and the artificial split between episodic and continuous gait disorders is ongoing.
5
 Objective and continuous gait analysis, especially when performed in ambulatory settings throughout long time periods, can provide useful insights regarding gait structure, clarifying how continuous, background gait alterations may predispose to the emergence of a clinical episode of FOG. PD patients with FOG have higher gait variability and asymmetry than PD patients without FOG, even outside FOG episodes.
65
, 
66
, 
67
, 
68
 In addition, stride time variability was recently used as a biomarker to modulate adaptative DBS, with positive effects in FOG.
69
 Enriching this “kinematic biomarker” with a comprehensive set of gait variables known to capture FOG could provide an attractive treatment strategy. In our work, the gait metrics that were most strongly associated with clinical or automatic FOG metrics were gait variability, asymmetry, and nonlinear metrics, with this holding true across all tested conditions and after dimension reduction (PCA).

This may argue that the existence of severe gait alterations will be associated to a decrease in the freezing threshold (as proposed by the threshold model
5
), and “normalization” of this background gait alterations could potentially translate into a lower likelihood of FOG. Nevertheless, to properly determine the background gait in PD freezers a comparison with an age‐ and disease‐duration‐matched nonfreezer group should be performed.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the small sample size restricts the generalizability of the results and may diminish statistical power to detect significant effects. Additionally, the absence of randomization and blinding in clinical evaluation, along with the use of a single rater for FOG assessment, introduces potential biases into the results. Furthermore, the presence of a carryover effect cannot be completely excluded, even if a strict 30‐minute interval was kept between stimulation changes. The LD dose administered during the LCT was determined based on pre‐surgery assessments and may underestimate the current dosage required. Accurately estimating the LD dose post STN‐DBS surgery is challenging due to the reduction in LEDD after surgery. Consequently, many STN‐DBS studies employ fixed LD doses of 200–250 mg for the LCT. In this study, an average LD dose of 433 mg was used, exceeding those typically employed in prior research. The use of these high doses of LD, while trying to ensure an adequate ON state, was also associated to high dyskinesia scores especially in the medication ON condition. Although severe dyskinesia and dystonia can affect gait, the highest scores on the modified Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) scale were observed in the conditions associated to lower #FOG episodes, suggesting that in our cohort the presence of dyskinesia or dystonia did not impact the evaluation of freezing. Finally, the lack of prospective data precludes conclusions regarding the lasting beneficial effects of LFS in those patients who improved in the acute setting.

In summary, STN‐DBS patients who experience post‐surgery FOG still respond to LD and DBS, even if the presurgical LD‐induced improvement cannot be fully reproduced. There seems to exist interindividual variability on treatment responses claiming for an individualized approach to FOG. Kinematic assessment of gait can play a crucial role in studying pathophysiological mechanisms of FOG. Importantly, we found that freezers who do not respond to medication may still respond to stimulation: a pivotal observation with implication for patients' selection for surgery.
