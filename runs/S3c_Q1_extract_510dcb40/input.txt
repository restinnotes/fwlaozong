# Gait and balance deterioration following deep brain stimulation in Parkinson’s disease: a counterfactual review

## Abstract
•Gait and balance often deteriorate after DBS despite sustained appendicular motor benefit.•Deterioration reflects multiple mechanisms, including stimulation effects, medication interactions, and disease progression.•Counterfactual contrasts (stimulation, medication, time) clarify attribution of postoperative outcomes.•Balance, particularly dynamic postural control, is more consistently affected than gait.•Interpreting outcomes through attribution may inform individualized long-term DBS management. Gait and balance often deteriorate after DBS despite sustained appendicular motor benefit. Deterioration reflects multiple mechanisms, including stimulation effects, medication interactions, and disease progression. Counterfactual contrasts (stimulation, medication, time) clarify attribution of postoperative outcomes. Balance, particularly dynamic postural control, is more consistently affected than gait. Interpreting outcomes through attribution may inform individualized long-term DBS management.

## Introduction

Gait and balance impairment are among the most disabling manifestations of Parkinson’s disease and represent a major contributor to falls, loss of independence, institutionalization, and mortality. Although dopaminergic therapy can improve gait in earlier stages of the disease, after some time, the gait and balance issues worsen and become progressively resistant to medication, reflecting the increasing involvement of non-dopaminergic and multisystem pathology [1], [2], [3].

Deep brain stimulation, most commonly targeting the subthalamic nucleus, is a well-established and effective treatment for advanced Parkinson’s disease, with a strong therapeutic effect on tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and motor fluctuations. Its effects on gait and balance, however, are less consistent and appear to evolve over the postoperative course [4], [5]. While some patients experience early improvement in gait, particularly in the medication OFF state, a substantial proportion develop worsening of freezing of gait, postural instability, or falls during follow-up, even when appendicular motor symptoms remain well controlled [6], [7]. This apparent dissociation between appendicular and axial outcomes has been reported across multiple observational cohorts and longitudinal studies and represents a major unresolved challenge in the long-term management of patients treated with STN-DBS [8]. Traditionally, late-onset gait and balance deterioration after STN-DBS has been attributed to disease progression and the emergence of non-dopaminergic deficits affecting locomotor and postural control networks [9]. This, however, does not fully explain the variability and timing of postoperative gait worsening. There are a number of indications that suggest a potential role of stimulation in gait and balance worsening in at least a subset of patients.

The early postoperative worsening, which in some cases occurs weeks to months after surgery, is difficult to explain in terms of a neurodegenerative process, and this phenomenon has been described in both cohort studies and mechanistic case series [10], [11]. In addition, gait deterioration may preferentially occur in specific medication or stimulation states or improve following stimulation adjustment, further challenging a purely progression-based explanation [12].

Gait and balance outcomes after STN-DBS are also sensitive to stimulation parameters. Changes in stimulation frequency, amplitude, or contact configuration can markedly influence freezing of gait and other axial symptoms, with some patients experiencing improvement following systematic parameter modification [13]. These findings highlight the complexity of the neural network subserving gait control and indicate that the effects of stimulation on neural networks might influence disease progression and dopaminergic state.

The anatomical aspects have added another layer of complexity to the understanding of the mechanisms. Acute levodopa-resistant FOG has been described in the context of electrode implantation and stimulation activation, and it has been attributed to stimulation of fibers located near or dorsal to the STN, indicating network disruption rather than neurodegeneration as a possible causal factor [14].

Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that STN stimulation and levodopa can have partly independent or even opposite effects on gait and postural control, highlighting the role of stimulation-medication interactions [15].

Other targets provide useful context, even though they are not the focus of this review. Comparative meta-analytic data suggest that STN-DBS and GPi-DBS result in a similar long-term motor benefit, without clear evidence that one target confers a uniform long-term gait advantage [16]. The pedunculopontine nucleus has also been targeted for severe gait freezing and postural instability, but published responses remain variable, with variable methodology, and are limited by small cohorts and uncertain durability [17].

However, despite extensive investigations, most of the studies are still descriptive, though they differ in terms of design, testing conditions, and follow-up time, making the results difficult to reconcile. Perhaps more importantly, none of the current studies clearly address the distinction between stimulation-induced effects, stimulation-therapy interactions, and disease progression, nor do they organize information along lines commonly utilized in counterfactual decision-making. In clinical practice, attribution of gait worsening is inherently counterfactual, involving systematic comparison across stimulation, medication, and time conditions. Counterfactual thinking, in this context, is a question of how gait or balance might have performed under a different stimulation, medication, or time condition. This is simple. It is also clinically useful.

This review aims to address these issues by reassessing gait and balance worsening following STN-DBS within a counterfactual, attribution-based model. This review was undertaken because a similar postoperative complaint can have different implications for management, such as acute worsening that varies across stimulation conditions, suggesting a need to adjust stimulation, versus late worsening that is consistent across stimulation conditions, suggesting disease progression and a need to adjust rehabilitation, fall risk, and axial care strategies. By reviewing mechanistic, programming, and observational studies, we hope to shed light on which aspects of gait and balance deterioration are plausibly stimulation-related, which reflect interactions with dopaminergic therapy, and which are more consistent with disease progression.

## Methods

### Study design and scope

This review has been performed as a structured narrative synthesis of systematic literature searches, specifically on the decline in gait and balance following subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation in PD. The review has aimed to attribute the decline in gait and balance following STN-DBS in PD to the effects of stimulation, drug-stimulation interactions, or natural disease progression over time. In contrast to other reviews that have simply described the occurrence of gait/balance decline following STN-DBS in PD or summarized the efficacy of specific programming interventions, this review has been specifically designed to address clinically relevant counterfactual questions.

### Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science from database inception through December 2025. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free-text keywords related to Parkinson’s disease, subthalamic nucleus, deep brain stimulation, gait, balance, postural instability, freezing of gait, axial symptoms, stimulation parameters, programming, and longitudinal or long-term outcomes. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, and full-texts of potentially eligible articles were reviewed. Reference lists of eligible articles were further screened for potentially suitable studies.

### Eligibility criteria and study selection

Studies were eligible if they were focused on Parkinson’s disease treated with STN-DBS and reported gait- or balance-related outcomes. In order to be involved, studies were required to provide at least one of the following: stimulation state during testing, medication state during testing, stimulation parameter manipulation, contact or target information relevant to interpretation, or follow-up duration sufficient to assess temporal change. Original studies, including observational cohorts, interventional programming studies, controlled experimental paradigms, and mechanistic case series, were included as primary sources of evidence. Animal studies, technical reports without clinical gait or balance outcomes, studies that did not separate axial outcomes from global motor scores, and duplicate reports without materially new gait analyses were excluded from the primary database. Conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail, non-English publications without accessible full text, and studies lacking interpretable postoperative gait or balance assessment conditions were also excluded. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and narrative reviews were added to contextualize findings and to compare methodological approaches, but were not used as sources of primary data.

### Data extraction

From each included study, data were extracted on study design, follow-up duration, gait and balance outcomes assessed, stimulation parameters, medication state during assessment, and temporal patterns of change. Particular attention was paid to whether outcomes were assessed under stimulation ON versus OFF conditions, medication ON versus OFF conditions, following stimulation parameter modification, or across different postoperative time points.

### Attribution framework and synthesis

Given heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted. Evidence was organized according to clinically relevant counterfactual contrasts routinely used in clinical decision-making, including stimulation ON versus OFF, medication ON versus OFF, stimulation parameter modification, and early versus late postoperative periods, to distinguish stimulation-related effects from stimulation-medication interactions and time-dependent changes consistent with disease progression. Counterfactual contrasts were defined as within-patient or within-cohort comparisons that approximate the clinical question: “What would gait or balance have been under an alternative stimulation, medication, or temporal condition?”.

### Methodological considerations

Formal risk-of-bias assessment tools were not applied due to the predominance of observational, mechanistic, and hypothesis-generating studies and the substantial heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and assessment conditions. Methodological limitations relevant to causal interpretation were considered qualitatively and integrated into the interpretation of findings.

### PRISMA compliance

The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [18]. Following database searching and reference screening, 29 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, comprising 18 original studies reporting primary gait or balance outcomes and 11 review articles used for contextual interpretation. In addition to these included articles, further background and methodological references, including reporting guidelines, were cited to support study design, interpretation, and contextual framing.

### Stimulation-related gait and balance worsening

Acute gait worsening temporally associated with stimulation activation, transient gait deterioration related to electrode implantation, and early postoperative deterioration emerging within weeks to months after surgery were reported in mechanistic case series and observational studies. Acute freezing of gait induced by STN stimulation and resolving with stimulation deactivation was described in early postoperative assessments [32]. Levodopa-resistant freezing of gait emerging immediately after electrode implantation was reported in isolated cases and small series independent of stimulation activation [33]. Acute axial worsening shortly after stimulation onset, with partial reversibility following stimulation adjustment, was observed in several reports [34].

Parameter-dependent modulation of gait outcomes was demonstrated in multiple interventional studies. Improvement in freezing of gait following systematic reduction of stimulation frequency from conventional high-frequency settings was reported in short-term crossover and interventional designs [35], [36], [37]. The literature supports a clinically relevant low-frequency range, usually 60–80 Hz in selected patients, rather than a single reproducible cutoff. The converse point is just as important: available studies do not identify a universal amplitude, pulse width, or contact threshold above which freezing of gait or imbalance reliably occurs. Contact location appears clinically relevant, but the signal is anatomical rather than binary; adverse axial effects have often been linked to current spread dorsal to the STN or into adjacent fibers, whereas improvement after contact relocation suggests that some cases are programming-sensitive. In practical terms, the available literature more often implicates dorsal or dorsally spreading stimulation than a specific lower contact as a trigger of gait worsening, likely because current spread above the intended STN motor territory may recruit pallidofugal, zona incerta, or other adjacent fiber pathways involved in locomotor control. However, the evidence does not support a uniform upper-versus-lower contact rule across patients, because contact effects depend on lead position, individual anatomy, and the volume of tissue activated. Evidence comparing monopolar with bipolar configurations remains limited and inconsistent, so a general rule cannot be justified. Additional programming strategies, including amplitude reduction, asymmetric stimulation, interleaving, and contact relocation, were associated with variable effects on gait and axial symptoms and were frequently accompanied by trade-offs in appendicular motor control [38], [39], [16].

### Stimulation-medication interactions

Several experimental and clinical studies reported differential gait and balance outcomes depending on combined stimulation and medication state. Independent and sometimes opposing effects of STN stimulation and levodopa on postural sway were demonstrated using quantitative posturography [40]. Differential freezing of gait responses across stimulation ON/OFF and medication ON/OFF conditions were reported, with effects strongly influenced by baseline dopaminergic responsiveness and postoperative medication reduction [41], [17]. Attenuation of levodopa-related improvement in gait and akinesia during STN stimulation was observed in controlled experimental paradigms [42]. Prospective studies further documented that freezing of gait responses varied across medication conditions, including improvement in the medication OFF state with stimulation and worsening when stimulation and medication were combined [43]. These interaction effects were observed despite sustained improvement in other motor domains.

### Time-dependent changes in gait and balance

Longitudinal cohort studies consistently demonstrated divergence between appendicular motor outcomes and axial features over time. Progressive worsening of gait and postural stability during extended follow-up was reported despite sustained improvement in tremor and rigidity [23], [24]. Long-term studies focusing on axial outcomes documented relative deterioration of gait and balance compared with appendicular motor symptoms several years after surgery [25], [44]. Persistent gait and balance impairment emerging with longer postoperative duration was reported across deep brain stimulation targets, with some alternative targets demonstrating short-term benefit but limited durability over time [45]. Meta-regression analyses and longitudinal syntheses demonstrated progressive worsening of balance and postural stability outcomes with increasing follow-up duration, while gait-related outcomes showed greater heterogeneity, even when global motor scores remained improved [46].

### Balance-specific outcomes

Quantitative posturography studies demonstrated that STN stimulation may differentially affect balance control, with improvement in some static measures of postural sway but worsening of dynamic or adaptive balance responses under certain conditions, despite improvement in other motor domains [29], [40]. Reviews focusing on balance physiology emphasized that postural instability reflects deficits in multisensory integration and anticipatory postural control, which were inconsistently responsive to STN stimulation across studies [28].

### Summary of findings across attribution domains

Across the included literature, three non-exclusive attribution patterns were observed, with parameter-dependent and contact-dependent phenomena grouped as stimulation-related/programming-sensitive effects. Early postoperative gait worsening that varied by stimulation state or stimulation parameters was reported in acute and short-term studies [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]. Differential gait or balance responses across medication conditions were documented in experimental and prospective investigations [40], [41], [17], [42], [43]. Progressive late-onset balance and postural stability decline, with more heterogeneous effects on gait, was most consistently reported in longitudinal cohorts with extended follow-up [23], [24], [25], [44], [45], [46]. ## Discussion

### Integration with prior reviews

Previous reviews have addressed gait and balance outcomes after STN-DBS, but most have approached the topic descriptively or interventionally rather than through explicit attribution. Systematic reviews have documented the frequency and timing of gait and balance worsening following STN-DBS, highlighting that axial symptoms often deteriorate despite sustained appendicular motor benefit [7]. Programming-focused reviews and meta-analyses have emphasized the potential of parameter modifications, particularly low-frequency stimulation, to alleviate freezing of gait while noting substantial heterogeneity and limited durability of benefit [13], [14], [35]. Consistent with prior narrative reviews, axial disability after DBS has been attributed predominantly to disease progression and non-dopaminergic pathology, although these reviews have not systematically disentangled stimulation effects from medication interactions or temporal evolution [5], [6].

The present review differs from these prior syntheses by explicitly organizing evidence around clinically relevant counterfactual contrasts. Rather than grouping studies by intervention category alone, findings were interpreted according to stimulation ON/OFF effects, stimulation-medication interactions, and time-dependent change. This framework allows apparently contradictory findings, such as improvement of freezing of gait in some contexts and worsening in others, to be reconciled within a unified attribution model.

### Stimulation-related gait and balance worsening

Acute gait worsening temporally associated with stimulation activation, transient gait deterioration related to electrode implantation, and early postoperative deterioration emerging within weeks to months after surgery have been reported in mechanistic case series and observational studies. Acute freezing of gait induced by STN stimulation and resolving with stimulation deactivation was described in early postoperative assessments [32]. Levodopa-resistant freezing of gait emerging immediately after electrode implantation was reported in isolated cases and small series, independent of stimulation activation [33]. Acute axial worsening shortly after stimulation onset, with partial reversibility following stimulation adjustment, was observed in several reports [34].

Parameter-dependent modulation of gait outcomes was demonstrated in multiple interventional studies. Improvement in freezing of gait following systematic reduction of stimulation frequency from conventional high-frequency settings was reported in short-term crossover and interventional designs [35], [36], [37]. The literature supports a clinically relevant low-frequency range, usually 60–80 Hz in selected patients, rather than a single reproducible cutoff. By contrast, available studies do not identify a universal amplitude, pulse width, or contact threshold above which freezing of gait or imbalance reliably occurs. Contact location appears clinically relevant, but the signal is anatomical rather than binary: adverse axial effects have more often been linked to dorsal or dorsally spreading stimulation above the intended STN motor territory, likely through recruitment of adjacent fiber pathways involved in locomotor control, whereas improvement after contact relocation suggests that some cases are programming-sensitive. However, the evidence does not support a uniform upper-versus-lower contact rule across patients, because contact effects depend on lead position, individual anatomy, and the volume of tissue activated. Evidence comparing monopolar and bipolar configurations remains limited and inconsistent, so no general rule can be justified. Even when deterioration is obvious after DBS, it should not automatically be solely interpreted as direct stimulation-induced harm, because medication reduction, altered network compensation, and time-dependent disease progression may contribute concurrently.

### Stimulation-medication interactions as a source of apparent worsening

Several studies indicate that gait and balance deterioration after STN-DBS may reflect complex interactions between stimulation and dopaminergic therapy rather than a unidirectional stimulation effect. Experimental studies have demonstrated that STN stimulation can attenuate the beneficial effects of levodopa on gait and akinesia, leading to paradoxical worsening in the medication ON state [42]. Prospective cohorts have further shown that freezing of gait responses differ depending on medication condition, with some patients improving in the OFF state but worsening when stimulation and medication are combined [17], [43]. These findings are supported by quantitative posturography studies showing partially independent and sometimes opposing effects of STN stimulation and levodopa on postural sway and balance control [40].

### Time-dependent progression and late axial decline

Long-term studies consistently demonstrate that gait and balance outcomes differ from appendicular motor responses over time. Multicentre cohorts with extended follow-up have shown progressive worsening of gait and postural instability despite sustained improvement in tremor and rigidity [23], [24]. Studies that explicitly focus on axial outcomes have identified time since surgery as a major determinant of late-stage gait and balance decline, often refractory to further stimulation or medication optimization [25], [45]. Regression and longitudinal syntheses reinforce this pattern, indicating that balance outcomes worsen with increasing follow-up duration, while gait outcomes show greater heterogeneity, even when overall motor scores remain improved [46]. These findings support the interpretation that late axial deterioration predominantly reflects disease progression and non-dopaminergic pathology rather than stimulation failure alone.

### Balance outcomes and non-locomotor mechanisms

Balance-specific outcomes have been studied less frequently than freezing of gait but offer important mechanistic insight. Experimental studies using posturography have demonstrated that STN-DBS can have divergent effects on balance, often improving static postural sway while impairing dynamic balance control and adaptive postural responses under certain conditions, despite improving other motor domains [40]. Reviews of postural control emphasize that balance impairment in Parkinson’s disease reflects deficits in multisensory integration, anticipatory postural adjustments, and brainstem network function, which may be less responsive to modulation by STN stimulation [28].

### Clinical and methodological implications

Systematic assessment of gait and balance across stimulation and medication states should be routine when postoperative worsening is reported. The first practical split is temporal and physiologic: early decline that changes with stimulation state, contact selection, or frequency is more likely to be stimulation-related; late decline that persists despite structured reprogramming is more likely to reflect disease progression. In programming-sensitive cases, clinicians should test alternative contacts, reduce current spread when possible, and consider lower-frequency stimulation in selected patients, while documenting trade-offs in tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia. No single contact level, pulse width, voltage, amplitude, or monopolar-bipolar configuration can be recommended as a universal gait-safe setting. Responses are patient-specific and should be determined empirically through structured testing. When deterioration proves stimulation-insensitive, management should shift toward medication review, rehabilitation, fall-risk reduction, assistive strategies, and counseling about axial progression rather than repeated empiric programming alone.

Future studies would benefit from standardized gait and balance outcomes, explicit reporting of stimulation and medication conditions, and longitudinal designs capable of capturing time-dependent change. Randomized trials of programming strategies should incorporate causal contrasts rather than relying solely on pre-post comparisons.

## Limitations

The included literature is heterogeneous, with most studies being observational, exploratory, or small interventional investigations rather than randomized controlled trials. Because counterfactual contrasts were reconstructed from non-randomized studies, causal attribution remains probabilistic and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Balance outcomes remain underrepresented relative to freezing of gait, limiting definitive conclusions in this domain.

## Conclusions

Gait and balance deterioration after DBS in Parkinson’s disease reflects a heterogeneous set of mechanisms, rather than a single uniform process. Evidence indicates that postoperative worsening may arise from stimulation-related effects, interactions between stimulation and dopaminergic therapy, or time-dependent disease progression. These mechanisms frequently coexist and may dominate at different postoperative stages. By organizing the literature around attribution-relevant counterfactual contrasts, this review provides a clinically grounded framework for interpreting postoperative gait and balance outcomes, guiding stimulation and medication adjustments, and informing long-term management after STN-DBS. Early, stimulation-sensitive deterioration should prompt systematic programming and medication-state testing, whereas late, stimulation-insensitive decline is more consistent with disease progression and warrants non-stimulation-based interventions.
