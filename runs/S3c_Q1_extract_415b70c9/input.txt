# Subthalamic Electrophysiological Mapping of Gait Initiation Dynamics and Freezing in Parkinson's Disease

## Abstract
The objective of this study was to investigate the relationships among subthalamic nucleus (STN) activity, gait initiation (GI), and freezing of gait (FOG) in patients with Parkinson's disease (PD). We recorded GI and STN local field potentials (LFPs) via externalized cables in 38 patients with PD (35 reporting FOG in daily life), both OFF‐ and ON‐dopamine (DOPA). GI was also recorded in 24 age‐matched controls. GI scores related to pace, rhythm, and balance were derived from kinetics, and FOG was identified using kinematics. We compared GI performance related to FOG and DOPA, and GI‐LFP relationships between posterior‐sensorimotor versus central‐associative STN regions. DOPAOFF, 12 patients with PD had FOG observed (FOG‐OBS, 263 episodes), occurring at a mean of 8 steps after GI, and 26 had no FOG observed (NOBS; FOG‐NOBS). GI pace scores were worse in FOG‐OBS than in FOG‐NOBS patients with PD, even when not followed by FOG, with weaker association with alpha/low‐beta activity in the posterior STN. Although rhythm/balance scores were similar between groups, their association with low‐beta activity was stronger in FOG‐OBS patients, with rhythm correlating more in the central STN and balance in the posterior STN. GI was worse preceding imminent FOG, with disrupted low‐beta GI‐LFP associations and emergent high‐beta correlations with reversed spatial distribution (pace/rhythm‐posterior STN, balance‐central). Dopamine improved pace, rhythm, and FOG, and partially restored STN activity. Our results reveal 3 distinct GI patterns in patients with PD associated with absence, predisposition to, or imminent occurrence of FOG, with STN neuronal modulations differing dynamically between the posterior and central subregions. These markers could support developing adaptive DBS strategies tailored for episodic gait impairments. ANN NEUROL 2025;98:977–990

Freezing of gait (FOG) – a sudden inability to lift the feet during walking – is a debilitating motor symptom in people with Parkinson's disease (PD).
1
 It can be precipitated or relieved by various environmental cues and is highly sensitive to the cognitive and emotional state. It typically occurs during gait initiation (GI), turning, navigating tight spaces, or multitasking, such as carrying objects or talking while walking.
1
 Its episodic nature suggests transient disruption of the locomotor circuit within the same individual, with alternating periods of occurrence and absence – indicating a predisposition rather than a constant motor deficit. Affecting 50 to 70% of people with PD after a decade of disease progression, FOG severely impacts mobility, increases fall risk, morbidity, mortality, and health care costs. FOG is inconsistently improved by dopaminergic therapy, worsens over time, and leaves patients with PD with limited treatment options.
1

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the subthalamic nucleus (STN) effectively improves parkinsonian motor signs, but its effects on gait and FOG vary. Approximately one‐third of patients with PD develop new or worsened FOG within a year following surgery.
2
 This variability may be due to individual differences in STN physiology and anatomy. Beta band (13–35 hertz [Hz]) activity in the STN has been extensively studied in patients with PD, in relationship with motor/premotor cortical activity.

 Exaggerated and prolonged bursts of low‐beta activity are associated with motor impairment, with dopaminergic treatment or STN‐DBS reducing it

10
 FOG has also been linked to elevated STN beta activity, at rest,

 Anatomically, beta oscillatory activity predominantly occurs in the posterior‐dorsal STN,

## Materials and Methods

### Participants

We recruited 38 patients with PD (mean age [SD] = 57.6 [9.2] years, 27 M/11 F; Supplementary Table S1) scheduled for STN‐DBS at Pitié‐Salpêtrière and Rouen University Hospitals, between February 2013 and September 2021. Patients with PD were included in 2 clinical trials: the GB‐MOV study (INSERM sponsor, n = 29) and the MAGIC study (CHU ROUEN sponsor, n = 9).
2
, 
26
 For both trials, the inclusion criteria were: (1) PD diagnosis based on the UK brain bank criteria; (2) age between 18 and 70 years; (3) validation of the indication of STN‐DBS according to the local staff,
26
, 
28
 including high responsiveness of motor disability to levodopa treatment, existence of levodopa‐related motor complications, no ongoing psychiatric disorders or dementia (Mini‐Mental Status score greater than 24), and no contraindication to surgical intervention for DBS implantation; (4) voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and signed a written consent, and (5) had social insurance. For the MAGIC study, additional inclusion criteria were: (1) FOG in the DOPAOFF condition (item 2.13 of the Movement Disorders Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale [MDS‐UPDRS] > 0 in usual life), (2) stability of others medical condition, and (3) effective contraception for woman of childbearing potential. Twenty‐four healthy age‐matched controls (HCs) were recruited in the GB‐MOV study (mean age [SD] = 52.9 [6.6] years, 14 M/10 F; Supplementary Table S2).

All studies complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Ethics approval was obtained (GB‐MOV: No. 2012‐A00225‐38 and MAGIC: 2019‐A01717‐50), and trials were registered on a clinical website (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01682668 and NCT04223427). All participants provided signed written informed consent.

### Surgical Procedure and Anatomical Localization of Recording Dipoles

Patients with PD underwent comprehensive preoperative assessment (baseline), followed by bilateral STN‐DBS implantation. The 2 electrodes were implanted within the STN on the same day with direct 1.5 T or 3 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) targeting and indirect targeting using a basal ganglia atlas.
2
, 
26
 Nineteen patients with PD received quadripolar unsegmented electrodes (Medtronic 33‐89, 4 contacts, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and 19 received segmented electrodes (Boston Scientific Cartesia DB‐2202, 8 contacts, Marlborough, MA, USA). The electrodes were temporarily connected to externalized cables for LFP recordings performed 1 to 4 days postoperatively, prior to neurostimulator implantation (Fig 1A, B).

### Experimental Protocol

Patients with PD and HCs underwent biomechanical and physiological recordings while barefoot on an instrumented gait platform. Each walking trial began with the subjects standing still on a force platform (0.9 × 1.8 m; AMTI, Advanced Mechanical Technology Inc. Watertown, MA, USA). Following a visual cue, the subjects initiated their gait, walked 6 to 8 m, made a 180‐degree turn, and returned to the starting position (Supplementary Fig S1). Ground reaction forces and moments of GI were recorded and sampled at 1,000 Hz. Full‐body kinematics of straight‐ahead walking and turning were recorded using a 32‐marker motion capture system (Vicon, Oxford, UK) (Fig 1A). Electromyographic activity (EMG) of the soleus and tibialis anterior muscles were recorded using surface electrodes and sampled at 2,000 Hz.

Patients with PD were assessed DOPAOFF following a 12‐hour interruption of antiparkinsonian medication, and DOPAON after the administration of the participant's usual morning levodopa dose +50 mg. Clinical motor severity was assessed via MDS‐UPDRS part 3 (total) and “axial” (sum of the items: “speech,” “arising from chair,” “gait,” “freezing of gait,” “postural stability,” and “posture”) scores, and the Gait and Balance Scale (GABS). The GABS is designed to assess gait, FOG, the gait cycle, balance, and posture, comprising measurement of 14 gait parameters during examination (see Supplementary Table S1).

### STN LFP Recordings

LFPs were recorded from adjacent contact pairs, using externalized cables connected to an amplifier (Porti 32; TMS International, Enschede, The Netherlands) placed in a small backpack worn by the patient (see Fig 1A). The Medtronic 389 electrode has 4 contacts (1.27 mm diameter, 1.5 mm height, separated by 0.5 mm; the most ventral labeled 0 and the most dorsal 3) yielding 3 recording sites (0–1, 1–2, and 2–3). The Cartesia DB‐2202 lead has 2 ring contacts (contacts 1 and 8, most ventral and dorsal, respectively) and 2 segmented contacts (2‐3‐4 and 5‐6‐7) allowing the recording of 9 dipoles (1.5 mm
2
 surface area, 1.3 mm diameter, 1.5 mm high, separated of 0.5 mm). The segmented contacts cover 90 degrees each and are separated by 30 degrees. LFPs signals were filtered (band‐pass filter = 0.25–300 Hz), sampled at 512 Hz, and transmitted via Bluetooth to the recording computer.

GI kinetics and LFP signals were simultaneously recorded, synchronized, and exported for off‐line analyses using MATLAB (MathWorks Inc.) toolbox (http://biomechanical-toolkit.github.io).

### Signal Processing and Analysis

#### Gait Initiation Parameters and FOG Events

Gait initiation was segmented into 2 phases: anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs), from the initial biomechanical event (t0) to foot‐off (FO1), and step execution, from FO1 to foot‐contact (FC; Fig 2A, B). We extracted ground reaction forces and moments with respect to the mediolateral (x), anteroposterior (y), and vertical (z) axes from the force platform data and calculated the displacements of the center of foot pressure (CoP) and accelerations of the center of mass (CoM) using Nexus software (version 10.2; NEXUS Software, Chesterland, OH, USA). Seventeen GI parameters were computed: (1) APAs duration, (2) antero‐posterior, (3) medio‐lateral, and (4) vertical displacements of the CoP; (5) CoM forward velocity at FO; (6) first step speed, (7) length, (8) width; durations of (9) first step, (10) double stance, (11) second step; (12) cadence based on the first 2 steps; CoM vertical velocity at (13) foot strike, and (14) foot‐contact with CoM braking.
26
 Finally, we computed during stepping (15) the maximum antero‐posterior CoM speed and (16) its timing, and (17) the maximum medio‐lateral displacement (see Fig 2B).

Trials with missing or corrupted force data were excluded, yielding 1,229 valid GI trials in DOPAOFF and 1,251 in DOPAON conditions for patients with PD (average of 24 trials per patient with PD and DOPA condition) and 2,720 trials in HCs (see Fig 1E).

In patients with PD, motion capture was used to annotate FOG episodes, characterized by an arrest in walking with failure to initiate or continue stepping. Each FOG episode was marked online and verified offline by 2 independent reviewers (see Supplementary Fig S1). We labeled patients with PD with FOG observed during recordings as FOG‐OBS and those without as FOG‐NOBS.

### Electrophysiological Data Processing

STN‐LFPs data were first high‐pass filtered at 1 Hz, notch filtered at 50 Hz, and epoched from 2 seconds before the cue to the end of the second swing phase. Time‐frequency decomposition was performed with a multi‐taper estimation algorithm (Chronux library in Matlab, http://chronux.org/) using 3 orthogonal tapers and a 500 ms window stepped by 30 ms. Power (0–100 Hz) was normalized against an 800 ms pre‐trial baseline and converted to decibels (10 × log10).

In our experiment, we recorded LFP signals at rest, while standing, and during GI. Time‐frequency representations were analyzed to detect transient artifacts. Trials with any average power increase exceeding 5.7 times (peak power >7.5 decibels [dB]) above baseline were excluded (−0.5 and + 0.5 seconds from cue, in 1–100 Hz). Each trial was also visually reviewed for saturation, spectral smearing, and movement‐related or cable‐induced artifacts, by at least 2 independent examiners (authors M.L.W., C.O., and M.Y.).

### Statistical Analyses

#### Gait Initiation Performance in Patients With PD and Effects of Levodopa Treatment

We applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the 17 GI parameters, capturing the main axes of variability across trials. Components with eigenvalue ≥ 1 were retained (Supplementary Fig S2), followed by varimax rotation to extract orthogonal dimensions (rotated principal components [RPCs]). Each RPC score was interpreted based on correlation with the original GI parameters using a Pearson's correlation test (Fig 2C).

We analyzed the effects of group (FOG‐OBS vs FOG‐NOBS), treatment (DOPAOFF vs DOPAON), and their interaction, using 2‐way ANOVA on RPC scores and clinical metrics, followed by post hoc paired t tests (RPCs) or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests (clinical scores). Correlations between RPCs were evaluated using Pearson's correlation tests.

#### Correlation Between GI Performance and STN LFP Activity

We examined the relationship between STN spectral power and GI RPC scores using linear mixed models (LMMs). The model included spectral power as the dependent variable, 3 categorical predictors: (1) group (FOG‐OBS vs FOG‐NOBS), (2) STN regions (posterior vs central), and (3) treatment condition (DOPAOFF vs DOPAON) and the RPC score as the continuous predictor. We added an interaction term across all predictors. The model included random intercepts nested by dipole within subjects. Separate LMMs were also run for comparisons within each STN subregion. Models were fit in R software using the lme4 package, and we estimated the slope between “RPC score” and “Spectral power” with the emtrends function of the emmeans package.

Frequency time‐courses analyses were performed by regressing RPC scores onto time‐resolved LFP power to assess how specific aspects of GI related to STN neuronal activity and FOG states. For each time point, we report estimated regression coefficient (β) ± standard error, t‐statistics (testing that the coefficient is equal to 0) and p values.

All statistical were performed in R software version 4.2.2. The p values were adjusted to control for the false discovery rate. We considered adjusted p values statistically significant when p < 0.05, with a stricter threshold (p < 0.001) used for time‐frequency plots.

## Results

### Gait Initiation Performance Relates to FOG Predisposition and Occurrence

Among the 38 patients with PD, 35 reported experiencing FOG in daily life, and 3 reported none (see Supplementary Table S1). During the DOPAOFF recording session (1,229 trials), we observed FOG episodes in 12 of these 35 patients with PD during straight‐ahead walking or turning (FOG‐OBS), whereas 23 patients with PD had no FOG episodes (FOG‐NOBS) (Fig 1E‐G). In FOG‐OBS patients with PD, we found more severe gait and balance disorders (higher axial and GABS scores) compared with FOG‐NOBS patients (see Supplementary Table S1 and Fig 3), with no other significant differences for age, disease duration, levodopa equivalent daily dosage (mg/d), severity of motor disability (MDS‐UPDRS part 3, DOPAOFF, and DOPAON) or FOG in daily life (MDS‐UPDRS part 2.13; see Supplementary Table S1 and Fig 3). Dopaminergic treatment significantly improved parkinsonian motor signs and gait in both groups to similar levels (see Supplementary Table S1 and Fig 3).

In the 12 FOG‐OBS patients with PD, we recorded 99 trials where a FOG event occurred in the DOPAOFF condition (FOG‐OBS+ trials), with a median onset of 8 steps (interquartile range [IQR] = 6–11.5) after GI, and a total of 263 FOG episodes (Fig 1E–G). These FOG‐OBS patients also completed 212 walking trials without FOG (FOG‐OBS− trials; see Fig 1E). In the 26 remaining patients with PD for which we observed no FOG episode, 918 trials were recorded (FOG‐NOBS, DOPAOFF; see Fig 1E). During the DOPAON session, FOG was fully resolved in all patients with PD except one (patient M_0102; see Supplementary Table S1), who experienced FOG in a few trials. These trials were excluded from further analyses. In total, 1,251 trials were recorded during the DOPAON condition (FOG‐OBS patients  = 349 trials and FOG‐NOBS patients = 902 trials).

PCA with varimax rotation of GI parameters from trials without FOG (1,130 trials) revealed 5 orthogonally RPC accounting for 73% of variance (see Fig 2C and Supplementary Fig S2). Scores for RPCs 1, 2, and 5 were significantly modified by dopaminergic medication (DOPAON–DOPAOFF estimate [SE] for RPC1 = 0.72 [0.11], p < 1.17e‐07; RPC2 = 0.55 [0.14], p = 4.64e‐04; RPC5 = –0.62 [0.12], p = 1.35e‐05; Fig 2D), whereas RPC3 and RPC4 scores showed no significant changes (RPC3 = 0.039 [0.14], p = 0.813; RPC4 = 0.31 [0.17], p = 0.322; see Supplementary Fig S2). RPC1 captured swing speed and other variables related to forward stepping vigor and propulsion, which we labeled “pace” (see Fig 2C, D). RPC2 reflected step times and cadence, which we labeled “rhythm.” RPC5 represented step width and lateral CoM speed, indicating lateral balance control or body sway, which we labeled “balance” (see Fig 2C, D). RPC3 included CoM fall and braking index, and RPC4 included APAs duration, double stance time, and posterior CoP displacement (see Fig 2C and Supplementary Fig S2).

In the DOPAOFF condition, FOG‐OBS patients exhibited significantly lower scores for pace (RPC1) than FOG‐NOBS patients (estimate [SE] = 0.75 [0.30], p = 0.025; see Fig 2D). Rhythm (RPC2) and balance (RPC5) scores showed no significant differences (RPC2 = 0.70 [0.40], p = 0.138; RPC5 = 0.25 [0.19], p = 0.406). In FOG‐OBS patients, we found significantly lower scores for rhythm when an FOG event will occurred (FOG‐OBS+ trials compared to FOG‐OBS− trials; RPC2, estimate [SE] = −0.243 [0.102], p = 0.035; see Fig 2D), indicating degraded stepping rhythm at GI, with no significant differences in pace or balance scores (estimate [SE] RPC1 = –0.102 [0.089], p = 0.246; RPC5 = –0.099 [0.179], p = 0.585).

Dopaminergic medication improved pace (RPC1) and rhythm (RPC2) in both patients with PD groups, eliminating significant differences between patientsgroups (DOPAON condition, FOG‐NOBS – FOG‐OBS estimate [SE] for RPC1 = 0.30 [0.30], p = 0.391; RPC2 = 0.42 [0.26], p = 0.14; see Fig 2D). Conversely, balance scores (RPC5) worsened in both groups (DOPAON – DOPAOFF estimate [SE] for FOG‐NOBS = 0.39 [0.13], p = 0.013; FOG‐OBS: estimate [SE] = 0.85 [0.21], p = 6.16e‐04), with a significantly greater worsening in FOG‐OBS compared to FOG‐NOBS patients (RPC5 estimate [SE] = 0.71 [0.28], p = 0.022; see Fig 2D). We found no significant differences in RPC3 and RPC4 scores among patients groups, and across dopaminergic treatment condition, thus these scores were not included in further analyses (see Supplementary Fig S2).

To investigate the differences and behavioral strategies for successful GI between FOG‐NOBS and FOG‐OBS patients in the DOPAOFF condition, we explored the link among pace, rhythm, and balance scores (Fig 2E). Pace (RPC1) and balance (RPC5) scores were positively correlated in FOG‐NOBS patients (r = 0.27, t(916) = 8.44, pc < 0.001), but this correlation was absent in FOG‐OBS patients (r = −0.04, t(210) = 0.61, pc = 0.54). Instead, FOG‐OBS patients displayed a significant negative correlation between rhythm and pace (r = −0.22, t(210) = 3.19, pc = 0.0022) and a significant positive correlation between rhythm and balance (r = 0.38, t(210) = 6.06, pc < 0.001). These relationships degraded further when an FOG event occurred (FOG‐OBS+ vs FOG‐OBS− trials; see Fig 2E).

### Predictive Patterns of FOG Occurrence

To explore STN dysfunction linked to FOG, we compared trials with freezing (FOG‐OBS+ trials) to those without (FOG‐OBS‐ trials) in FOG‐OBS patients with PD (DOPAOFF condition; Fig 6). We observed stronger negative associations between high‐beta band power and pace (RPC1) score in FOG‐OBS‐positive versus FOG‐OBS‐negative trials, significant only in the posterior STN (see Fig 6A–C and Supplementary Fig S6). ### Dopaminergic Medication Improves Gait Disability and Reestablishes STN Activity

In the DOPAON condition, we analyzed 875 LFP traces (633 from FOG‐NOBS patients and 242 from FOG‐OBS patients ). ## Discussion

This study distinguishes 3 distinct FOG states in PD—absence, predisposition, and occurrence—each characterized by distinct GI strategies and STN activity patterns. Patients with PD who experienced FOG during recordings showed reduced pace linked to decreased associations with alpha/low‐beta band STN activity, even for GI that was not followed by FOG. In these patients, LFP associations with rhythm and balance were exaggerated in the low‐beta band of the posterior and central STN, respectively. FOG was preceded by further degraded stepping rhythm at gait initiation, with dramatically altered theta and high‐beta associations in the posterior STN. Balance was also further degraded prior to FOG, which occurred with phasically disrupted high‐beta associations in the central STN. Dopaminergic treatment improved GI patterns and reduced the differences in STN‐LFPs between patients with PD groups.

### Study Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, this study was conducted under controlled experimental conditions, which may not fully capture the variability of FOG in everyday life. However, it includes a large dataset of STN‐LFP recordings during both FOG and non‐FOG trials in the same patients. Second, the absence of long‐term follow‐up limits our understanding of the durability of STN modulation changes and their relationship to long‐term gait outcomes post‐STN‐DBS. Third, we used a 3D co‐registered to each patient's MRI to localize electrode contacts, which may introduce some inaccuracies, particularly in patients with anatomic variations. However, electrode locations were carefully verified, and only dipoles within the STN were included. Last, whereas the FOG events were primarily detected using motion capture, each trial was verified online and reviewed offline by independent raters, although video recordings were not used. Future studies should incorporate video‐based validation to improve rater reliability and analysis of exact FOG onset.

## Conclusions

Our results support the existence of a predisposed state for FOG at gait initiation, which may be partially mitigated through compensatory mechanisms involving widespread low‐beta modulations within the STN; and an actual occurrence of FOG, marked by an early modulation in high‐beta activity in the posterior STN. These findings underscore the STN's critical role in encoding GI dynamics and provide a refined rationale for aDBS strategies. Targeted modulation of low‐beta activity in the central STN and high‐beta in the posterior STN may represent a dual‐site approach for preventing FOG. With ongoing advancements in sensing technologies and adaptive algorithms, personalized aDBS that dynamically respond to neural and behavioral markers could potentially transform the management of FOG in patients with PD.
